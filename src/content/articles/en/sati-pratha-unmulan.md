## What was sati

**Sati** is the name of the practice in which, after a man's death, his widow died by burning on his funeral pyre, or was forced to do so. The practice was not equally common in all parts of India or among all communities; in the 18th and 19th centuries it was recorded more in Bengal, especially around Calcutta, and in some parts of Rajputana. In many cases the widow's "consent" was manufactured through family pressure, greed for property and social pressure.

## Earlier efforts

Efforts to stop sati had been made even before the British. Akbar ordered a stop to forced sati, and in 1663 Aurangzeb also issued an order against it, although these were obeyed only to a limited extent. The Portuguese in Goa had banned it as early as the 16th century.

The East India Company hesitated for a long time to interfere in Indian religious customs. In the early 19th century it adopted a middle path: "voluntary" sati was allowed, but the police had to see that there was no force or intoxication and that the widow was neither a minor nor pregnant. This had the opposite effect: some people took government supervision as proof that the practice was legitimate, and recorded cases rose from 378 in 1815 to 839 in 1818.

## Ram Mohan Roy's campaign

The learned Bengali reformer **Raja Ram Mohan Roy** was the most influential Indian opponent of the practice. According to tradition, he was deeply affected by seeing his sister-in-law become sati on the death of his elder brother. In 1818 he published a pamphlet in Bengali and English that examined the scriptures in the form of a dialogue between a supporter and an opponent of sati. His argument was that the major scriptures prescribe a life of restraint and spiritual discipline for a widow, and do not make self-immolation compulsory.

Ram Mohan Roy wrote articles in newspapers, sent petitions, and even went to cremation grounds to try to persuade families. The Serampore missionary **William Carey** and his colleagues were also counting cases of sati and building public opinion against it.

## 4 December 1829: Regulation XVII

In 1828 **Lord William Bentinck** arrived as Governor-General. He consulted judges, army officers and officials on whether a ban would provoke a rebellion. Most said it would not. On **4 December 1829**, Bentinck's council passed **Bengal Regulation XVII**:

- Sati was declared illegal and made punishable in the criminal courts.
- Those who helped to burn or bury a widow alive could be tried for **culpable homicide**; the widow's "consent" was not a defence.
- Zamindars, village headmen and officials had to report any such preparation to the police immediately.

In February 1830 the same rule was extended to the Madras and Bombay Presidencies.

## Opposition and the Privy Council

Orthodox Hindus of Calcutta, among whom **Radhakanta Deb** was prominent, called it interference in religion. They organised the **Dharma Sabha** and sent a petition signed by hundreds of people to the **Privy Council** in England. Ram Mohan Roy went to England in 1830, where he argued in favour of the ban. In **1832** the Privy Council rejected the appeal and the ban remained.

The princely states were outside the reach of the Company's law. There, bans were imposed gradually between 1840 and 1861, and isolated incidents continued here and there even later.

## Significance and debates

Regulation XVII is regarded as the first major social reform law of British rule, and it opened the way for later reforms such as widow remarriage (1856). Historians also point out that in this debate both sides made their case by citing the scriptures, and the own voices of the women who became sati were almost unheard.

The law could not fully change social attitudes. After the sati of **Roop Kanwar** at Deorala in Rajasthan in 1987, Parliament passed the **Commission of Sati (Prevention) Act, 1987**, which makes both incitement to sati and its glorification crimes.
