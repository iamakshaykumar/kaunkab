## Discovery

In November 1954, the archaeologist **S. R. Rao** (Shikaripura Ranganatha Rao) of the Archaeological Survey of India and his team were searching for Harappan sites in the area of the **Sabarmati estuary** in Gujarat. During this they found a mound near the village of **Sargwala**, which the local people called **"Lothal"** — in Gujarati this is said to mean "mound of the dead".

Systematic excavation went on here from 13 February 1955 to 19 May 1960, and some work was done again in 1961. Rao published his findings in a detailed report of the Archaeological Survey of India.

## Layout of the City

Lothal is in the **Bhal region** of Dholka taluka in **Ahmedabad district**. The main city was about 7 hectares — much smaller than Harappa or Mohenjo-daro. Even so, its plan was like that of Harappan cities:
- A raised part (the **citadel or acropolis**), where there were the residences of the ruling class, bathing platforms and drains.
- A **lower town**, with markets, shops and houses along a north-south main street.
- A large **warehouse**, built on cube-shaped brick platforms, whose slope led towards the dock. Only twelve of these platforms survived the later floods.

## The Dock — Harbour or Reservoir?

Lothal's most famous structure is a huge trapezoidal **basin** of baked bricks, about 215 metres long north-south and about 35 metres wide east-west. Rao described it as a **dockyard**, where boats came from the old course of the Sabarmati through a channel and berthed.

In support of this view, the National Institute of Oceanography (Goa) found remains of marine microorganisms (**foraminifera**) and gypsum in the basin, suggesting that salty sea water once filled it. But not all scholars agree. In 1968 Leshnik and later scholars like Possehl argued that it was mainly a **reservoir** for irrigation or drinking water, and that the salinity could also have come from floods. This debate is still not fully settled.

## Crafts and Trade

Lothal was a major centre of work in beads, shells and metal. A **bead factory** has been found here, with a courtyard in the middle, about eleven rooms, a storeroom and a round kiln with two chambers. A round seal in the Persian Gulf style and another seal believed to be linked with Bahrain are signs that its trade extended across the sea. **Fire altars** have also been found here, in houses and public places.

## Decline

A flood in about 2050 BCE weakened the city. Around 2000 BCE the river changed its course and the dock's connection was cut off. A severe flood of about 1900 BCE did great damage to the settlement. Climate change and natural disasters are considered the main reasons for its abandonment.

## Significance

Lothal shows that the Harappan civilisation was not confined to cities settled along rivers, but had developed sea trade and craft production too. The archaeological museum here displays objects found in the excavation. In April 2014 Lothal was nominated for UNESCO's Tentative World Heritage list.
