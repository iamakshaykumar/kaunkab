## From Officer to Scientist

**Chandrasekhara Venkata Raman** was born on **7 November 1888** in **Tiruchirappalli** in Tamil Nadu. He studied at **Presidency College**, Madras, and passed his B.A. in **1904** and M.A. in **1907** with the highest distinction. At that time there were very few opportunities for research in science in India, so in **1907** he passed the Indian Finance Service examination and began a government job in **Calcutta**.

But his connection with science did not break. In Calcutta he found the laboratory of the **Indian Association for the Cultivation of Science (IACS)**, where alongside his job he kept experimenting on sound and light. In **1917** he became the first **Palit Professor** of Physics at Calcutta University and devoted himself fully to research.

## The Blue of the Sea (1921)

In **1921**, while crossing the Mediterranean on his way to England, the question arose in Raman's mind of **why the sea looks blue**. The famous scientist **Lord Rayleigh** held that the sea mainly reflects the blue of the sky. Raman felt that the water molecules themselves scatter light, and he did a few experiments with a small spectroscope on the ship itself. Returning to India, he began, with his students, a systematic study of the **scattering of light** in liquids, gases and solids.

## The Discovery of 28 February 1928

Ordinarily, when light strikes a transparent substance and scatters, its colour (wavelength) stays the same. Raman and his colleague **K. S. Krishnan** observed that a **very small part** of the scattered light changes its wavelength, and that this change depends on the structure of the substance's molecules. This discovery came to light on **28 February 1928** and was called the **"Raman Effect"**.

The importance of this effect is that by observing the scattered light, information about the molecules of a substance can be obtained — as if each substance had its own "fingerprint". The change is very faint — only an extremely small fraction of the scattered light changes colour — so catching it required a bright light source, filters and patient observation. Raman's team observed it repeatedly in many kinds of liquids and gases and confirmed that it was not a property of one substance but a general natural phenomenon. Today **Raman spectroscopy** is used in chemistry, the pharmaceutical industry, mineralogy, the examination of works of art and even forensic science.

## The Nobel Prize (1930)

This discovery brought Raman international fame. In **1929** the British government conferred a **knighthood** on him, and in **1930** he received the **Nobel Prize in Physics**. He was the **first Indian and first Asian** to win a Nobel in any branch of science. This achievement was also special because a large part of the research was done in India, in a laboratory with limited resources.

There is also a disputed side to this episode. **K. S. Krishnan** was a main participant in the experiments, but the Nobel went only to Raman and Krishnan's name was not in the nomination. Many historians believe that Krishnan's contribution did not get due credit, and later the relations between the two were also strained.

## Builder of Institutions

In **1933** Raman became the first Indian director of the **Indian Institute of Science (IISc), Bengaluru**. He founded the **Indian Academy of Sciences** and in **1948** started the **Raman Research Institute** in Bengaluru, where he worked until the end of his life. He trained many young Indian scientists.

In **1954** he received the **Bharat Ratna** and in **1958** the **Lenin Peace Prize**. He died in Bengaluru on **21 November 1970**.

## National Science Day

In memory of the discovery of the Raman Effect, **National Science Day** is celebrated in India every year on **28 February**. The proposal came in **1986** and the first Science Day was celebrated in **1987**. On this day science exhibitions and lectures are held in schools, universities and laboratories — so that the habit of asking questions with curiosity, as Raman did, reaches the new generation.
