## Lessons from Chandrayaan-2

India's lunar programme began in **2008** with **Chandrayaan-1**, whose instruments played an important part in finding signs of water molecules on the Moon. The orbiter of **Chandrayaan-2**, sent in **2019**, successfully reached lunar orbit, but on the night of **6–7 September 2019** its **Vikram lander** lost control in the final stage of descent and crashed into the surface.

After this failure **ISRO** decided to make the lander more robust. No orbiter was sent with Chandrayaan-3, because Chandrayaan-2's orbiter was already circling the Moon and could help with communication. The new lander's legs were strengthened, more solar panels were fitted, more fuel was carried and the possible landing zone was enlarged, so that options for a safe landing would remain even if something went wrong.

## Launch and Journey

Chandrayaan-3 had three main parts — the **propulsion module**, which carried the craft to lunar orbit; the **Vikram lander**, named after **Vikram Sarabhai**, the father of the Indian space programme; and the six-wheeled, roughly 26-kilogram **Pragyan rover**.

On **14 July 2023** at 2:35 pm the **LVM3-M4** rocket launched it from the **Satish Dhawan Space Centre** at **Sriharikota** in Andhra Pradesh. To save fuel the craft did not head straight for the Moon, but moved forward by circling the Earth in gradually larger orbits. On **5 August** it reached lunar orbit, and on **17 August** the lander separated from the propulsion module.

Around the same time Russia's **Luna-25** was also preparing to land in the Moon's south polar region, but it crashed on 19 August. This fixed the whole world's attention on Chandrayaan-3.

## 23 August 2023 — the Historic Landing

At about **6:04 pm** (Indian time) on **23 August 2023**, after an automated descent of about 19 minutes, the Vikram lander made a successful **soft landing** **near the Moon's south pole**, at about **69 degrees south latitude**. Scientists at the control centre in Bengaluru were overjoyed, and the live broadcast was watched across the country.

With this India became the **fourth country**, after the **Soviet Union, the United States** and **China**, to make a soft landing on the Moon, and the **first country** to land so close to the south pole. At the time **S. Somanath** was ISRO's chairman, while **P. Veeramuthuvel** was project director and **S. Mohanakumar** mission director. Prime Minister **Narendra Modi** named the landing site the **"Shiv Shakti point"** and declared **23 August** as **National Space Day**. The spot where Chandrayaan-2's lander crashed was named the **"Tiranga point"**.

## The Pragyan Rover's Experiments

A few hours after the landing, the **Pragyan rover** rolled down a ramp and began moving on the lunar soil. In about one lunar day (roughly 14 Earth days) of work it covered a distance of about **100 metres**. The mission's main scientific results were:

- The rover's **LIBS** instrument made the first direct confirmation of **sulphur** in the surface of the south polar region; aluminium, calcium, iron, chromium, titanium, manganese, silicon and oxygen were also found.
- The lander's **ChaSTE** instrument measured the temperature of the soil to a depth of about 10 centimetres. The top surface was found to be as hot as about 70 degrees Celsius, while just a few centimetres below the temperature fell sharply.
- **RAMBHA** studied the surface plasma near the polar region, and the **ILSA** seismometer recorded the rover's movements as well as a possible natural tremor.

On **3 September** Vikram also successfully carried out a small "hop" experiment. Before the lunar night began, the lander and rover were put into "sleep mode". The temperature falls very low at night, and contact could not be re-established with them the next morning — they had essentially been built for a single lunar day.

## Significance

The estimated cost of Chandrayaan-3 was about **615 crore rupees**, considered very low compared with many space missions around the world. Water may exist as ice in permanently shadowed craters in the south polar region, so this area is considered extremely important for future human missions. This success put the disappointment of Chandrayaan-2 behind it, increased ISRO's credibility and placed India among the leading countries in lunar exploration. After this ISRO moved ahead with plans such as **Chandrayaan-4**, to bring back samples from the Moon, and a joint lunar mission with Japan.
