## Humayun's Death and the Plan for the Tomb

The Mughal emperor **Humayun** recaptured Delhi in 1555 after a long exile, but only a few months later, in **January 1556**, he died after falling from the stairs of the library at Dinpanah (Purana Qila). He was first buried there; later his body was brought to the new tomb.

The credit for building the tomb is mainly given to Humayun's widow **Bega Begum**, who came to be called **Haji Begum** after her pilgrimage to Mecca. Most sources give the period of construction as about **1565 to 1572**, while the Archaeological Survey of India gives a date around 1569–70. Its cost is said to have been about 1.5 million rupees. Some historians believe that the real patron was the young emperor **Akbar** — this question is not fully settled.

## A Persian Architect, Indian Stone

According to the contemporary historian Badayuni, its plan was made by the Persian architect **Mirak Mirza Ghiyas**, who had earlier worked in Herat and Bukhara. He died before the construction was complete and the work was finished by his son **Sayyid Muhammad**.

The building has a unique blend of Iranian and Indian elements:

- Bands and inlay of **white marble** on walls of **red sandstone**.
- A **double dome** resting on a high drum — a low ceiling inside, a tall form outside. This appeared in India on such a large scale for the first time.
- **Chhatris** in the Rajasthani-Indian style on the roof.
- A plan of octagonal chambers around the central chamber, which in Persian is called **hasht-bihisht** (eight paradises).

The tomb is about **47 metres** high. The grave seen in the upper chamber is symbolic; the real grave is below in the crypt.

## The Charbagh: A Plan of Paradise on Earth

The tomb stands in the middle of a vast **charbagh** — a square garden divided into four parts by water channels and walkways. It was based on the idea of the garden of paradise described in the Quran. It was the first garden-tomb of such a scale in the Indian subcontinent. Humayun's father Babur was a great lover of gardens, and this tradition became monumental architecture here.

## The 'Dormitory of the Mughals' and 1857

In later generations this complex became the burial ground of the Mughal family — there are more than a hundred graves here, many of them unidentified. Along with Humayun, Bega Begum, Akbar's mother Hamida Banu Begum and Shah Jahan's son **Dara Shikoh** are also believed to be buried here.

When the British retook Delhi in **September 1857**, the last Mughal emperor **Bahadur Shah Zafar** took refuge here. Captain William Hodson arrested him from this very place — this scene of the end of the Mughal dynasty took place in the same building that had symbolised the start of its zenith.

## Legacy and Conservation

Humayun's Tomb is often called the **forerunner of the Taj Mahal**: the high platform, the charbagh, the double dome and the symmetrical plan — all these appeared in a more refined form in Agra about seventy years later.

In **1993** UNESCO declared it a World Heritage Site. From 1999 the Aga Khan Trust for Culture and the Archaeological Survey of India worked on restoring the garden's water channels and fountains, completed in 2003; in later years the tomb and the surrounding buildings were also extensively conserved. Today it is one of the most popular historic sites in Delhi.
