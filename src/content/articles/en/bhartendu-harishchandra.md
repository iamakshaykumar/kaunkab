## A Gifted Boy from Kashi

**Harishchandra** was born on **9 September 1850** into a wealthy and respected Vaishya family of **Kashi (Varanasi)**. His father **Gopal Chandra** wrote poetry in Braj Bhasha under the pen name 'Girdhardas', so the home had a literary atmosphere. He lost both his parents in childhood. He could not have much formal schooling, but he learned Hindi, Sanskrit, Urdu, Bengali and English at home.

As a teenager, on a family pilgrimage to **Jagannath Puri**, he passed through Bengal and was deeply impressed by the new plays, newspapers and reform movements there. He felt that the Hindi-speaking region could awaken in the same way; it only needed new literature in its own language.

## Journalism — A Laboratory for Hindi Prose

At just 18, in **1868**, he started the magazine **'Kavivachan Sudha'**. At first it printed old poetry, but gradually it began to carry articles on politics, society and the state of the country. In **1873** he brought out **'Harishchandra Magazine'**, which was later published as **'Harishchandra Chandrika'**. In **1874** the magazine **'Balabodhini'** was started for the education of women.

These magazines gave **Khari Boli Hindi prose** a laboratory. At that time **Braj Bhasha** was the norm for poetry, while the language of prose was under debate — on one side **Raja Shivprasad 'Sitara-e-Hind'** favoured a language full of Persian and Urdu words, and on the other Raja Lakshman Singh favoured Sanskritised Hindi. Bharatendu took a middle path — simple Hindi close to everyday speech, written in Devanagari, which also included the words of ordinary people. Bharatendu himself wrote that in **1873** **"Hindi took on a new gait"**.

## The Birth of Hindi Theatre

Bharatendu is regarded as the **father of Hindi drama**. He made translations from Sanskrit, Bengali and English and also wrote original plays. His main plays are:

- **'Vaidiki Hinsa Hinsa Na Bhavati' (1873)** — a satire on meat-eating and hypocrisy in the name of religion;
- **'Satya Harishchandra'** — the mythological tale of truth and sacrifice;
- **'Bharat Durdasha'** — a moving picture of the country's poverty, idleness and disunity under British rule;
- **'Andher Nagari' (1881)** — a sharp political satire on a foolish king and a city where "vegetables cost a taka a ser, and sweets cost a taka a ser";
- **'Neeldevi' (1881)** — the story of a brave Rajput queen.

'Andher Nagari' is still staged across the country, because its satire on a system that rules without thinking feels fresh in every age. Bharatendu acted himself and encouraged theatre troupes in Kashi.

## 'Nij Bhasha Unnati' and Concern for the Country

Bharatendu's famous lines are **"Nij bhasha unnati ahai, sab unnati ko mool; bin nij bhasha-gyan ke, mitat na hiy ko sool"** — that is, the progress of one's own language is the root of all progress, and without knowledge of one's own language the ache of the heart does not go. He supported giving Hindi and Devanagari a place alongside Urdu in the courts and offices, and is counted among the main supporters of this demand.

His writings show a double attitude towards British rule. He spoke of adopting new things such as modern education, railways and the printing press, but at the same time wrote that British rule was carrying India's wealth abroad. 'Bharat Durdasha' held both foreign rule and Indians' own weaknesses responsible for India's poverty. He also supported women's education, the use of swadeshi goods and social reform. In religion he was a Vaishnava devotee and also wrote beautiful poems of devotion to Krishna.

His generosity was famous. He spent freely on writers, poets and the needy, which greatly reduced the family fortune. Honouring his services and talent, the scholars of Kashi gave him the title **'Bharatendu'** (the Moon of India) in **1880**, and this name became his identity.

## The Bharatendu Age and Legacy

Bharatendu died on **6 January 1885** at the age of only **34**. In this short life he produced a large body of work in poetry, drama, essays, travel writing and history.

A group of writers formed around him, called the **'Bharatendu Mandal'** — it included writers such as **Pratapnarayan Mishra**, **Balkrishna Bhatt** and **Badrinarayan Chaudhary 'Premghan'**, who brought out magazines of their own. Historians of Hindi literature call the period from about **1850 to 1900** the **'Bharatendu Age'** after him. In this age Hindi literature moved away from courtly and romantic poetry and began to speak of society, the nation and the common man. The stream of modern Hindi prose, journalism and theatre that still flows today has its source, to a large extent, in Bharatendu Harishchandra.
