## Background: the farmers of Kheda and Amul

At the time of independence India suffered from a severe shortage of milk, and the needs of big cities were met by imported milk powder. In Kheda (Kaira) district of Gujarat, small livestock keepers were forced to sell milk at prices fixed by middlemen and a private dairy company. Inspired by Sardar Vallabhbhai Patel, under the leadership of **Tribhuvandas Patel** the farmers here formed their own cooperative union in 1946, the **Kaira District Cooperative Milk Producers' Union**, which later became famous as **Amul**.

In 1949 the government sent the engineer **Verghese Kurien** (1921–2012) to a government creamery at Anand. He wanted to quit and go back, but at Tribhuvandas Patel's urging he stayed there and joined the cooperative union. His colleague **H. M. Dalaya** developed the technology to make skimmed milk powder and condensed milk from buffalo milk; since buffalo milk was abundant in India, this proved decisive.

## From the Anand model to the NDDB

The special feature of the 'Anand model' was a three-tier cooperative system: the village milk cooperative society collected the milk, the district union processed it, and a state-level federation sold it. The farmers themselves were the owners of these institutions, so a large share of the profit reached them.

In October 1964 Prime Minister **Lal Bahadur Shastri** came to Anand and was impressed by this system. At his initiative the **National Dairy Development Board (NDDB)** was set up at Anand itself in **1965**, with Kurien given the charge. The aim was to build cooperative societies like Anand's across the whole country.

## Operation Flood: three phases

The NDDB launched 'Operation Flood' in **1970** (the exact date of the start differs among sources). The idea was a 'national milk grid' that would link village producers directly with consumers in cities.

- **First phase (1970–1980):** Its cost was raised by selling in India milk powder and butter oil donated by the European Economic Community (EEC) through the World Food Programme. Eighteen major 'milksheds' (milk regions) of the country were linked to Delhi, Mumbai, Kolkata and Chennai.
- **Second phase (1981–1985):** Milksheds grew from 18 to 136; by the end of 1985 about 4.25 million milk producers had joined through about 43,000 village cooperative societies. A World Bank loan was also used in this.
- **Third phase (1985–1996):** About 30,000 new societies were added, and services such as veterinary care, cattle feed and artificial insemination were expanded. The number of women members and women's societies also grew.

## Achievements

Within thirty years the availability of milk per person in the country nearly doubled. In **1998** India overtook the United States to become the world's largest milk producer. Dairying became a major source of self-employment in rural India, especially for landless and small farmers and for women, for whom even a few animals became a source of regular cash income.

On the same model a cooperative project for edible oil ('Operation Golden Flow') was later run. Sri Lanka, Pakistan and several other countries also showed interest in the Anand model.

## Criticisms and debate

The White Revolution also drew some criticism. Some scholars questioned how justified the early dependence on donated foreign milk powder was, and whether the emphasis on crossbred animals led to neglect of indigenous breeds. Political interference in cooperative institutions and the failure to repeat Anand's success in many states were also mentioned. In the 2000s differences also surfaced between Kurien himself and his successor Amrita Patel over the direction of the NDDB and Amul.

## Importance

The White Revolution showed that small producers, by organising, can compete with big companies in the market. Together with the Green Revolution it strengthened India's food security. Kurien received the Ramon Magsaysay Award in 1964, the World Food Prize in 1989 and the Padma Vibhushan in 1999; even today he is called the 'Father of the White Revolution'.
