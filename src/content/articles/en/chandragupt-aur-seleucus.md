## Background: The Vacuum After Alexander

After Alexander's death in 323 BCE, his vast empire was divided among his generals. The Greek satraps and armies left behind in north-western India gradually weakened. In this period **Chandragupta Maurya** removed the Nanda dynasty, took the throne of Magadha and extended his power to Punjab and the Indus region.

Meanwhile **Seleucus**, one of Alexander's generals, took control of Babylon and then extended his kingdom eastwards, up to Iran and Bactria. Recovering Alexander's former Indian territories was his next aim. Here he clashed with Chandragupta. Greek writers call Chandragupta **"Sandrocottus"**.

## The Conflict: What Happened, and How Much Do We Know?

In about **305 BCE** Seleucus crossed the Indus river and advanced towards India. The Roman historian **Appian** writes that Seleucus crossed the Indus and fought Sandrocottus until the two came to friendship and a marriage alliance.

How this war was fought, and whether any major battle took place, is not recorded in any surviving account. Historians are also divided on who won. Many scholars, such as A. L. Basham, believe that Seleucus suffered a loss, since he had to give up territory. Other scholars point out that no ancient writer clearly names either side as the winner, and that the conflict was perhaps neither long nor very severe.

## Terms of the Treaty

According to the Greek geographer **Strabo**, Seleucus gave the lands along the Indus to Chandragupta — "on the condition of a marriage alliance and receiving in return **five hundred elephants**." Modern scholars hold that the treaty had three main terms:
- **Transfer of territories** — Gandhara, Paropamisadae (the Kabul region) and parts of eastern Gedrosia (Makran), and perhaps Arachosia (the Kandahar region) as well. Whether Aria (Herat) was included is deeply disputed, and many scholars consider this wrong.
- **500 war elephants** — which Seleucus received.
- **A marriage alliance** (the Greek word *epigamia*) — its exact meaning is unclear. It is possible that a woman of Seleucus's family was married to Chandragupta, or that intermarriage between the two peoples was permitted.

The Greek and Aramaic inscriptions of Ashoka found at Kandahar in Afghanistan show that this region later remained part of the Mauryan empire.

## The Elephants' Later Journey

These elephants proved very useful to Seleucus. In 301 BCE, at the Battle of **Ipsus** (in present-day Turkey), Seleucus and his allies defeated the rival general Antigonus, and the elephants are believed to have played a big part in this victory. This victory gave Seleucus's empire a firm foundation. After this, Indian elephants became a hallmark of the Seleucid kings — elephants also appear on their coins.

## Envoys and Friendship

After the treaty Seleucus sent **Megasthenes** as ambassador to Chandragupta's court at Pataliputra. His work *Indica* became the greatest source of Greek knowledge about Mauryan India. The exchange continued — a Greek envoy named **Deimachus** came to the court of Chandragupta's son **Bindusara**. Ashoka's Thirteenth Rock Edict also mentions a Greek king named "Antiyoka", who is taken to be Seleucus's grandson Antiochus II.

## Significance

This treaty is among the earliest known agreements between an Indian state and a Greek-Macedonian state. It brought the western border of the Mauryan empire up to around the Hindu Kush, and for about three generations this border remained peaceful. This contact also opened the way for an exchange of knowledge, trade and art between India and the Mediterranean world.
