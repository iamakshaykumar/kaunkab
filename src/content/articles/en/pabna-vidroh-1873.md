## Background: The Growing Burden of Rent

After the **Permanent Settlement** of **1793**, zamindars in Bengal were recognised as owners of the land. The revenue they paid the government was fixed, but zamindars kept finding new ways to raise rent from the peasants (ryots). Besides the fixed rent, many illegal levies called **'abwab'** were collected, and peasants who refused were **evicted** from their land.

The **Act X of 1859** had given **occupancy rights** to peasants who had cultivated the same land continuously for twelve years, and had also limited the grounds for raising rent. But powerful zamindars evaded these provisions by repeatedly shifting peasants from one plot to another or by forging papers.

In **Pabna** district of East Bengal, **jute** cultivation had at that time improved the condition of some peasants and also given them confidence. But around **1873** jute prices fell and famine-like conditions began to develop. At this very moment some zamindars announced steep increases in rent.

## The Spark: The Urkandi Case

When 43 leading peasants of **Urkandi** village in **Yusufshahi pargana** (now **Sirajganj** district of Bangladesh) refused to pay the enhanced rent, the zamindars sued them. In **April 1872** the munsif of Shahzadpur ruled in favour of the zamindars, but in **December 1872** the civil judge of Rajshahi reversed this decision and found that the zamindars had forged documents. This victory gave the peasants confidence that a legal battle could be won if they were organised.

## Formation of the Agrarian League

In **May 1873** the peasants of Yusufshahi pargana formed an **'Agrarian League'** (krishak sangh). Its main leader was **Ishan Chandra Roy**, affectionately called **'Ishan Raja'**, along with **Shambhunath Pal** and **Khudi Mollah**. The League's methods were:

- Holding meetings from village to village and gathering peasants by beating drums.
- **Collective refusal** to pay enhanced rent and illegal levies.
- **Raising funds** to face the zamindars' lawsuits and fighting together in court.
- Volunteer bands for protection against the zamindars' lathiyals.

The peasants' slogan was that they wanted to be **"the ryots of Her Majesty the Queen and of Her alone"**, meaning that they had no quarrel with the British government and their protest was only against the zamindars' arbitrariness. In some places peasants even declared their parganas free of zamindari control.

## Spread of the Movement and the Government's Stand

The movement remained largely **peaceful and legal**, though in some places there were violent clashes between the zamindars' agents and peasants. Soon similar agrarian leagues began to form in other East Bengal districts such as **Dhaka, Mymensingh, Tripura, Bakarganj, Faridpur, Bogra and Rajshahi**.

In a proclamation of **4 July 1873**, the Lieutenant Governor of Bengal, **Sir George Campbell**, said that the government would protect people from coercion and extortion, and advised the zamindars to pursue their claims only through legal means. At the same time the police acted against those causing disturbances. The zamindars and newspapers that supported them tried to portray the movement as a revolt of Muslim peasants against Hindu zamindars, but the leadership included people of both communities and this colouring did not take hold much.

Several educated Indians spoke up for the peasants, prominent among them **Romesh Chunder Dutt**, **Bankim Chandra Chattopadhyay** and later **Surendranath Banerjee**'s **Indian Association**. Amid the famine of **1873–74** and police action the movement gradually calmed down, but its waves kept rising in different places until **1876**.

## Results and Significance

The Pabna movement forced the government to reconsider Bengal's tenancy laws. After long inquiry and debate, the **Bengal Tenancy Act** was passed in **1885**. It gave occupancy rights to a peasant who had cultivated land in a village for twelve years, and restricted zamindars from arbitrarily raising rent and from evicting tenants without a court order.

Historians count the Pabna uprising among the nineteenth-century peasant movements that relied more on **organisation, solidarity and legal struggle** than on arms. Its target was not the colonial government but zamindari exploitation; this was both its limitation and the reason for its success. The Pabna Agrarian League became an important example for later peasant movements.
