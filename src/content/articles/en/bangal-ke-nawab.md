## Murshid Quli Khan — From Diwan to Nawab

Sources differ on the early life of **Murshid Quli Khan**. According to one view based on Jadunath Sarkar and the Persian work 'Maasir-ul-Umara', he was born in a Hindu Brahmin family (under the name Surya Narayan Mishra) and in childhood was bought by the Iranian official Haji Shafi, who raised him under the name Muhammad Hadi. His skill in revenue administration brought him to the notice of **Aurangzeb**, and around **1700** he was made the **diwan** (head of revenue) of Bengal.

After a clash with Prince **Azim-ush-Shan**, the governor of Bengal, he moved the diwani office from **Dhaka** to Makhsusabad, which around 1703–04 was named **Murshidabad** after him. In **1717** Emperor **Farrukhsiyar** made him governor of Bengal. Now both diwani and nizamat were in one hand, and Bengal became in practice an autonomous nawabi.

## Revenue System and the Zamindars

Murshid Quli Khan built a system of strict revenue collection. Khalisa land was increased in place of jagirs, and land revenue began to be collected through contractors (ijaradars), which increased the power of big **zamindar** families. Even so, he kept sending revenue regularly to Delhi — at a time when money from the other provinces had almost stopped coming. He died on **30 June 1727**, and his son-in-law **Shuja-ud-din** became nawab, followed by Sarfaraz Khan.

## Alivardi Khan and the Maratha Invasions

**Alivardi Khan**, the deputy governor (naib nazim) of Bihar since 1733, defeated and killed Nawab **Sarfaraz Khan** at the Battle of **Giria** on **10 April 1740** and became nawab himself. Later he also obtained recognition from Delhi.

Most of Alivardi's reign was spent battling invasions by the armies of **Raghuji Bhonsle** of Nagpur. From 1742, for about ten years, Maratha horsemen — called "bargis" in Bengal — carried out heavy plunder in West Bengal, Bihar and Orissa. In 1744 Alivardi had the Maratha commander Bhaskar Pant murdered on the pretext of negotiations, but the invasions did not stop. Finally, in the treaty of **March 1751**, Alivardi agreed to pay an annual **chauth of 1.2 million rupees** and to hand over a large part of Orissa to the Marathas.

## The European Companies

The trade of Bengal — cloth, saltpetre, silk — was extremely valuable to the British, French and Dutch companies. Alivardi let them trade, but kept them from fighting among themselves in his territory or from increasing their fortifications.

## Siraj-ud-Daulah and Plassey

On **9 April 1756** Alivardi died, and his grandson **Siraj-ud-Daulah**, about 23 years old, became nawab. His opponents in the court — including his relatives, the commander **Mir Jafar** and the big banker Jagat Seth — were already active. Angered by the British strengthening Calcutta's fortifications without permission and misusing trade concessions, Siraj captured Calcutta in **June 1756**. In the 'Black Hole' story linked with this event, historians have serious doubts about the number of those who died.

In early 1757 Clive retook Calcutta and then made an agreement with the conspirators in the court. On **23 June 1757** at **Plassey**, Siraj was defeated because Mir Jafar's troops stayed inactive, and he was killed a few days later. Mir Jafar became nawab with the support of the Company.

## Significance

This forty-year period of the Nawabs of Bengal shows how almost independent regional states emerged from within the Mughal empire itself. Bengal was India's richest province, and after Plassey, control over its revenue gave the East India Company the base to build an empire in India.
