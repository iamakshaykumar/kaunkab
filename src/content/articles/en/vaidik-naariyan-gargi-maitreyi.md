## The Voice of Women in the Texts

Our knowledge of the Vedic age (about 1500 to 500 BCE) comes mainly from the **Vedas**, the **Brahmana** texts and the **Upanishads**. These texts are mostly believed to be the work of male sages, but in them the names of some women also appear, as seers of mantras (**rishikas**) or as learned women taking part in philosophical discussion. Later tradition called such women **brahmavadinis** — that is, those who discussed the deep knowledge of Brahman and the Veda. In contrast, for women focused on the household the word **sadyovadhu** is found.

By one count, about **27 women** in the Rigveda are regarded as rishikas of hymns, with names such as **Lopamudra**, **Ghosha**, **Vishvavara** and **Apala**. Note that the names of the composers of hymns are tied to tradition; modern historians are cautious about accepting all of them as historical authors.

## Lopamudra: A Dialogue in the Rigveda

**Lopamudra** was the wife of the sage **Agastya**. **Hymn 1.179** of the first mandala of the Rigveda is a dialogue between husband and wife. In it Lopamudra tells her ascetic husband that her age is passing in years of austerity and that household life and love should not be neglected; Agastya answers. This dialogue is special because in it a woman openly expresses her desire and feeling.

In the later **Mahabharata** there is a story of Lopamudra in which she is raised as the daughter of the king of **Vidarbha** and sets her own conditions before marrying Agastya. This story is mythological, not historical evidence.

## Ghosha: Praise of the Ashvins

**Ghosha** was the daughter of the sage **Kakshivan** and granddaughter of **Dirghatamas** — both sages associated with Rigvedic hymns. **Hymns 39 and 40** of the tenth mandala of the Rigveda are in her name. The first hymn praises the physician gods, the **Ashvins**, and the second is more personal, reflecting a wish for married life.

According to tradition, Ghosha suffered for a long time from a skin disease, because of which she could not marry. The story says that the Ashvins cured her, after which she married and had a son, **Suhastya**, in whose name too there is a hymn. The parts about the disease and miraculous cure should be taken only as legend.

## Gargi: Questions in Janaka's Assembly

The **Brihadaranyaka Upanishad** of the Later Vedic period describes a famous assembly of **Janaka**, king of Videha, where the greatest knower of Brahman was to be decided. Here many scholars questioned the sage **Yajnavalkya**, among whom **Gargi Vachaknavi** — daughter of the sage Vachaknu and of the Garga clan — is the most famous.

Gargi asked one after another in what this world is "woven" (like warp and weft) — in what is water, in what is air, in what is space? This chain of answers finally reaches **space (akasha)** and then the **Akshara** (the imperishable principle, Brahman). In the Upanishad Gargi questions twice (in the sixth and eighth Brahmanas of chapter 3) and the second time tells the assembly that if Yajnavalkya answers her two questions, no one will be able to defeat him. Her questions became a symbol of deep curiosity in Indian philosophy.

## Maitreyi: Wealth or Immortality?

The same Upanishad mentions two wives of Yajnavalkya — **Maitreyi** and **Katyayani**. When Yajnavalkya, before taking up renunciation, wants to divide his property between the two, Maitreyi asks whether even the whole earth filled with wealth could make her immortal. On getting the answer "no", she says — **"What shall I do with that by which I cannot become immortal?"** After this Yajnavalkya teaches her about the **Atman**: husband, wife, children or wealth — all are dear only because of the Atman. This dialogue comes in two places in the Upanishad (**2.4** and **4.5**).

Scholars disagree on the interpretation of this dialogue. Some, such as Karen Pechilis, regard Maitreyi as an independent-minded questioner; while scholars such as Wendy Doniger say that the real teacher here is Yajnavalkya and Maitreyi is merely a character in the story.

## A Balanced View

The examples of these learned women show that in Vedic society some women got the opportunity for education, composing mantras and philosophical debate. But it would not be right to take them as evidence of the condition of all women. These are a few names, mostly connected with sage families; Vedic society was fundamentally **patriarchal**, and many historians believe that in the Later Vedic period women's rights gradually became limited. Some call this age a "golden age" for women, while others consider that an exaggeration.

Even so, the names of Gargi, Maitreyi, Lopamudra and Ghosha have today become symbols of knowledge and curiosity — there is even a college named after Maitreyi in New Delhi.
