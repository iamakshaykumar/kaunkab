## Heir of the Chahamana Dynasty

**Prithviraj III**, whom folk tradition also calls **Rai Pithora**, was a king of the **Chahamana (Chauhan)** dynasty. The kingdom of this dynasty had begun in the Shakambhari (Sambhar) region and in the 12th century its capital was **Ajmer**. On the basis of the astrological details given in the contemporary poem *Prithviraj Vijaya*, he is believed to have been born in Gujarat in **about 1166 CE**. His father **Someshvara** was a Chahamana king and his mother **Karpuradevi** a Kalachuri princess.

After Someshvara's death in **1177**, Prithviraj became king while still a child. In the early years the government was run by the regency of his mother Karpuradevi and a council of ministers, in which the minister **Kadambavasa** was prominent. According to the historian Dasharatha Sharma, by about **1180** Prithviraj had taken the government into his own hands.

## Early Campaigns

As soon as he took charge of the kingdom Prithviraj had to face the revolt of his cousin **Nagarjuna**, which he suppressed and regained control of Gudapura. According to a Jain text, before 1182 he defeated a group called the **Bhadanakas**.

In **1182–83** he attacked Jejakabhukti (Bundelkhand), the kingdom of the Chandela king **Paramardi**; the Madanpur inscriptions say that he laid waste this region. The plunder of Mahoba and the stories of the bravery of Alha and Udal spread in later folk tradition. Historians believe that this occupation was short-lived, because Paramardi continued to rule for about ten years after Prithviraj's death.

In the west there was also a conflict with Bhima II, the **Chaulukya (Solanki)** king of Gujarat, which ended in a treaty before 1187. In the east the **Gahadavala** king **Jayachandra** of Kannauj was his rival.

## Tarain: Victory and Defeat

From the north-west **Muhammad of Ghor**, the ruler of Ghor, was advancing. In **1191**, in the first battle of Tarain (in present-day Haryana), Prithviraj's army defeated Ghori; Ghori returned wounded. After this Prithviraj laid a long siege to the fort of Tabarhind (Bathinda).

In **1192** Ghori returned with a large cavalry force, especially fast mounted archers, and in the **second battle of Tarain** Prithviraj was defeated. A detailed account of this battle is in a separate article, "The Battle of Tarain".

## The End: Differences in the Sources

Prithviraj died in **1192**, but sources do not agree on how. Hasan Nizami writes that he was put to death some time later on a charge of conspiracy; the *Prabandha Chintamani* speaks of beheading, and the *Hammira Mahakavya* of death in captivity. Some coins found in Delhi bear the names of both Prithviraj and 'Muhammad bin Sam', from which some historians conjecture that after the defeat he was kept for some time as a subordinate ruler.

Ghori made Prithviraj's son **Govindaraja** a subordinate king at Ajmer, but Prithviraj's brother **Harirāja** removed him, and Govindaraja went to Ranthambore.

## Prithviraj Raso: Poetry or History?

Prithviraj's popular image today is formed mainly from the **Prithviraj Raso**, credited to his court poet **Chand Bardai**. Its available form is thought to be from many centuries later and it contains later passages, exaggerations and chronological errors, so historians do not regard it as a reliable historical source. In comparison, Jayanaka's contemporary Sanskrit poem **Prithviraj Vijaya** is more trustworthy.

Two famous stories fall in the category of **legend**:
- **Samyogita** — Prithviraj carrying her off from the swayamvara of Jayachandra's daughter. This story is in the Raso and later texts, but is not found in Gahadavala inscriptions or contemporary sources.
- **The sound-guided arrow (shabdabhedi baan)** — the blinded Prithviraj killing Ghori at Ghazni. This is not historical; Ghori died in 1206, about fourteen years after Prithviraj.

## Legacy

Prithviraj was a powerful and ambitious king of 12th-century North India, who fought continuous wars with neighbouring kingdoms. After his defeat the Delhi-Ajmer region came under Ghori's commanders, which later laid the foundation of the Delhi Sultanate. Folk poetry made him a symbol of valour — when reading history it is necessary to recognise these two layers separately.
