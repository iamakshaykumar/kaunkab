## Government Changes after the Revolt

**The revolt of 1857** was the biggest blow to British power. Fighting went on for months in centres such as Meerut, Delhi, Kanpur, Lucknow and Jhansi. The revolt was crushed, but in Britain the question was loudly raised of how far it was right to let a trading company rule such a large country.

**The East India Company** was formed for trade in 1600, but after **Plassey (1757)** and **Buxar (1764)** it gradually became the ruler of a large part of India. Parliament had already increased its control over the Company through laws such as the Regulating Act of 1773 and Pitt's India Act of 1784. After 1857 the British government decided that the Crown would now take over the government completely.

## The Government of India Act 1858

The bill was introduced by the government of Prime Minister **Lord Palmerston**, but his government fell and the law was passed under the government of **Lord Derby**. It received royal assent on **2 August 1858**. It is also called the "Act for the Better Government of India". Its main provisions were:

- **End of Company rule** — all the Company's governing powers, territories, army and property were handed over to the British Crown.
- **Secretary of State for India** — a member of the British cabinet became the **Secretary of State for India**, with full responsibility for the government of India. He was answerable to the British Parliament. The first Secretary of State was **Lord Stanley** (later the 15th Earl of Derby).
- **Council of India** — an advisory council of **15 members** (the Council of India) was created to assist the Secretary of State. It only gave advice; the final decision belonged to the Secretary of State.
- **Board of Control and Court of Directors abolished** — the system of dual government run through these Company bodies was ended.
- **Viceroy** — the Governor-General of India was now also the representative of the Crown, and so came to be called the **Viceroy**. **Lord Canning** became the last Governor-General (of the Company) and the first Viceroy.

## The Allahabad Durbar and the Queen's Proclamation

The law came into force on **1 November 1858**. On that day a grand durbar was held at **Allahabad**, where Lord Canning read out **Queen Victoria's Proclamation**. It was addressed to "the Princes, Chiefs, and People of India" and was read out across the country in English and in Indian languages.

The main promises of the Proclamation were:

- **No annexation of the states** — the British would not extend their territory any further. The rights, dignity and honour of the native princes would be respected, and all their treaties with the Company would be honoured by the Crown. With this, Dalhousie's **Doctrine of Lapse** came to an end in practice.
- **Religious toleration** — the government would not impose its religion on anyone and would not interfere in the religious beliefs and worship of its subjects.
- **Equal opportunity** — qualified Indians would be appointed to government offices without discrimination of caste, creed or race.
- **Respect for traditions** — in making and enforcing laws, due regard would be paid to the ancient rights, customs and land rights of India.
- **Amnesty** — pardon would be granted to all those who had taken part in the revolt and were not directly involved in the murder of British subjects.

## Promises and Reality

The language of the Proclamation was generous, but in practice many promises remained unfulfilled. The examination for the **Indian Civil Service**, which filled the higher posts of the administration, was held in London, making it very difficult for Indians to get in. The army was reorganised so as to increase the proportion of European soldiers and to recruit from castes and regions considered "loyal".

The British made the princely states their allies. Rulers who had sided with them in 1857 were rewarded, and the states later became a strong pillar of British rule. The policy of not interfering in the customs of Indian society also slowed the pace of social reform.

Later, in **1876**, the British Parliament passed the **Royal Titles Act**, and on **1 January 1877**, at Lord Lytton's **Delhi Durbar**, Queen Victoria was proclaimed **"Empress of India"**.

## Significance and Legacy

The Act of 1858 marked the formal beginning of the **British Raj** (Crown rule) in India, which lasted until **1947**. It created a centralised structure of government — the Secretary of State in London, the Viceroy in India and the provincial governments below him. Later constitutional laws, such as the **Indian Councils Acts of 1861, 1892 and 1909**, were built on this structure.

The Queen's Proclamation became a touchstone in Indian politics. Early nationalist leaders such as **Dadabhai Naoroji** called it the **"Magna Carta of India"** and demanded that the British honour its promises of equality. In this way the end of Company rule was a turning point in Indian history — the form of government changed, but the struggle for India's freedom still lay ahead.
