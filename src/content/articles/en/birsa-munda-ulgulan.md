## Background: The Breakdown of the Khuntkatti System

Among the **Munda** tribals of Chotanagpur there was a tradition of collective rights over land, called the **Khuntkatti** system — the descendants of the clan that cleared the forest and founded the village were regarded as joint owners of that land. In the nineteenth century, with British rule, landlords, contractors and moneylenders who came from outside (called **'dikus'** in the tribals' language) spread across the region. The Mundas were made to do forced labour, heavy rent was collected, and their lands slowly began to slip out of their hands. New forest laws also restricted their traditional rights over the forest.

For several decades the Munda chiefs tried to recover their land through petitions and lawsuits — this is called the 'Sardari Larai' (the chiefs' struggle) — but they did not achieve much success.

## Birsa's Early Life

Birsa Munda's birth is generally placed on **15 November 1875** in the village of **Ulihatu** (today in Khunti district, Jharkhand); some sources give a different year. His family were poor sharecroppers. His early schooling was in Jaipal Nag's school, then he studied at the German mission school at **Chaibasa**, where he adopted Christianity. Later, disillusioned with the missionaries, he left the mission.

## Dharti Aba: From Religious Reform to Political Movement

In the mid-1890s Birsa emerged as a religious reformer. He preached worship of one God, giving up intoxicants, pure conduct and staying away from old superstitions. His followers began calling him **'Dharti Aba'** (Father of the Earth), and his creed was called 'Birsait'.

His message soon became political. Birsa said that Munda rule would return and that the dikus and British rule must leave this land. In August 1895 he was arrested and sentenced to two years. After release from jail, he went on secretly organising his followers.

## The Ulgulan: The Great Rebellion

Around **Christmas** 1899, Birsa's followers began the **Ulgulan** (great rebellion). In Khunti, Tamar, Basia and the areas around Ranchi, attacks with bows and arrows were made on missions, police stations, government buildings and the bases of landlords and contractors. In early January 1900 the Khunti police station was attacked.

In January 1900, government forces fired on Birsa's supporters gathered on **Dombari Buru** (Dombari hill). The number of those killed is deeply disputed — official figures are low, while folk memory and some accounts give far higher numbers of deaths, including women and children.

## Arrest and Death

Birsa stayed hidden in the forests, but on **3 February 1900** he was captured from the Jamkopai forest near Chakradharpur. Hundreds of his companions were tried. During the trial itself, on **9 June 1900**, Birsa died in **Ranchi jail**. The official report gave the cause of death as illness (cholera), but doubts have also continued to be expressed. He was then about twenty-five years old.

## Consequences and Legacy

The Ulgulan was crushed, but it forced the government to pay attention to the question of tribal land. Land surveys and settlements were carried out, Khuntkatti rights received some recognition, and in **1908** the **Chotanagpur Tenancy Act** was passed, which prohibits the transfer of tribal land to non-tribals.

Today Birsa Munda is a symbol of self-respect and resistance for the tribal communities of Jharkhand and the whole country. The airport at Ranchi and many universities and institutions are named after him.
