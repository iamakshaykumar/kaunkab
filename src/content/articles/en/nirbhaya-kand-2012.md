## That Night

On the night of **16 December 2012**, a young physiotherapy intern, returning home with a friend after watching a film, boarded a private bus from the **Munirka** area of south Delhi. There were six men on the bus, including the driver, who badly beat her friend in the moving bus, gang-raped the young woman and inflicted serious injuries on her. Later both were thrown out by the roadside.

On information from passers-by, police took both to **Safdarjung Hospital**. The young woman's condition was extremely critical and she underwent several operations. On **26 December** the government decided to send her to **Mount Elizabeth Hospital** in Singapore for better treatment, but she died there on **29 December 2012**.

The law does not permit the victim's name to be published, so the country knew her as **'Nirbhaya'** (fearless). Later her mother **Asha Devi** said publicly that she had no hesitation in revealing her daughter's name.

## A Country on the Streets

As soon as news of the incident spread, anger erupted in Delhi. Thousands of students, young people, women and ordinary citizens came onto the streets without a call from any political party. On **21–22 December** big protests took place at **India Gate** and at **Raisina Hill**, near Rashtrapati Bhavan and Parliament. To stop the crowds the police used tear gas, water cannon and batons, which drew strong criticism. The administration closed several metro stations and imposed Section 144 in the area.

The protests were not limited to Delhi. Candlelight marches and sit-ins were held in Mumbai, Kolkata, Bengaluru, Chennai and many smaller cities too. After Nirbhaya's death, condolence meetings were held across the country. The protesters demanded strict punishment for the culprits, speedy trials in sexual offence cases, police accountability and safe public spaces for women. Several international bodies, including the United Nations, also expressed concern over the incident.

## The Justice Verma Committee

In view of the public outrage, on **22 December 2012** the central government set up a three-member committee chaired by former Chief Justice of India **Justice J. S. Verma**. It included **Leila Seth**, former Chief Justice of the Himachal Pradesh High Court, and former Solicitor General **Gopal Subramanium**. The committee was to suggest within a month what changes should be made to criminal law to deal with sexual offences.

The committee submitted its report in only **29 days**, on **23 January 2013**. Meanwhile it received about **80,000 suggestions** from ordinary citizens, lawyers, women's organisations and experts. The report said that failures of the government and the police lay at the root of crimes against women. The committee made suggestions such as clearly defining offences like stalking, voyeurism and acid attacks, police reforms, and attention to sexual offence cases registered against people contesting elections. The committee did not recommend the death penalty for rape.

## The Criminal Law (Amendment) Act, 2013

On the basis of the report, President **Pranab Mukherjee** issued an ordinance on **3 February 2013**. Later a bill was brought in its place, which was passed by the Lok Sabha on **19 March** and the Rajya Sabha on **21 March 2013**. Presidential assent came on **2 April 2013**, and it was deemed to be in force from **3 February 2013**. In common speech it is also called the **'Nirbhaya Act'**.

This Act made several changes to the Indian Penal Code, the Code of Criminal Procedure and the Evidence Act:

- The definition of rape was broadened.
- **Acid attack**, **stalking**, **voyeurism** and sexual harassment were added as separate offences.
- Provision was made for punishment up to the **death penalty** where rape causes the victim's death or leaves her in a persistent vegetative state, and for repeat offenders.
- Punishment was laid down for police officers who fail to register a complaint, along with a sensitive procedure for recording the victim's statement.

## Trial and Further Changes

All six accused were arrested. One accused died in Tihar Jail in March 2013. One accused was a juvenile, who was sent to a reform home for the maximum of three years under the juvenile justice law. The remaining four were sentenced to death in **September 2013** by a fast-track court in Delhi, which was upheld by the Delhi High Court in 2014 and by the Supreme Court in **May 2017**. After all appeals were rejected, the four were hanged in Tihar Jail on **20 March 2020**.

After the debate over the juvenile accused's light sentence, the **Juvenile Justice Act, 2015** was enacted, under which juveniles aged 16 to 18 can be tried as adults for heinous crimes. The government also created the **Nirbhaya Fund** for schemes related to women's safety.

## Legacy

The Nirbhaya case changed the public debate on women's safety in India for ever. After it, awareness about reporting sexual offences grew, and helplines, 'One Stop Centres' and fast-track courts were set up in many cities. Still, many experts and women's organisations say that strict laws alone are not enough; police sensitivity, speedy justice and a change in society's thinking are equally necessary. That incident of December 2012 still reminds us that the courage of one young woman and the voices of crores of people forced the country to rethink its laws and its responsibility.
