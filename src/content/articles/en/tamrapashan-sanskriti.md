## Copper and Stone Together

**Chalcolithic** means the period when people began using **copper** along with small stone tools. In India, during and after the time of the Harappan cities, many such village farming communities flourished in western and central India. These people did not build cities and did not use writing, but lived in permanent villages practising farming and animal herding.

These cultures are identified mainly by their **painted pottery**, and they are named after the sites where they were first recognised.

## The Ahar-Banas Culture (Rajasthan)

More than 90 sites of this culture have been found in the valley of the **Banas** and **Berach** rivers in south-eastern Rajasthan — the main ones being **Ahar** near Udaipur, **Gilund** and **Balathal**. Because they lived near the copper mines of the Aravalli range, the people here made copper axes and other objects. An old name of Ahar is also said to be **Tambavati**.

The culture is identified by its **black-and-red** pottery, decorated with white lines and dots. At Gilund, a storeroom full of more than 100 seal impressions has also been found. Its dates are debated — some scholars place it as far back as the third millennium BCE, and even earlier on the basis of radiocarbon dates from Balathal.

## The Malwa Culture (Madhya Pradesh)

The Malwa culture has been found at sites such as **Kayatha**, **Navdatoli** (near Maheshwar, on the Narmada bank) and **Eran** on the Malwa plateau of Madhya Pradesh. Its pottery was thick, pink or red in colour, and was painted in black with geometric and natural designs. It is generally placed at about **1600–1400 BCE**, though different sources give different dates. People of the Malwa culture later reached the Deccan as well.

## The Jorwe Culture (Maharashtra)

In Maharashtra the **Jorwe culture** followed Malwa, named after the village of Jorwe in Ahmednagar district. Two phases are recognised — **Early Jorwe** (about 1400–1000 BCE) and **Late Jorwe** (about 1000–700 BCE). More than 200 sites have been found, of which **Daimabad**, **Inamgaon**, **Nevasa** and **Prakash** are the main ones. Daimabad was the largest settlement, surrounded by a mud wall.

Jorwe pottery was red-orange, wheel-made and decorated with black geometric designs. Around 1000 BCE most Jorwe settlements were abandoned — probably because of drought or famine. Those that survived show growing poverty.

## Inamgaon — The Whole Life of a Village

**Inamgaon**, on the banks of the **Ghod River**, about 89 kilometres east of Pune, was excavated by a Deccan College, Pune team led by **H. D. Sankalia**, **M. K. Dhavalikar** and **Z. D. Ansari**. Three phases of settlement from about 1600 to 700 BCE were found here — Malwa, Early Jorwe and Late Jorwe.

The houses here were first round and then large and rectangular; in the Late Jorwe period small round huts returned. The excavators identified public structures such as **granaries**, **irrigation channels** and **embankments**. In a large house near the granary, a man was buried in a seated posture in a **large four-legged urn**, which the excavators took to be the grave of the village **headman**. This interpretation suggests that social inequality had begun to develop here — though scholars are not fully agreed even on this conclusion.

## Significance

The Chalcolithic cultures show that settled farming life continued in large parts of India outside the Harappan cities and after their decline. The roots of the settlement patterns, crops and burial traditions of many Deccan villages are seen in these communities.
