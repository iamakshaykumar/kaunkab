## Rise of the Namdhari Sect

The Namdhari sect in Punjab is believed to have begun with **Baba Balak Singh** (Hazro, North-West Frontier), who stressed simplicity and remembrance of the Name. His disciple **Ram Singh**, who had earlier served in Maharaja Ranjit Singh's army, made his village **Bhaini** in Ludhiana district (now Sri Bhaini Sahib) the centre. According to Namdhari tradition, on **12 April 1857** (Baisakhi) he formally founded the sect there by hoisting a white triangular flag. Sources differ on Ram Singh's year of birth; 1816 is usually given.

## Reform and Boycott

The Namdharis wore white clothes and a distinctive style of turban, kept away from meat, liquor and intoxicants, and opposed idol worship. Ram Singh promoted simple mass weddings against evils such as child marriage and dowry.

The movement also had a political side. Followers were told to boycott jobs in the British government, courts, schools and foreign cloth, and to wear hand-woven cloth. For this reason many historians and Namdhari tradition count Ram Singh among the early practitioners of **non-cooperation and swadeshi**. The movement even created its own postal system and built an organisation by dividing the whole of Punjab into 'subas'.

## Cow Protection and Confrontation

After the annexation of 1849, slaughterhouses opened in Punjab under British administration, which caused deep resentment among many Sikhs and Hindus. In 1871 some Kukas attacked butchers at **Amritsar** and **Raikot**, and several people were killed. Many Namdharis were hanged in these cases. Ram Singh publicly kept away from violence, but the government began to view the whole movement with suspicion.

## The Malerkotla Incident (January 1872)

In January 1872 a band of about 150–200 Kukas, led by **Hira Singh** and **Lahna Singh**, first attacked the fort at **Malaudh** and then, on **15 January**, the Muslim princely state of **Malerkotla**, where there was tension over cow slaughter. People were killed on both sides and the band was later captured.

**John Lamb Cowan**, the Deputy Commissioner of Ludhiana, ordered the prisoners to be tied to the mouths of cannons and blown away, without any trial. On **17 January 1872**, 49 Kukas were blown away in groups of seven; the next day 16 more were killed on the orders of Commissioner **Forsyth**. The total is given as 65 or 66. In Namdhari tradition the story of **Bishan Singh**, about 12 years old, is famous: he was offered pardon but refused it. Cowan was removed from service for defying the instructions of senior officials.

## Ram Singh's Exile and Legacy

Although he was not directly involved in the incident, **Satguru Ram Singh** was arrested under Regulation III of 1818 and exiled to **Rangoon** (Burma); later he was sent to Mergui. British records give his death as 1885, but the Namdhari community does not accept this.

The Kuka movement was a mixture of religious reform, social reform and political resistance. Historians differ in their assessment of its violent cow-protection incidents, but the brutal punishment at Malerkotla made it a poignant memory of Punjab's freedom struggle.
