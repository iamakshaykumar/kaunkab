## The Chandela Dynasty

In the ninth century the **Chandela** dynasty rose in Bundelkhand (part of present-day Madhya Pradesh and Uttar Pradesh). At first they were feudatories of the Gurjara-Pratiharas of Kannauj, but in the tenth century they became an independent power. Their strong fort was **Kalinjar**, and **Khajuraho** was their religious and cultural centre.

The most active period of temple building was between about **950 and 1050 CE**, although some temples were built earlier and some later. Three kings are especially associated with it:
- **Yashovarman** — in his time the **Lakshmana Temple** was built, completed in the mid-tenth century and dedicated to the Vaikuntha form of Lord Vishnu.
- **Dhanga** — many temples, such as the Vishvanatha Temple, were built under his rule.
- **Vidyadhara** — in his time the **Kandariya Mahadeva Temple** (c. 1025–1050 CE) was built, the largest surviving temple of Khajuraho.

## How Many Temples, and Where

According to tradition and inscriptions, there were about **85 temples** here up to the twelfth century. Today about **25** of them survive, spread over about six square kilometres. The archaeologist Alexander Cunningham divided them into three groups:
- **Western group** — major temples such as Kandariya Mahadeva, Lakshmana, Vishvanatha and Chitragupta.
- **Eastern group** — **Jain** temples such as **Parshvanath**, Adinath and Shantinath.
- **Southern group** — the Duladeo and Chaturbhuj temples.

The presence of Hindu (Shaiva, Vaishnava, Shakta) and Jain temples in one complex is regarded as a sign of the religious tolerance of the time.

## The Nagara Spire

The Khajuraho temples are excellent examples of the **Nagara style** of northern India. They are built of light yellow-brown **sandstone** and stand on a high platform (jagati). The stones are joined with grooves and mortise-and-tenon joints, without lime mortar.

In each temple the entrance porch, mandapa, mahamandapa and sanctum come in a straight line, and their roofs rise gradually higher. The main **spire** above the sanctum is made up of a cluster of smaller spires, so that the whole temple looks like a range of mountain peaks — Kailasa or Meru, the abode of the gods. The Kandariya Mahadeva Temple is about 31 metres high.

## Sculpture

The outer and inner walls of the temples are full of sculpture — gods and goddesses, apsaras (surasundaris), musicians, warriors, hunting and scenes of daily life, animals and imaginary creatures. Khajuraho is also known for some erotic sculptures, but these are a small part of the total. Scholars explain them in different ways, linking them to the Indian view of the four aims of life — dharma, artha, kama and moksha — to the Tantric tradition, or to auspicious symbols; there is no single universally accepted explanation.

## Obscurity, Rediscovery and Conservation

After the Chandelas weakened, Khajuraho gradually came to be neglected. Because of its remote location it escaped major destruction to a large extent, but the forest covered it. Local people knew of it, but it was brought to the outside world in 1838 by the report of the British engineer **T. S. Burt**. Later Alexander Cunningham made a detailed survey of them.

In **1986** UNESCO included the Khajuraho group of monuments in the World Heritage List. Today the Archaeological Survey of India looks after them, and every year in February the **Khajuraho Dance Festival** is held here, in which classical dances are performed against the backdrop of the temples.

## Significance

Khajuraho is a unique site that gives a glimpse of the art, religion and society of medieval India. These temples show how vivid and balanced a world the craftsmen created in stone under the patronage of the Chandela rulers.
