## Why Study the Sun

The **Sun** is the basis of life on Earth, but its activity — such as **solar flares** and **coronal mass ejections** (huge clouds of charged particles thrown out by the Sun) — affects the space around the Earth. Strong solar storms can disrupt satellites, communications, GPS and even power grids. This is called **space weather**.

Another great mystery for scientists is why the Sun's outer layer, the **corona**, has a temperature of over a million degrees, while its visible surface is only about 5,500 degrees Celsius. To study these questions India prepared its first solar mission, named **"Aditya"** after the Sanskrit name for the Sun.

## What Is the L1 Point

There are some special places in space where the gravity of the Earth and the Sun balance in such a way that a spacecraft can stay relatively stable, keeping pace with both. These are called **Lagrange points** — named after the mathematician **Joseph-Louis Lagrange**. The point between the Sun and the Earth, about **1.5 million kilometres** from Earth, is called **L1**.

The biggest advantage of L1 is that the Sun is visible from here **all the time** — neither does the Earth come in between, nor is there any obstruction from eclipses. Also, particles coming from the Sun pass through here before reaching the Earth, so some advance warning of solar storms can be had. Aditya-L1 moves in a large **halo orbit** around this point.

## Launch and Journey

On **2 September 2023** at 11:50 am — just ten days after Chandrayaan-3's landing — the **PSLV-C57** rocket launched Aditya-L1 from the **Satish Dhawan Space Centre** at **Sriharikota**. This spacecraft of about **1,500 kilograms** was first placed in Earth orbit, and its orbit was then raised by firing its engines several times. In mid-September it was taken out of Earth orbit and sent towards L1.

After a journey of about **126 days**, with a final engine firing at about 4 pm on **6 January 2024**, it was placed in a halo orbit around L1. The mission's project director was **Nigar Shaji**, and ISRO's chairman at the time was **S. Somanath**. The first halo orbit was completed on **2 July 2024**.

## Seven Scientific Instruments

Aditya-L1 carries **seven instruments** (payloads), built jointly by ISRO and several of the country's scientific institutions. Four of them look directly at the Sun, and three measure the particles and magnetic field around them at L1 itself:

- **VELC (Visible Emission Line Coronagraph)** — the largest instrument, which studies the corona by creating an artificial "eclipse"; it was built by the **Indian Institute of Astrophysics**, Bengaluru.
- **SUIT (Solar Ultraviolet Imaging Telescope)** — takes pictures of the Sun in ultraviolet light; it was built by **IUCAA**, Pune, with ISRO.
- **SoLEXS** and **HEL1OS** — measure the X-rays from solar flares; both come from the **U. R. Rao Satellite Centre**, Bengaluru.
- **ASPEX** — studies the particles of the solar wind; it was built by the **Physical Research Laboratory**, Ahmedabad.
- **PAPA** — measures the electrons and ions of the solar wind; it was built by the **Vikram Sarabhai Space Centre**, Thiruvananthapuram.
- **Magnetometer** — measures the interplanetary magnetic field at L1.

## Achievements and Significance

Aditya-L1's instruments had started working even on the way; in December 2023 SUIT sent the first full-disk ultraviolet images of the Sun. The powerful solar storm of **May 2024** — counted among the strongest storms of this century, which caused auroras to be seen in many parts of the world — was recorded by several of Aditya-L1's instruments.

The mission's planned life is about **five years**. With Aditya-L1 India joined the small group of countries and agencies whose spacecraft watch the Sun from the L1 point, such as the American-European **SOHO**. Coming just days after Chandrayaan-3, this launch showed that India's space programme was now stepping confidently beyond the Earth and the Moon into scientific missions in deep space as well.
