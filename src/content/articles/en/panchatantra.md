## Five 'Tantras', One Aim

**Panchatantra** means 'five parts' or 'five books'. It is a collection of animal and bird stories written in prose and verse, in which one story is woven inside another. Behind every story there is a lesson — how to make friends, how to avoid enemies, what the results of haste are. In Sanskrit it is regarded as a text of **niti-shastra** — that is, the wisdom of conduct and governance.

## Vishnu Sharma and the Story of the Princes

The introduction of the text is a **frame story**. The three sons of King **Amarashakti** of the city of Mahilaropya avoided studies. Then the old Brahmin **Vishnu Sharma** promised that he would make them skilled in niti in six months — and he did this work through stories.

Keep in mind that this story is part of the text, not proven history. In some versions the author's name is given as **Vasubhaga**, and scholars believe both names are probably imaginary. The date of the text also rests on **estimate** — the general consensus is about **300 CE**, although some scholars have placed it as far back as 200 BCE. This much is certain, that it had been composed before the Pahlavi translation of the sixth century.

## The Five Tantras

1. **Mitrabheda** — Division among friends (the jackal Damanaka breaks the friendship of the lion Pingalaka and the bull Sanjivaka)
2. **Mitrasamprapti** — Gaining friends (the friendship of a crow, a mouse, a tortoise and a deer)
3. **Kakolukiyam** — The war of the crows and the owls
4. **Labdhapranasha** — Loss of what has been gained (the famous story of the monkey and the crocodile)
5. **Aparikshitakaraka** — The result of acting without thinking

## From Iran to Baghdad

The most unusual thing about the Panchatantra is its journey. In the sixth century, in the time of the Sasanian emperor of Iran **Khusrau I (Anushirvan)**, the physician **Burzoy** translated it into **Pahlavi** (Middle Persian). This Pahlavi text is no longer found, but a **Syriac** translation was made from it. Then, about **750 CE**, **Ibn al-Muqaffa** wrote it in Arabic under the name **'Kalila wa Dimna'**. It is counted among the early masterpieces of Arabic prose literature, and most later translations were made from it.

## The Panchatantra in Europe

From Arabic these stories reached Europe:
- In the 12th century **Rabbi Joel** made a Hebrew translation of it.
- From that, **John of Capua** made the Latin *Directorium Humanae Vitae*, which was printed around **1480**.
- In **1483** a German translation was printed.
- In **1570** Sir Thomas North published an English version based on the Italian edition.

Within India too the Panchatantra reached almost every major language, and later Sanskrit works such as the **Hitopadesha** adopted many of its stories.

## Significance

The Panchatantra shows that in ancient India education was given not only by sermon but by story. Its journey also shows that ideas and literature do not respect borders — a Sanskrit tale of conduct travelled through Persian, Arabic, Hebrew, Latin and English to reach children all over the world.
