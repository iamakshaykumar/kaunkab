## Background

After the Maurya empire weakened, new powers rose in different parts of India. In the Deccan, the plateau south of the Vindhya mountains — today's Maharashtra, Telangana and Andhra Pradesh — this role was played by the **Satavahanas**. The Puranas call them **"Andhra"** or "Andhrabhritya".

Scholars disagree about when the Satavahanas began. Some place it in the 3rd century BCE, while many historians consider the 1st century BCE more likely. Their decline is similarly placed at about the beginning of the 3rd century CE.

## Early rulers

- **Simuka** is regarded as the founder of the dynasty.
- **Satakarni I** was a powerful ruler. The inscription at **Naneghat** near Pune, associated with his queen **Nayanika**, shows that he performed Vedic sacrifices such as the Ashvamedha.
- On a gateway (torana) of the Great Stupa at Sanchi there is a donation inscription by the head of the artisans of "King Sri Satakarni".

The early capital of the Satavahanas was **Pratishthana** (today's Paithan, Maharashtra).

## Gautamiputra Satakarni: the greatest king of the dynasty

In the 1st century CE, the **Shaka satraps** of western India had seized large parts of the northern Deccan from the Satavahanas. The dynasty was rescued from this crisis by **Gautamiputra Satakarni** (c. 106–130 CE).

- He defeated **Nahapana**, the Shaka ruler of the Kshaharata line, and won back the lost territories.
- His mother **Gautami Balashri** had a long eulogy inscribed in a cave at Nashik. It describes him as the destroyer of the Shakas, Yavanas and Pahlavas.
- According to this inscription, his influence extended across the Deccan and into several regions of western and central India.

After him, **Vasishthiputra Pulumavi** and, later, **Yajna Sri Satakarni** were the major rulers. Some coins of Yajna Sri bear the image of a ship, showing the importance of sea trade. In later times the centre of the dynasty shifted east to the Andhra region (Amaravati–Dharanikota).

## Society, religion and trade

- The Satavahana kings themselves supported the Vedic tradition, but they also donated villages and land to **Buddhist monks**. Satavahana inscriptions are counted among the earliest epigraphic evidence of land grants to monks.
- Rock-cut Buddhist caves such as **Karla, Nashik and Bhaja** in the Western Ghats, and the **Amaravati** stupa in Andhra, are examples of the art of this period. The oldest caves at Ajanta also date to about this time.
- Trade with the Roman empire was flourishing. Spices, cloth and precious stones were exported from ports such as Bharuch (Bhrigukachchha) and Sopara.
- The language of inscriptions and coins was mainly **Prakrit**. The Prakrit poetry collection **"Gathasaptashati"** is traditionally attributed to the Satavahana king **Hala**.

## Decline and importance

By the beginning of the 3rd century CE, Satavahana power had weakened and their kingdom broke up into several smaller dynasties, such as the Abhiras in the west and the Ikshvakus in the east.

The importance of the Satavahanas lies in the fact that they brought the Deccan together in a large political structure for the first time, served as a cultural bridge between north and south India, and enriched trade, Buddhist art and Prakrit literature.
