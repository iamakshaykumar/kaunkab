## The Boy from Kilimanoor

**Raja Ravi Varma** was born on **29 April 1848** in the **Kilimanoor** palace in the princely state of Travancore (today's Kerala). His family was connected with the royal house of Travancore. As a child he was already drawing on the walls with charcoal. Seeing his talent, the ruler of Travancore, **Ayilyam Thirunal**, took him under his patronage and called him to the capital, **Thiruvananthapuram**.

There he learned by studying the paintings kept in the palace. He received early training in traditional painting in Madurai, learned watercolour from **Ramaswami Naidu**, and understood the technique of **oil painting** by watching the British painter **Theodore Jensen**, who had come to the palace, at work. In those days very few people in India knew oil colours, so Ravi Varma learned many things by experimenting on his own. In **1866** he married Bhageerathi Bayi of the Mavelikkara royal house.

## European Technique, Indian Subjects

Ravi Varma's greatest distinction was that he applied the **European realist style** — light and shade, correct anatomy, the illusion of depth — to **Indian subjects**. In **1873** one of his paintings won an award at an exhibition in **Vienna**, which increased his fame. In **1893** his paintings won several medals at the **World's Columbian Exposition** held in **Chicago** in America.

His paintings fall into two broad groups:

- **Mythological and literary paintings** — scenes such as **Shakuntala** writing a letter, **Damayanti talking to the swan**, Rama humbling the pride of the ocean, Arjuna and Subhadra, and Sairandhri;
- **Portraits and scenes of life** — portraits of kings, British officials and aristocratic families, and paintings such as **'There Comes Papa' (1893)** and **'Galaxy of Musicians' (1889)**, which show the dress of women from different regions of the country.

Ravi Varma travelled all over India to understand the dress, faces and surroundings of different regions. Maharaja **Sayajirao Gaekwad** of **Baroda** commissioned from him a large series of paintings on mythological themes, which can still be seen at Baroda's **Laxmi Vilas Palace**. In his paintings goddesses and heroines were often shown as Indian women wearing saris, and this image later settled in people's minds.

## The Ravi Varma Press and the Oleograph

Ravi Varma's original oil paintings hung in the palaces of kings and wealthy people. Ordinary people could not see them. To remove this distance, in **1894** he started a **lithographic press** at **Ghatkopar** in Bombay. Machines and technicians were brought from Germany. Colour prints were made by preparing a separate impression for each colour on stone plates, and they were varnished to give them a glossy look like an oil painting. Such prints are called **oleographs**.

In **1899** the press was moved towards Pune, to **Malavli** near **Lonavala**. Thousands of prints of gods, goddesses and mythological scenes — **Lakshmi, Saraswati, Shiva-Parvati, Rama's court, Krishna** and others — were printed at this press and sold cheaply. For the first time, a colourful, beautiful picture of the gods could reach even the poorest home.

But Ravi Varma was not as successful in business. The press fell into debt, and in **1901** he sold it to his German technician **Fritz Schleicher**, who continued to print under Ravi Varma's name.

## Criticism and Debate

Ravi Varma's work was debated in his own time and later. Some people objected to the human, realistic depiction of goddesses. In the early twentieth century, artists of the **Bengal School** and nationalist art critics argued that Ravi Varma's style was an imitation of European academic art and lacked the soul of Indian art; they spoke of returning to the tradition of Ajanta and of Mughal and Rajput miniatures. On the other hand, his supporters say that Ravi Varma connected Indian art with modern media and carried it to the masses.

## Legacy

Ravi Varma died on **2 October 1906**. Today he is among the most famous names in modern Indian art. Calendar art, religious posters, film posters and the costumes of mythological films were all deeply marked by his paintings. The way we see Lakshmi or Saraswati on calendars today owes a great deal to Ravi Varma and his press.

His original paintings are preserved today in the **Sree Chitra Art Gallery** in Thiruvananthapuram, at the Laxmi Vilas Palace in Baroda and in many museums. At auction his paintings are counted among the most expensive works of Indian art. The Kerala government gives the **'Raja Ravi Varma Award'** in his name in the field of art.
