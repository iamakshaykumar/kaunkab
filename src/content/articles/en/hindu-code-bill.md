## Background — Scattered Personal Laws

Under British rule, matters such as marriage, property and succession among Hindus were decided by differing interpretations of the Dharmashastras, local customs and court rulings. Different traditions such as **Mitakshara** and **Dayabhaga** prevailed in different regions. In most systems the property rights of daughters and widows were very limited, men could marry more than once, and there was no general provision for divorce.

To turn these laws into a written code, a committee was set up in **1941**, later chaired by **B. N. Rau**. This committee prepared a draft that included reforms such as inheritance for daughters, monogamy and divorce. After independence this task came before the new government.

## Ambedkar's Bill and the Opposition

Independent India's first Law Minister, **Dr B. R. Ambedkar**, developed this draft into a comprehensive **Hindu Code Bill**. It included provisions such as a share in property for daughters and widows, making **monogamy** compulsory, a right to **divorce** in certain circumstances, and recognition of marriages between different castes. Prime Minister **Jawaharlal Nehru** also supported it. According to its supporters, it was a big step towards giving women equality and bringing uniformity to Hindu society.

But in the Constituent Assembly (Legislative), which was then functioning as Parliament, it met fierce opposition. Among its opponents were many traditionalist members within the Congress, the **Hindu Mahasabha**, **Syama Prasad Mookerjee** and religious leaders such as **Swami Karpatri**. President **Rajendra Prasad** also raised objections to some aspects of the bill. Critics said that divorce was not in keeping with Hindu tradition, that it would weaken the **joint family**, and that an elected Parliament and public opinion should be consulted before such sweeping changes. Some critics also asked why reform was only for Hindus, and why there should not be a uniform civil code for all communities.

## 1951 — Ambedkar's Resignation

After long debate, only a few clauses of the bill could be passed in **September 1951**, and it was then not taken further. The government decided it would be brought back after the first general election. Disappointed, Ambedkar **resigned** from the cabinet on **27 September 1951**. In his resignation statement he named the shelving of this bill as one of the main reasons for his displeasure. In his view, to leave the inequality between class and class and between sex and sex untouched and to go on passing only economic legislation was to make a farce of the Constitution and to "build a palace on a dung heap".

## Reform Split into Four Laws

In the first general election of **1951–52** the Congress won a big victory. Nehru also regarded this as a mandate for the reform. Now, instead of one big bill, it was brought to Parliament split into **four separate laws**:

- **The Hindu Marriage Act, 1955** — in force from **18 May 1955**. It made **monogamy** compulsory (a second marriage while a spouse is alive became void), fixed a minimum age for marriage, and granted the right to **divorce** on grounds such as adultery, conversion and serious mental illness.
- **The Hindu Succession Act, 1956** — it made daughters, widows and mothers **co-sharers with sons** in the property of a father or husband, and treated a woman's property as her **absolute property**.
- **The Hindu Minority and Guardianship Act, 1956** — it set out rules on guardians of minor children.
- **The Hindu Adoptions and Maintenance Act, 1956** — it laid down rights of adoption and of maintenance for wives, children and dependants; women too gained the right to adopt.

In these laws the definition of "Hindu" was kept broad, including **Buddhists, Jains** and **Sikhs**.

## Later Changes and Debate

These laws kept changing over time. A **1976** amendment added a provision for **divorce by mutual consent** to the Hindu Marriage Act. In **2005** the Hindu Succession Act was amended to give daughters rights equal to sons in **ancestral property** (coparcenary) by birth — a major reform that had remained incomplete in 1956.

The debate over the Hindu Code Bill continues today. Supporters regard it as a historic step towards women's rights. Some critics say the initial laws were weaker than Ambedkar's original draft, while others question why only Hindu laws were changed while the personal laws of the Muslim, Christian and Parsi communities remained separate. This question connects with the debate on a **Uniform Civil Code**.

## Significance

The Hindu Code Bill set the direction of independent India's social legislation. It changed the rules on marriage, divorce and property in crores of families and gave women far more legal rights than before. Ambedkar's resignation, and Nehru's passing of the reform in parts after the election, also show how deep social reforms in a democracy pass through debate, opposition and compromise.
