## Background: The Foundation of the Nuclear Programme

The foundation of India's nuclear programme was laid by **Homi Bhabha**. The Atomic Energy Commission was formed in 1948, and in 1956 **Apsara**, Asia's first research reactor, went into operation at Trombay. The **CIRUS** reactor built with Canada's help and the plutonium reprocessing plant built in the 1960s later proved important.

The 1962 war with China and China's first nuclear test in 1964 increased India's security concerns. India did not sign the Nuclear Non-Proliferation Treaty (NPT) of 1968, saying that the treaty was discriminatory — it gives five countries the right to hold weapons and the rest not.

## 'Smiling Buddha' — 18 May 1974

On the instructions of Prime Minister **Indira Gandhi**, a small team led by the physicist **Raja Ramanna** at the Bhabha Atomic Research Centre (BARC) prepared the device in great secrecy; the chairman of the Atomic Energy Commission was **Homi Sethna**, and DRDO also cooperated. It was a plutonium-based 'implosion' design device weighing about 1,400 kg.

On **18 May 1974**, at about 8:05 in the morning, this explosion was carried out underground at the **Pokhran** test range in Rajasthan. Estimates of its yield differ — generally given as between 6 and 10 kilotons, and the matter has been disputed. The Government of India called it a **'peaceful nuclear explosion'** and expressed no intention of making weapons.

## The First Reactions

Canada protested strongly, because the plutonium had been made in the CIRUS reactor given by it, and it halted nuclear cooperation with India. Pakistan called it a threat to its security. The United States expressed protest, but for some time continued to send fuel for Tarapur. The most far-reaching consequence of this test was the formation in 1975 of the **Nuclear Suppliers Group**, because of whose rules India found it difficult to obtain nuclear technology and material for the next three decades.

## From 1974 to 1998

In the next two decades India did not conduct a nuclear test, but work on weapons and missile capability continued; under **A. P. J. Abdul Kalam** the Integrated Guided Missile Development Programme was run. In 1995, in the time of Prime Minister Narasimha Rao, there were reports of preparations for a test, but after surveillance by American satellites and pressure, that test did not take place. In 1996 India also refused to sign the Comprehensive Nuclear-Test-Ban Treaty (CTBT).

## 'Operation Shakti' — May 1998

In March 1998 a new government was formed under **Atal Bihari Vajpayee**. On **11 May 1998** three tests were carried out at Pokhran simultaneously and on **13 May** two more — five in all (Shakti-I to Shakti-V). According to the government, one of them was a thermonuclear (hydrogen) device; some scientists have expressed doubt about its actual yield. **A. P. J. Abdul Kalam** (then head of DRDO) and the chairman of the Atomic Energy Commission **R. Chidambaram** played key roles in the tests. The government declared India a nuclear-weapon state and announced a voluntary moratorium on further testing.

## International Reaction and Consequences

The United States, Japan and several other countries imposed economic sanctions on India, and the UN Security Council condemned the tests through **Resolution 1172**. Within a few days, on **28 and 30 May 1998**, Pakistan also carried out nuclear tests at Chagai, raising worldwide concern about an arms race in South Asia. The Government of India said the tests were linked to security needs, while critics drew attention to regional instability and economic cost.

With time most of the sanctions were lifted, and in 2008 the NSG granted India a special waiver for civil nuclear trade. In India, 11 May is observed as **National Technology Day**.
