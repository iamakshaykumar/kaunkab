## A Deccan Sunk in Debt

In the nineteenth century the peasants of the Deccan region of the **Bombay Presidency** — Poona, Ahmednagar, Satara and Sholapur — were mostly of the **Kunbi** community. Here the British had introduced the **ryotwari system**, under which each peasant paid **land revenue** directly to the government. Revenue rates were high, and whether the harvest was good or bad, the revenue had to be paid in cash and on time.

For revenue, seed, bullocks and household expenses, peasants borrowed from **sahukars** (moneylenders). Many of these moneylenders were **Marwari** and **Gujarati** traders, who were seen as "outsiders" in the villages. Loans given at high interest kept growing. The British courts treated written bonds as firm proof, and illiterate peasants often could not understand what paper they had put their thumbprint on. A law of 1859 limited the term of bonds to three years, but the moneylenders simply had new bonds written in place of the old ones, so it brought no relief.

## The Cotton Boom and Bust

When the **American Civil War** began in **1861**, Britain's textile mills stopped receiving cotton from America. British merchants turned to India. Cotton cultivation expanded rapidly in the Deccan, and moneylenders lent freely to the peasants.

As soon as the war ended in **1865**, American cotton returned to the market and the demand and price of Indian cotton fell suddenly. The peasants were left with debts but reduced income. In this same period a new revenue settlement took place in several talukas and the revenue was raised. Now the moneylenders cut back on lending and, through the courts, began to have the peasants' land, bullocks and houses attached. Many peasants became labourers on their own land.

## The Fire Kindled at Supa

The anger first appeared as a **social boycott**. In many villages the peasants decided that they would not work on the moneylenders' fields, would not sell them grain, and that village artisans such as barbers, potters and carpenters would not serve them either.

On **12 May 1875**, in **Supa**, a market village in Bhimthadi taluka of Poona district, a crowd of peasants stormed the houses and shops of the moneylenders. They seized account books and **debt bonds** and burned them in public. Some moneylenders were beaten, and many fled the village. Within a few days the movement spread to **more than 30 villages** in Poona and Ahmednagar districts, and its effect was also seen in Satara and Sholapur.

This revolt was mainly directed against the moneylenders, not directly against the British government. The police and army suppressed it, many peasants were arrested, and by September 1875 the situation had returned to normal, though the boycott continued in some villages for some time.

## The Riots Commission and the 1879 Law

The government set up the **Deccan Riots Commission** to investigate the causes. The Commission accepted that a major reason for the peasants' misery was debt and the passing of land into the hands of moneylenders, though it played down the role of high revenue — or so its critics argued. On its recommendations, changes were first made to the Code of Civil Procedure in 1877, and then in **1879** the **Deccan Agriculturists' Relief Act** (Dekkhan Agriculturists' Relief Act, Act XVII of 1879) was passed.

The main provisions of this law were:

- A ban on **arresting and imprisoning** a peasant for failure to repay a debt.
- A ban on the **attachment and sale of a peasant's land** in execution of a decree, unless it had been specifically mortgaged.
- Courts could examine the whole history of a loan transaction, so that arbitrary interest could be reduced.
- Officers to bring about settlements at the village level (**conciliators**) and local arrangements for small cases.

## Significance and Legacy

The Deccan Agriculturists' Relief Act was among the earliest laws in India to protect peasants from moneylenders. It first applied only to the four riot-affected districts and was later extended to other districts of the Bombay Presidency. Its results were mixed — the fear of arrest declined, but moneylenders reduced lending or adopted new methods. Later laws such as the Punjab Land Alienation Act (1900) were built on the same thinking.

The Deccan riots show that the burden of colonial land revenue policy and of fluctuations in the world market ultimately fell on the village peasant. Historians count them among the major **peasant movements** of the nineteenth century — together with the Indigo revolt and the Pabna movement — which prepared the ground for peasants' issues in later Indian nationalism.
