## The Tradition of the Delhi Durbar

Under British rule, a 'Delhi Durbar' was a grand imperial ceremony held to mark a British king or queen becoming ruler of India. Such durbars were held three times: in **1877** to proclaim Queen Victoria 'Empress of India', in **1903** for the coronation of Edward VII, and in **1911** for George V. The durbar of 1877 was also criticised because it was held at a time of terrible famine.

## 12 December 1911

**King George V** and **Queen Mary** came to India this year after their coronation in London. This was the first occasion on which a reigning British monarch was personally present at a Delhi Durbar. On **12 December 1911**, the durbar was held amid huge tents and pavilions on the ground in north Delhi that is today called **Coronation Park**.

Maharajas and nawabs of the princely states, British officers, army contingents and thousands of spectators took part. The rulers of the states presented themselves one by one before the king and paid 'homage'. A controversy also arose in the British newspapers over the manner in which the Maharaja of Baroda, Sayajirao Gaekwad, saluted, which some considered disrespectful.

## Two Big Announcements

At the end of the durbar the king himself made two unexpected announcements:

- **The Partition of Bengal annulled**: the partition made by Lord Curzon in 1905, against which the Swadeshi movement had been fought, was ended. The Bengali-speaking areas became one province again, while **Bihar and Orissa** were made a separate province and **Assam** a separate unit.
- **Delhi as capital**: it was announced that the capital of British India would be shifted **from Calcutta to Delhi**.

Nationalists regarded the annulment of the partition as a victory for their movement. On the other hand, many Muslim leaders of East Bengal, who had supported the partition, were disappointed by it.

## Why Was the Capital Changed?

The government argued in favour of Delhi on grounds of its central geographical position and administrative convenience. Historians also list some other reasons: Calcutta had become a centre of Swadeshi and revolutionary activity, and Delhi had for centuries been a symbol of power, so ruling from there would make the British appear the successors of the Mughals and the older empires. These are interpretations; in government documents mainly administrative reasons were given.

## The Building of New Delhi

On **15 December 1911**, George V and Mary laid the foundation stones of the new capital. Later the Delhi Town Planning Committee chose a site in the south instead of the north, and it was decided to build the Viceroy's House on **Raisina Hill**. Until construction was complete, work was carried on from a temporary secretariat in north Delhi.

The plan of the city and its main buildings were prepared by the architects **Edwin Lutyens** and **Herbert Baker**: the Viceroy's House (today **Rashtrapati Bhavan**), the North and South Blocks, the broad Rajpath (today **Kartavya Path**) and the All India War Memorial (**India Gate**). The work slowed because of the First World War. After about two decades, on **13 February 1931**, the Viceroy **Lord Irwin** formally inaugurated New Delhi.

## Significance

The durbar of 1911 was the greatest display of the splendour of the British Empire, but in it the government's yielding to nationalist pressure was also visible. The capital that the British built as a symbol of their power became, after 1947, the capital of independent India, and its buildings are today home to the institutions of the Indian republic.
