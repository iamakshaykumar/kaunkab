## A Small Post on the Frontier

At the end of the nineteenth century, there was repeated conflict between the British and Pashtun tribes on the **North-West Frontier** of British India (today's Khyber Pakhtunkhwa in Pakistan). In 1897 many tribes rose in large-scale rebellion, after which the British launched the **Tirah Campaign**.

On the **Samana Ridge** the British had two forts, **Fort Lockhart** and **Fort Gulistan**. There was no direct contact between the two, so a small post called **Saragarhi** was built in between, which passed on messages by **heliograph**. **21 soldiers** of the **36th Sikhs** were stationed here, commanded by **Havildar Ishar Singh**. Ishar Singh was born in **1858** in the village of **Jhorran** in Ludhiana district. Some accounts also mention a non-combatant helper named **Daad** at the post, who was killed along with these 21 soldiers.

## The Battle of 12 September 1897

On the morning of **12 September 1897**, thousands of fighters of the **Orakzai** and **Afridi** tribes surrounded Saragarhi. The sources differ considerably on their number; different accounts give it as **8,000 to 10,000** or even more. The post was visible from Fort Lockhart, but because of the tribal encirclement help could not be sent from there in time.

From inside the post the soldiers resisted continuously for several hours, according to accounts about **seven hours** (eight in some sources). The attackers tried to bring down part of the post's wall and set it on fire. Signaller **Gurmukh Singh** kept sending information about the situation to Fort Lockhart by heliograph throughout the battle. It is believed that after sending the last message he left the signalling instrument and entered the fighting himself.

In the end the post was captured and **all 21 soldiers** fell fighting. They did not surrender. The sources also do not agree on the attackers' casualties: some accounts give about 180–200 dead and several hundred wounded, while later it is mentioned that about 600 bodies were seen near the ruins, some of these deaths may also have been caused by the artillery bombardment carried out to retake the post.

## Military Significance of the Battle

The tribesmen's being held up at Saragarhi cost them time and strength. This helped the defence of **Fort Gulistan**, which survived this attack. Two days later the British army retook the Saragarhi area.

## Honours and Memorials

The British government posthumously honoured **all 21 soldiers** of Saragarhi with the **Indian Order of Merit**, which at that time was the highest gallantry award an Indian soldier could receive. Their families were also given land and cash rewards. The British government had **Saragarhi Gurdwaras** built in their memory at **Amritsar** and **Ferozepur**; the Ferozepur memorial was inaugurated in **February 1904**. At the site of Saragarhi too a memorial pillar was made from the bricks of the post.

## Memory Today

The Indian Army, especially the **Sikh Regiment**, celebrates **Saragarhi Day** every year on **12 September**. The 36th Sikhs is known today as the **4 Sikh** battalion. Books, TV serials and the 2019 Hindi film **"Kesari"** have also been made on this battle.

It is important to remember that this battle was part of the British Empire's frontier wars, in which Indian soldiers were fighting on the side of the British army. Historians therefore also view it in an imperial context. Even so, the Saragarhi soldiers' dedication to duty and standing firm to the last moment are regarded as an extraordinary example. Some claims spread about it, such as that this battle is taught in schools in France, are not based on solid evidence.
