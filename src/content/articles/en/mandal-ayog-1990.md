## Background: The Question of the Backward Classes

India's Constitution provided reservation for the Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes from the start, but under **Article 340** the task of identifying the "socially and educationally backward classes" was left to the government. The first commission in this direction was formed in 1953 under **Kaka Kalelkar**, but the central government took no concrete action on its 1955 report.

The **Janata Party** government that came to power in 1977 raised the question again. The government of Prime Minister **Morarji Desai** set up the Second Backward Classes Commission in **1979**. Its chairman was **Bindheshwari Prasad (B. P.) Mandal**, a former Chief Minister of Bihar and Member of Parliament, so it came to be popularly called the **Mandal Commission**. The commission had five members, including one Dalit member, **L. R. Naik**.

## The Commission's Report (1980)

The commission carried out surveys across the country and framed **11 indicators** on social, educational and economic grounds to measure backwardness. The main findings of the report, submitted in **December 1980**, were:

- **3,743 castes and communities** across the country were identified as Other Backward Classes (OBCs).
- According to the commission's estimate, the OBC population was about **52 per cent** of the total. This estimate was based mainly on the **1931 census**, since no caste-based census had been held after it.
- To keep total reservation below 50 per cent, in line with earlier Supreme Court judgments, the commission recommended **27 per cent** reservation for OBCs in central government jobs and public sector undertakings. Together with the 22.5 per cent for the Scheduled Castes and Tribes, total reservation would have been **49.5 per cent**.

The report was placed before Parliament in 1982, but it was not acted upon for almost a decade under the governments of Indira Gandhi and Rajiv Gandhi.

## 1990: The Announcement and Nationwide Turmoil

After the 1989 election, a **National Front** government was formed and **V. P. Singh** became Prime Minister. On **7 August 1990** he announced in Parliament that the government would implement 27 per cent reservation for OBCs in central government jobs, as recommended by the Mandal Commission. A government order (office memorandum) to this effect was issued on **13 August 1990**.

The announcement drew a sharp reaction. In many cities of north India, students in particular staged protests. Those opposing it argued that expanding caste-based reservation would neglect merit and divide society. On **19 September 1990**, **Rajiv Goswami**, a student of Deshbandhu College in Delhi, attempted self-immolation; several more young people made such attempts afterwards, and many of them died. On the other side, supporters argued that giving a large section of society, kept away from education and power for centuries, a share in the government system was a demand of **social justice**. OBC politics then became a major current in the politics of north India.

The order was challenged in the Supreme Court, which stayed it pending a final decision. In November 1990 V. P. Singh's government fell for other reasons. Later, in **September 1991**, the government of **P. V. Narasimha Rao** amended the order to give priority to the poorer sections among the OBCs and to add a separate **10 per cent** reservation for economically backward people from other classes.

## The Indra Sawhney Judgment (1992)

The validity of these orders was heard by a **nine-judge** Constitution Bench in **Indra Sawhney v. Union of India**. The main points of the judgment, delivered by a 6–3 majority on **16 November 1992**, were:

- **27 per cent reservation** for OBCs in central government jobs is **valid**, and caste can be one basis for identifying a backward class.
- The well-off section of the OBCs, the **'creamy layer'**, must be excluded from the benefit of reservation.
- In normal circumstances, total reservation should not exceed **50 per cent**.
- Reservation applies only at the time of appointment, **not in promotions**.
- The 10 per cent reservation given purely on economic grounds was held not to be in line with the Constitution and was struck down.
- A **permanent commission** should be set up to examine the lists of backward classes. Following this, the **National Commission for Backward Classes** was formed in 1993.

After the judgment, OBC reservation actually came into force in central government jobs in 1993.

## The Road Ahead

The Mandal judgment became the basis of almost every later dispute over reservation in India. On the question of reservation in promotions, Parliament later passed constitutional amendments for the Scheduled Castes and Tribes. In **2006** the central government passed a law extending 27 per cent OBC reservation to higher education institutions such as the IITs, IIMs and central universities, which the Supreme Court upheld on **10 April 2008** on the condition that the creamy layer be excluded. In 2019 a constitutional amendment also added a separate 10 per cent reservation for the Economically Weaker Sections (EWS), which the Supreme Court upheld by a majority in 2022.

## Legacy and Significance

The implementation of the Mandal Commission's recommendations is regarded as a major turning point in the politics and society of independent India. It increased the participation of the backward classes in government jobs and education and brought many new regional parties and leaders to the fore. Supporters see it as a step that deepened democracy, while critics have continued to voice concern about the growth of caste-based politics and the question of merit. The **50 per cent ceiling** and the concept of the **creamy layer** from the Indra Sawhney judgment remain at the centre of every debate on reservation today.
