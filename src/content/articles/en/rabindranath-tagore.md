## The House at Jorasanko

**Rabindranath Tagore** was born on **7 May 1861** at **Jorasanko** in Calcutta, in a well-known Bengali family. His father **Debendranath Tagore** was a leading figure of the Brahmo Samaj. The home had an atmosphere of literature, music and drama, which influenced Rabindranath from childhood. He never liked the rigid routine of school — this experience later became the foundation of his experiments in education.

He began writing poetry at a very young age and went on to write poems, stories, novels (*Gora*, *Ghare Baire*), plays, essays and about **two thousand songs**, which are called **Rabindra Sangeet**. He is also credited with starting the tradition of the modern short story in Bengali.

## The School at Santiniketan (1901)

Debendranath Tagore had built an ashram at **Santiniketan** near Bolpur, where a house of worship was built in **1863**. In **1901** Rabindranath started a small experimental school right here. Classes here were often held in the open under trees, and along with studies there was an emphasis on music, painting, drama and closeness to nature. Tagore wanted education to awaken curiosity and creativity rather than rote learning.

## Gitanjali and the Nobel Prize (1913)

The Bengali **Gitanjali** was published in **1910** and contained 157 poems. During a visit to England in **1912** Tagore himself translated some of his poems into English prose. In London he met the Irish poet **W. B. Yeats**, who wrote the introduction to these translations. In **November 1912** the **India Society** of London printed 750 copies of *Gitanjali: Song Offerings*. It contained **103** poems — 53 of them from the original Bengali Gitanjali and the rest from his other books. The image in this article — the sketch of Tagore by the painter **William Rothenstein** — was printed as the frontispiece of this very edition.

On **13 November 1913** the Swedish Academy announced that the Nobel Prize in Literature would be given to Tagore. He was the **first Indian and the first non-European** to receive this honour. The Academy praised his "profoundly sensitive, fresh and beautiful verse". At the ceremony on 10 December a British diplomat received the prize on his behalf.

Some critics said later that the music and depth of the Bengali poems did not fully come through in the English translation, and that the West to some extent saw Tagore in the image of an "Eastern mystic". Even so, the prize gave Indian literature recognition on the world stage. In **March 1913** Macmillan reprinted Gitanjali, and in the next two years it went through twenty reprints. Tagore himself went to Sweden in **May 1921** and gave his Nobel lecture there.

## Visva-Bharati and Sriniketan

Tagore's dream was that Santiniketan should become a meeting place of the knowledge of East and West. In **1918** the foundation of **Visva-Bharati** was laid, and around **1921** it formally began as a university. At nearby **Sriniketan** he began experiments in village reconstruction, farming, handicrafts and cooperation. In **1951** Visva-Bharati was given the status of a **central university** by an Act of Parliament.

## Nation, Freedom and Humanity

In **1915** the British government gave Tagore a **knighthood**, but in **1919** he renounced the title in protest against the **Jallianwala Bagh massacre**. He was a critic of British rule, but also opposed narrow nationalism and stressed humanism and internationalism. He had a deep relationship of respect with **Mahatma Gandhi**, yet on many issues — such as some methods of the Non-Cooperation Movement — the two held open debates.

In the last years of his life, after the age of about sixty, he also took up **painting** and made thousands of pictures. He died in Calcutta on **7 August 1941**. Even today "Gurudev" Tagore stands at the centre of the cultural identity of both India and Bangladesh.
