## Vatapi: The Capital of the Chalukyas

In the middle of the sixth century the **Chalukya** dynasty rose in northern Karnataka. **Pulakeshin I**, regarded as the founder of this dynasty, fortified **Vatapi** — today's **Badami** — and made it his capital around **544 CE** (one view says 543 CE). This is known from an inscription engraved on a rock there. Surrounded by tall rocks of red-brown sandstone and a lake in the middle, the place was very suitable from the point of view of defence.

In these same rocks, above the man-made lake called **Agastya Tirtha**, four main **cave temples** were cut in the time of the Chalukya kings. These temples are important because they show the early stage of temple art in South India — shortly after this, the freestanding temples built of joined stones were raised at nearby **Aihole** and **Pattadakal**.

## Four Caves, Three Traditions

Each cave generally has a pillared veranda, behind it a mandapa (assembly hall), and innermost a small sanctum. There is fine carving on the walls, pillars and ceilings.

- **Cave 1 (Shaiva):** It is dedicated to Shiva. Its most famous image is that of the **18-armed Nataraja**, about 1.5 metres high. There are also images of **Harihara**, half Shiva and half Vishnu, **Ardhanarishvara**, half Shiva and half Parvati, and **Mahishasuramardini** Durga.
- **Cave 2 (Vaishnava):** It is dedicated to Vishnu. In its veranda are large panels of **Trivikrama** (Vamana measuring the three worlds in three strides) and the **Varaha** avatar. In the mandapa there are also small scenes from the stories of Krishna.
- **Cave 3 (Vaishnava):** This is the largest and grandest cave. Several forms of Vishnu — Vishnu seated on Shesha, Varaha, Narasimha, Trivikrama and Harihara — are carved in huge panels. Its ceiling once had coloured **murals**, of which some portions survive.
- **Cave 4 (Jain):** This is the smallest and is dedicated to the Jain Tirthankaras. It has images of **Parshvanatha**, **Bahubali** and, in the sanctum, **Mahavira**. It is generally dated to the latter part of the seventh century or somewhat later.

## The Inscription of Cave 3: 578 CE

The firmest basis for dating the Badami caves is the inscription of **Cave 3**. Written in the old Kannada script, this inscription tells us that in **Shaka year 500** (about **578–579 CE**) the Chalukya prince **Mangalesha** dedicated this Vishnu temple. At that time his elder brother **Kirtivarman I** was king, and according to the inscription the merit of this pious act was dedicated to him. Later Mangalesha himself became king.

On the basis of this same inscription historians also estimate the dates of the other caves. Caves 1 and 2 are generally dated to the end of the sixth or the beginning of the seventh century. Scholars differ on whether the numbering of the caves (1 to 4) accurately reflects the order in which they were made.

## Features of the Art

The sculptures of Badami show the influence of both the Gupta tradition of North India and the local style of the South. The images are full-bodied and dynamic, and in one and the same cave both Shaiva and Vaishnava forms (such as Harihara) are found. This reflects the **religious tolerance** of that time: under the patronage of a single dynasty, temples of Shiva, Vishnu and the Jain Tirthankaras — all three — were built.

The soft **sandstone** was easy to carve, so the craftsmen could carve fine ornaments, the folds of garments and the expressions of faces in it. However, this same softness has also caused the sculptures to wear away under the weather.

## Later History

Chalukya power reached its peak in the time of **Pulakeshin II** (c. 610–642 CE). But in **642 CE** the Pallava king **Narasimhavarman I** attacked Vatapi, defeated Pulakeshin II and took the title 'Vatapikonda' (conqueror of Vatapi). Later the Chalukyas regathered their strength, but the tradition of temple building at Badami flourished onwards at Aihole and Pattadakal.

Today the Badami caves are protected by the **Archaeological Survey of India**, and under the name "Aihole-Badami-Pattadakal: The Evolution of Temple Architecture" the region has been on the Tentative List of **UNESCO** World Heritage since 2004.
