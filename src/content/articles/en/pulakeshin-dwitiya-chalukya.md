## Background: The Chalukyas of Badami

In the middle of the 6th century the **Chalukya** dynasty arose in the northern part of Karnataka. **Pulakeshin I** in about 543 CE built a fort on the hill of **Vatapi** (today's Badami, Bagalkot district) and made it his capital. Surrounded by rocks of red sandstone, this place was very strong from the point of view of defence.

His successors Kirtivarman I and **Mangalesha** expanded the kingdom. In the time of Mangalesha an inscription of 578 CE is engraved in a famous cave of Badami (Cave 3).

## The Struggle for the Throne

Kirtivarman's son **Pulakeshin II** had to fight a civil war with his uncle Mangalesha to gain the throne. He became king in about 610 CE. Several neighbouring powers had raised their heads by taking advantage of the internal strife, so the early years of his rule were spent in reorganising the kingdom.

## The Conquest of the Quarters

Pulakeshin II carried out successful campaigns in all four directions:
- In the **south** he subdued the Kadambas of Banavasi, the Gangas and the Alupas.
- In the **west** he defeated the Mauryas of the Konkan and took their sea-capital Puri.
- In the **north** the rulers of Lata (south Gujarat), Malava and the Gurjaras accepted his overlordship.
- In the **east** he conquered Vengi (Andhra) and made his brother **Kubja Vishnuvardhana** its ruler.

## The Clash with Harsha on the Narmada

At that time North India was dominated by **Harshavardhana**, the emperor of Kannauj. Harsha tried to advance southward, but Pulakeshin stopped him near the **Narmada river**. Scholars do not agree on the exact date of this battle; it is placed anywhere between about 618–619 CE and around 630 CE.

This victory is described in the Sanskrit inscription (634–635 CE) engraved in the Meguti Jain temple at **Aihole**, composed by Pulakeshin's court poet, the Jain **Ravikirti**. The inscription says in a poetic way that in the battle Harsha's "harsha" (joy) came to an end. After this victory the Narmada became the boundary between the two great powers of north and south.

## The Struggle with the Pallavas and the End

In the south the **Pallavas** of Kanchi were the Chalukyas' greatest rivals. Pulakeshin defeated the Pallava king **Mahendravarman I** and reached close to Kanchi. But Mahendravarman's son **Narasimhavarman I** struck back. In about 642 CE he captured Vatapi, and Pulakeshin II is believed to have died in this very conflict. Because of this victory Narasimhavarman came to be called "Vatapikonda" (conqueror of Vatapi). A few years later Pulakeshin's son Vikramaditya I re-established Chalukya power.

## Legacy

In the time of Pulakeshin II Chalukya power was at its peak. The Chinese traveller **Xuanzang** passed through his kingdom, and according to the Arab historian Tabari he exchanged envoys with the Sasanian king Khusrau II of Iran. The Chalukyas began a tradition of temple architecture at Badami, Aihole and Pattadakal in which a blend of North and South Indian styles can be seen. The monuments of Pattadakal are today a UNESCO World Heritage Site.
