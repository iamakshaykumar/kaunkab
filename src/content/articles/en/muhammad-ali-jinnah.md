## From Karachi to London

**Muhammad Ali Jinnah** is believed to have been born on **25 December 1876** in **Karachi**, into a Khoja merchant family. Karachi was then part of the Bombay Presidency. As a teenager he went to **London** to learn business, but there he chose to study law instead. After completing his studies at **Lincoln's Inn**, he was called to the bar in **1895**, at the age of only 19.

While in London he was influenced by liberal leaders such as **Dadabhai Naoroji**. Returning to India in 1896, he began practising law in **Bombay** and within a few years was counted among the city's most successful lawyers. He became known as a sharp, disciplined constitutional politician with a Western way of life.

## Congress Leader and 'Ambassador of Unity'

Jinnah entered politics at the Bombay session of the Congress in **1904**. He was close to **Gopal Krishna Gokhale** and was regarded as part of the moderate wing. When the **All-India Muslim League** was formed in Dhaka in **1906**, Jinnah opposed demands such as separate electorates. In 1910 he was elected to the Imperial Legislative Council.

In **1913** he also joined the Muslim League, but did not leave the Congress. His aim was to bring the two organisations together. In the **Lucknow Pact** of **1916**, the Congress and the League jointly demanded constitutional reforms and agreed on representation for Muslims. Because of his important role in this pact, **Sarojini Naidu** called Jinnah the **"ambassador of Hindu-Muslim unity"**. In 1918 he married **Rattanbai Petit**.

## Distance from the Congress

Around 1920 the character of Indian politics changed. Under **Mahatma Gandhi's** leadership the Congress chose the path of the **Non-Cooperation Movement** and mass satyagraha, and with the **Khilafat Movement** religious issues also entered politics. Jinnah supported constitutional methods and opposed this policy. After the Nagpur session in **1920**, he left the Congress.

The **Nehru Report** of **1928** proposed reserved seats for Muslims instead of separate electorates. Jinnah suggested amendments, which were not accepted. In **1929** he then put forward his famous **"Fourteen Points"**, which demanded provincial autonomy and safeguards for Muslim rights. Disappointed after the Round Table Conferences, he lived mostly in London from **1930 to 1934**, practising before the Privy Council.

## The Muslim League and the Demand for Pakistan

Returning to India in **1934**, Jinnah began reorganising the weakened Muslim League. In the **1937** provincial elections the League performed poorly, and the Congress formed governments in several provinces. Many historians believe that these elections, and the Congress's subsequent refusal to form joint governments with the League, decisively changed Jinnah's politics.

On **23 March 1940**, at the League's Lahore session, the **Lahore Resolution** was passed, demanding "independent states" for Muslim-majority regions. This later came to be known as the demand for **Pakistan**. Jinnah put forward the **"two-nation theory"** — that Hindus and Muslims are two separate nations. The Congress rejected this theory.

In the **1945–46** elections the League won most of the seats reserved for Muslims. After agreement on the **Cabinet Mission Plan** broke down, the League declared **16 August 1946** as **"Direct Action Day"**, after which terrible communal violence broke out in Calcutta. Finally, under **Lord Mountbatten's** plan, India was partitioned, and on **14 August 1947** Jinnah became Pakistan's first **Governor-General**.

## Differing Views of Historians

Historians differ deeply over Jinnah's role. In Pakistan he is honoured as **"Quaid-e-Azam"** (Great Leader) and the Father of the Nation. In India he is generally seen as the principal leader responsible for Partition.

Scholars such as historian **Ayesha Jalal** have argued that for Jinnah the demand for Pakistan was initially a "bargaining" tool to secure an equal share for Muslims within a loose federation. Other historians disagree with this interpretation and hold that after 1940 Jinnah was firmly set on the goal of a separate nation. His speech of **11 August 1947** to Pakistan's Constituent Assembly, in which he said that the state would have nothing to do with the religion of its citizens, is seen by some as proof of his secular outlook, while others read it differently in the context of his politics as a whole.

## Last Days and Legacy

At the time of independence Jinnah was suffering from a serious lung disease, which he had kept from the public. About thirteen months after Pakistan was created, he died in Karachi on **11 September 1948**. He was buried at the **Mazar-e-Quaid** in Karachi. His sister **Fatima Jinnah** remained his closest companion throughout his life.

Jinnah's story shows how one person who was once a champion of national unity went on to become the leader of a separate state. Understanding his politics is still considered essential for understanding the **Partition of India**, the communal politics of the subcontinent and India-Pakistan relations.
