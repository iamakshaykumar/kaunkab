## Background: Bengal after the Permanent Settlement

After the **Permanent Settlement** of **1793**, the zamindars' hold over land in Bengal became very strong. Most peasants of East Bengal were Muslims, while many of the new zamindars were Hindus, and European **indigo planters** also pressed hard on the peasants. Besides the fixed rent, zamindars collected all sorts of extra levies, called **'abwab'**. In this atmosphere of poverty and insecurity a religious reform movement was born, which gradually became a major platform for peasant resistance.

## Haji Shariatullah and the Beginning of the Movement

**Haji Shariatullah** was born in **1781** in the village of **Shamail** in the Madaripur area of Faridpur district, into a minor talukdar family. In **1799**, at about eighteen, he went to Mecca and studied religion there for nearly twenty years. He returned to Bengal in **1818** and began preaching; this year is taken as the start of the movement. At first people showed little interest, so he went back to Mecca for advice and, returning in **1820**, took up the work with fresh energy.

The movement took its name from its stress on **'faraiz'**, the **obligatory duties** of Islam: the declaration of faith, prayer, fasting, zakat and Hajj. His main teachings were:

- Firm belief in **tawhid** (the oneness of God) and self-purification through **tawbah** (repentance).
- Opposition to rites such as Fatiha, Urs and Milad, which he considered 'bid'ah' (innovations outside the Quran and Sunnah).
- Treating British-ruled India as **'dar-ul-harb'**: hence, in his view, Friday and Eid prayers were not obligatory without Muslim rule.
- The terms **'ustad-shagird'** in place of 'pir-murid' for teacher and disciple, and emphasis on equality and brotherhood among Muslims.

Traditional ulama such as Karamat Ali Jaunpuri openly opposed these ideas, especially the point about Friday and Eid prayers.

## From Religion to the Peasant Question

Shariatullah argued that the zamindars created by the Permanent Settlement had no right to demand a share of the peasants' crops beyond the government rent. He told his followers not to pay **abwab**. In particular, the levy taken from Muslim peasants for Hindu festivals was opposed. As a result zamindars, indigo planters and conservative Muslims all turned against him. The police detained him several times on charges of stirring up peasants in Faridpur, and in **1837** zamindars alleged that he wanted to set up a kingdom of his own. Even so, during his lifetime the movement spread to **Dhaka, Barisal and Comilla**. He died in **1840**.

## Dudu Miyan: Organisation and Struggle

Shariatullah's son **Muhsinuddin Ahmad**, famous as **Dudu Miyan**, was born in **1819** and became head of the movement in **1840**. In his time the movement clearly took on the character of a **peasant movement**. A slogan linked to him was **"Land belongs to God"** (al-ardu lillah), meaning that the zamindar had no right to impose arbitrary levies on land.

Dudu Miyan built a strong structure:

- A **village khalifa** in each settlement, a **superintendent khalifa** over a group of villages (a **gird**), and advisory khalifas at the top.
- Headquarters at **Bahadurpur**; disputes were settled in their own **panchayats** rather than in government courts.
- Bands of their own **lathiyals** (stick-fighters) to face the zamindars' lathiyals.

He clashed many times with zamindars and indigo planters. In **December 1846** his followers attacked an indigo factory at Auliapur managed by an Englishman named **Dunlop**. His opponents brought many cases against him, but because of his popularity witnesses were hard to find and he was often acquitted. After the **Revolt of 1857** the government arrested him as a precaution, held him in **Alipore Jail** near Calcutta, and released him in **1861**. He died in Dhaka in **1862**.

## The Later Phase

After Dudu Miyan, a board of guardians was set up for his minor sons. From **1864**, **Abdul Ghafur 'Naya Miyan'** took over the leadership, but by then the movement's militant side had weakened and it was mostly confined to religious reform. In the twentieth century the Faraizi leader **Badshah Miyan** joined the Khilafat and Non-Cooperation movements. The Faraizi tradition still exists in Bangladesh as a religious current.

## Significance and Legacy

The Faraizi movement was among the earliest movements in Bengal in which religious reform and the economic discontent of peasants came together. Historians view it in both ways: on the one hand it organised peasants against zamindari exploitation, and on the other, because of its religious identity, the zamindar-peasant conflict in places also took the form of Hindu-Muslim tension. **Dudu Miyan**'s village-level organisation, with its khalifas, panchayats and lathiyals, became an example for later peasant movements. Bangladesh's **Shariatpur** district still recalls Haji Shariatullah today.
