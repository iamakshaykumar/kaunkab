## A Tragedy That Began with Drought

In **1876** the monsoon almost completely failed in south India. Crops such as jowar, bajra and ragi withered on the **Deccan Plateau**. Scientists today believe that a very strong **El Niño** was then under way in the Pacific Ocean, which caused drought not only in India but also in countries such as China and Brazil.

The famine hit the **Madras Presidency** hardest, which is why it is also called the **Madras Famine**. The **Bombay Presidency** and the princely states of **Mysore** and **Hyderabad** were also badly affected. In **1877** the rains were poor again and the famine spread to parts of the **Central Provinces**, the **North-Western Provinces** and **Punjab**. The crisis lasted about two years.

## A Terrible Death Toll

Deaths in the famine were not caused by hunger alone. **Cholera**, dysentery and the **malaria** that spread in **1878** also took a heavy toll on weakened bodies. According to various estimates, between **5.6 and 9.6 million** people died in this famine; one careful modern estimate is about **8.2 million**. The population of Mysore state alone fell by about **874,000** compared with the 1871 census.

Whole villages were emptied. Hungry people migrated towards towns and relief camps in search of work and grain, and cattle too died in large numbers.

## Lytton's Policy and the 'Temple Wage'

The Viceroy of India at the time was **Lord Lytton**. His government followed the principle of the **free market** (laissez-faire) and avoided interfering in the grain trade. Despite the famine, **record exports of wheat** from India to England continued. Critics argued that the government valued saving money above human lives. In this same period a lavish **imperial durbar** was held in Delhi on **1 January 1877**, which also drew criticism.

The government entrusted famine relief to **Sir Richard Temple**. To cut costs, he reduced the ration of labourers on relief works to about **one pound (roughly 450 grams) of grain and one anna**, which came to be called the **"Temple wage"**. This amount was far too little for a person doing hard physical labour. **W. R. Cornish**, the Sanitary Commissioner of Madras, opposed it and said that people could not survive on so little food. Later the ration in Madras was raised somewhat. In Bombay, relief workers even went on strike in protest.

The journalist **William Digby** wrote detailed reports on the Madras famine and drew public attention in Britain to the tragedy, and private relief funds were also set up in Britain and India.

## The Famine Commission and the Famine Codes

After the famine, the government set up the **Indian Famine Commission** under the chairmanship of **Sir Richard Strachey**, whose report appeared in **1880**. The Commission's main recommendations were:

- During famine, those able to work should be given wages through **relief works** (roads, tanks, canals and so on), and the helpless should receive free food.
- In times of drought, **land revenue should be remitted or suspended**, and loans should be given for seed and bullocks.
- **Railways and irrigation** should be expanded to protect against famine.
- Each province should have a **Famine Code**, laying down in advance when and how relief would begin.

On the basis of these recommendations, the **Provisional Famine Code** was drawn up in **1883**. It defined stages such as "near-scarcity", "scarcity" and "famine" and set out the duties of the administration at each stage. The government also arranged to set aside about **1.5 crore rupees** every year as a "Famine Insurance Grant" for famine relief and protective works.

## Significance and Legacy

The famine of 1876–78 was one of the greatest human tragedies in India's history. The Famine Codes that followed remained the basis of famine management in India for many decades. Even so, severe famines struck again in 1896–97 and 1899–1900, and the **Bengal Famine** of 1943 shows how deadly policy failures could be.

This famine also had a deep effect on the thinking of Indian nationalists. Writers such as **Dadabhai Naoroji** and later **R. C. Dutt** linked India's poverty and its repeated famines to British economic policies and the **"drain of wealth"**. Britons such as **William Wedderburn** and **A. O. Hume** were also influenced by it, and they later played a role in founding the Indian National Congress.
