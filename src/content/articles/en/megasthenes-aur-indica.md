## The Ambassador Megasthenes

After the treaty between **Chandragupta Maurya** and **Seleucus I** (about 305-303 BCE), envoys began to travel between the two kingdoms. Seleucus made a Greek named **Megasthenes** his ambassador and sent him to the Mauryan capital **Pataliputra**. Earlier he had lived at the court of Sibyrtius, the satrap of Arachosia (today's Kandahar region).

The exact date of his journey is not known; most scholars place it after the treaty, some before it. Arrian writes that Megasthenes himself said he met Sandrocottus (Chandragupta), "the greatest king of the Indians". Arrian also adds that Megasthenes had not seen a very large part of India.

## A Lost Book, Surviving Fragments

Based on what he saw and heard in India, Megasthenes wrote a work called **Indica**. This original work has not survived. We know it only from quotations in later writers:
- **Diodorus** (1st century BCE)
- **Strabo** (1st century BCE-1st century CE), whose *Geography* has its fifteenth book on India
- **Pliny** (1st century CE)
- **Arrian** (2nd century CE), whose own *Indica* depends heavily on Megasthenes

In 1846 **Schwanbeck** compiled these fragments in one place, and in 1877 **J. W. McCrindle** published an English translation of them. But note that each writer chose quotations according to his own preference and sometimes put them in his own words, so what exactly Megasthenes said is not always certain.

## Description of Pataliputra

According to Arrian, Megasthenes described **Palibothra** (Pataliputra) as the largest city of India, situated at the confluence of the Ganga and the **Erannoboas** river (often identified with the Son).
- The city was **80 stadia** long and **15 stadia** wide — that is, about 15 kilometres long and nearly 2.75 kilometres wide.
- All around it was a deep and wide moat.
- The wooden wall had **570 towers** and **64 gates**.

Excavations at places such as Bulandibagh in Patna have found remains of a thick wooden palisade, which are thought to match this description. Strabo's quotations also mention six committees of five members each for the administration of the city.

## The "Seven Classes" of Indian Society

The best-known part of Megasthenes's account is its division of Indian society into **seven classes**:
1. **Philosophers** — few in number but most respected; they performed sacrifices and made predictions.
2. **Farmers** — the largest class; they were not disturbed even in times of war.
3. **Herdsmen and hunters** — who lived away from villages and towns.
4. **Artisans and traders**
5. **Soldiers** — second in number after the farmers; they received a salary from the state.
6. **Overseers** — who reported news of the country and the cities to the king.
7. **Counsellors and ministers** — a small but influential class.

According to him, it was forbidden to marry outside one's class or to change one's occupation, except that anyone could become a philosopher. These "seven classes" do not match the four varnas of Indian texts — it seems that Megasthenes mixed varna, jati and occupation and made a classification of his own understanding.

## How Far Can We Trust Him?

Many of Megasthenes's statements are disputed. He wrote that in India there is **no slavery** and that **famine never occurs** — yet Indian texts such as the Arthashastra mention slaves. He also wrote hearsay stories of gold-digging ants and strange races of men. For this reason Strabo called him unreliable. On the other hand, modern historians believe that many of his statements about geography, city life and governance are valuable. Some scholars also say that he deliberately painted an ideal picture, to show India as a powerful friend of Seleucus.

## Significance

Despite its shortcomings, *Indica* is considered the oldest detailed account of India by a foreigner of which parts survive. For centuries the Greek-Roman world knew India mainly through this work. Read together with Kautilya's Arthashastra and Ashoka's inscriptions, it makes the picture of the Mauryan period clearer.
