## Thanjavur: Fertile Ground for Music

In the eighteenth century the Maratha court of **Thanjavur** (Tanjore) was a major centre of art and music in south India. The temple towns of the fertile Kaveri delta, such as **Tiruvarur**, **Tiruvaiyaru** and Kumbakonam, had a deep tradition of music, dance and devotion. In this atmosphere were born three musicians who changed the direction of **Carnatic music**.

All three were born in **Tiruvarur**: **Syama Sastri** (1762), **Tyagaraja** (1767) and **Muthuswami Dikshitar** (1775; 1776 in some sources). Before them, saints like **Purandara Dasa** had laid the foundation of the teaching method of Carnatic music; the Trinity took the composition form called the **kriti** to its peak. Their compositions bring together subtlety of raga, variety of tala and deep devotion.

## Syama Sastri: Master of Tala

**Syama Sastri** (born **26 April 1762**, died **1827**) was named Venkata Subrahmanya as a child. His family were priests of the temple of the goddess **Bangaru Kamakshi** in Thanjavur, and most of his compositions are in praise of the goddess **Kamakshi**. He used the mudra **"Syama Krishna"** in his compositions.

His compositions are fewer in number (about **300** are counted) but are famous for their use of difficult **talas** and rhythmic craft. He made the **swarajati** an independent form for singing and instrumental music as well as dance; his three swarajatis in the ragas Bhairavi, Yadukulakambhoji and Todi are called the **"Ratnatrayam"** (three jewels). He wrote mainly in a Sanskrit-heavy **Telugu**. According to a well-known story, he defeated the visiting singer **Kesavayya** in a musical contest at the Thanjavur court.

## Tyagaraja: Singer of Devotion to Rama

**Tyagaraja** (**4 May 1767** – **6 January 1847**) was born into a Telugu-speaking Brahmin family and spent most of his life at **Tiruvaiyaru** on the bank of the Kaveri. He learnt music from **Sonti Venkataramanayya**, the chief musician of the Thanjavur court. He was a devoted follower of Lord **Rama**.

More than **700** of his compositions are available today, mostly in **Telugu**. The most famous of them are the five **"Pancharatna kritis"**: Jagadanandakaraka (Nata), Duduku Gala (Gaula), Sadhinchane (Arabhi), Kanakana Ruchira (Varali) and Endaro Mahanubhavulu (Sri raga), all in **Adi tala**. He also composed musical dramas such as **"Prahlada Bhakti Vijayam"** and **"Nauka Charitam"**.

It is said that Raja **Serfoji II** of Thanjavur invited him to the court, but Tyagaraja chose the path of devotion over wealth and royal honour. His famous kriti "Nidhi Chala Sukhama" (Does wealth give greater happiness?) is linked with this feeling.

## Muthuswami Dikshitar: Poet of Sanskrit and Temples

**Muthuswami Dikshitar** (**1775** – **21 October 1835**) was the son of the noted musician **Ramaswami Dikshitar**. In his youth he went to **Varanasi** with the ascetic **Chidambaranatha Yogi** and stayed there for several years studying music, philosophy and yoga; here he also became familiar with **Hindustani music**. According to tradition, his career as a composer began with the blessing of Lord Murugan at **Tiruttani**, and from this came his mudra **"Guruguha"**.

His roughly **500** compositions are mainly in **Sanskrit** and praise the deities of many temples of south India. The **Kamalamba Navavarana** kritis and the **Navagraha kritis** on the nine planets are his famous series. He was a skilled **veena** player, and the gamaka style of the veena shows in his compositions. He also created **"nottuswara"** pieces by setting Sanskrit words to about forty Western tunes. He died on the day of Deepavali at **Ettayapuram**, where his memorial stands today.

## Legacy

The Trinity created the core repertoire of the Carnatic concert (kutcheri). Even today the compositions of these three are sung prominently at any Carnatic music concert. Their styles differed (Tyagaraja's emotion and spontaneity, Dikshitar's gravity and purity of raga, and Syama Sastri's mastery of tala) but devotion was central to all three.

Every year in January-February, on Tyagaraja's death anniversary, the **Tyagaraja Aradhana** is held on the bank of the Kaveri at **Tiruvaiyaru**, where hundreds of musicians sing the Pancharatna kritis together. India's postal department has issued stamps on all three. Interestingly, the three were contemporaries and lived in the same region, yet very little authentic information exists about meetings between them; many stories about them come from the disciple tradition and folk memory. Printing was not common at the time, so for a long time their compositions passed from teacher to student only orally, and were compiled and published later in the nineteenth and twentieth centuries. The tradition of these composers' disciples carried their compositions forward from generation to generation, and that tradition is the backbone of Carnatic music today.
