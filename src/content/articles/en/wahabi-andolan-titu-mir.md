## The 'Wahabi' Label and Syed Ahmad Barelvi

**Syed Ahmad Barelvi** was born on **29 November 1786** at **Rae Bareli** (Awadh). In Delhi he studied under **Shah Abdul Aziz**, son of **Shah Waliullah**. For a time he also served as a horseman in Amir Khan's army. From about **1818** he led a reform movement called the **'Tariqa-i-Muhammadiya'**, which aimed to 'purify' Islam of customs he regarded as shirk or bid'ah, such as excessive veneration of shrines.

British officials called this movement **'Wahabi'**, because it seemed to them similar to the Wahhabi movement of Arabia. But Syed Ahmad and his followers saw themselves as linked to the Hanafi Sunni and Sufi tradition and did not accept the name. Later the word became a British label for any Muslim movement they considered a threat to the empire.

## Hajj, Preaching and the Frontier Struggle

Syed Ahmad left for Hajj in **1821** and returned in **1823**. On the way and after his return he gained thousands of followers in many places, including Calcutta; he deeply influenced **Titu Mir** of Bengal. His chief companion was **Shah Ismail Dehlvi**.

From **1826**, with the Peshawar Valley as his base, he waged jihad against the **Sikh Empire of Punjab**. He won success at Akora Khattak in December 1826, and in 1827 his followers declared him imam. But rivalry among local Pathan chiefs and pressure from Maharaja **Ranjit Singh**'s army weakened the movement. On **6 May 1831**, fighting a Sikh army led by Sher Singh in the battle of **Balakot**, both Syed Ahmad and Shah Ismail were killed.

The movement did not end with his death. The brothers **Wilayat Ali** and **Inayat Ali** of **Patna** reorganised it. Patna became the centre for raising money and fighters, while bases such as **Sitana** survived on the frontier. Now the British were also a target. In the **Ambela campaign** of **1863** the British sent a large army, and after the **'Wahabi trials'** held at Ambala, Patna, Rajmahal and Malda from **1864 to about 1871**, the movement broke up.

## Titu Mir: The Movement in Bengal

**Titu Mir**'s real name was **Syed Mir Nisar Ali**. He was born on **27 January 1782** in a village in the **24 Parganas** region (now West Bengal); his birthplace is given as Haidarpur or Chandpur, and sources do not agree on this. He was a hafiz of the Quran and knew Bengali, Arabic and Persian. As a young man he worked as a zamindar's bodyguard and was also jailed over disputes about heavy taxes on peasants. In **1822** he went on Hajj, where he was influenced by Syed Ahmad Barelvi.

On his return he preached religious reform among poor Muslim peasants and weavers around Barasat. Gradually his movement became a peasant struggle against the exploitation of **zamindars and indigo planters**.

## A Tax on Beards and the Clash

In **June 1830**, **Krishnadeb Rai**, the zamindar of Punra, imposed a special tax on Titu Mir's followers who wore beards. On Titu Mir's advice the peasants refused to pay. The quarrel grew, a mosque was burnt, and violence spread. The zamindars formed a front with the indigo planters, and the conflict began to take an armed form against the zamindar-Company alliance.

On **15 November 1830** the Joint Magistrate of Barasat, **Alexander**, came against him with about 120 policemen, but some 500 of Titu Mir's followers defeated them; several people, including the officer in charge of Baduria police station, were killed in the clash. By **1831** Titu Mir had proclaimed his own rule in the area and made his nephew **Ghulam Masum** commander of his forces.

## The Bamboo Fort of Narkelberia

In the later part of **1831**, Titu Mir had a fort of bamboo and mud (**'basher kella'**) built at the village of **Narkelberia** near **Barasat**. It became the centre of his movement. The Company government saw it as an open challenge and sent regular troops. A force of about 300 soldiers with two cannon, led by Major **Scott**, surrounded the fort on the evening of **18 November 1831**. The next morning, **19 November 1831**, after about three hours of cannon fire, the bamboo fort collapsed. Titu Mir was killed along with about 50 of his companions.

Afterwards about **800** people were arrested and tried at the court in Alipore. **Ghulam Masum** was hanged near the ruins of the fort, and about 140 people were imprisoned.

## Significance and Legacy

Syed Ahmad's movement began essentially as religious reform and jihad against the Sikh state; later it also turned against the British. Historians are divided in judging it: some see it as resistance to colonial rule, while others point to its rigid religious outlook and narrow social base. In Bengal, **Titu Mir**'s struggle was deeply tied not only to religious inspiration but also to the economic exploitation of peasants. In Bangladesh he is regarded as a folk hero: Government Titumir College in Dhaka, the naval base **BNS Titumir** and a 1992 postage stamp honour his memory, and a Kolkata Metro station is also named after him.
