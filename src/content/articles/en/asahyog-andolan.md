## Background: From the Rowlatt Act to Khilafat

In the First World War (1914–1918) Indians had supported Britain in the hope of receiving greater self-government after the war. Instead, in 1919 came the **Rowlatt Act**, which permitted arrest without trial. The massacre at **Jallianwala Bagh** in Amritsar on 13 April 1919 shook the country.

At the same time there was deep discontent among Muslims against Britain, because the Ottoman Sultan of Turkey, who had lost the war and was regarded as the **Caliph**, was being treated harshly. The **Khilafat Movement** began under the leadership of the brothers **Mohammad Ali** and **Shaukat Ali**. Gandhiji supported it, seeing it as an opportunity for Hindu-Muslim unity.

## Beginning of the Movement

The Non-Cooperation Movement formally began on **1 August 1920**. At a special session in Calcutta in September 1920 the Congress accepted the resolution on non-cooperation, and it received final approval at the **Nagpur session** of December 1920. Gandhiji gave the call of 'Swaraj in one year'.

The programme of the movement had two aspects:

- **Boycott:** Giving up government titles and posts, and boycotting government schools and colleges, courts, legislative councils and foreign cloth.
- **Constructive work:** Promoting khadi and the charkha, setting up national schools and colleges, settling disputes through panchayats, opposing untouchability and promoting Hindu-Muslim unity.

## Widening Mass Participation

The movement created unprecedented public enthusiasm:

- Thousands of students left government educational institutions. National institutions such as Kashi Vidyapith, Gujarat Vidyapith, Jamia Millia Islamia and the National College at Lahore came into being.
- Famous lawyers such as **Chittaranjan Das** and **Motilal Nehru** gave up their practice.
- Bonfires of foreign cloth were lit in many places and pickets were held at liquor shops.
- In November 1921, on the arrival of the **Prince of Wales** in India, there were strikes and boycotts across the country.
- In different regions, peasants, tribals and workers also linked their local issues to this movement.

The government made arrests on a large scale; by the end of 1921 thousands of people were in jail.

## Chauri Chaura and the Withdrawal of the Movement

On **4 February 1922** at **Chauri Chaura** in Gorakhpur district of the United Provinces (today's Uttar Pradesh), a clash took place between a procession and the police. After the police fired, an enraged crowd set fire to the police station, in which 22 policemen were killed.

Gandhiji was deeply hurt by this violence. He believed that the country was not yet fully ready for a completely non-violent struggle. On **12 February 1922** the Congress Working Committee at Bardoli decided to suspend the movement. Many leaders and young people found this decision sudden and disappointing. In March 1922 Gandhiji was arrested and sentenced to six years in prison.

In the Chauri Chaura case the court later sentenced a large number of people to death; after appeals, 19 people were hanged.

## Historical Significance

The Non-Cooperation Movement could not achieve its immediate goal — swaraj in one year — yet its significance is very great. It took the national movement out of the educated classes of the cities and carried it to villages, peasants and ordinary people. The Congress became a mass organisation, and khadi and the charkha became symbols of national self-respect. The movement reduced people's fear of British power and prepared the ground for the later Civil Disobedience Movement.
