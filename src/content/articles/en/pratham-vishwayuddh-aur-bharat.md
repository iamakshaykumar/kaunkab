## India in the War

On **4 August 1914** Britain declared war on Germany. Because India was a colony, no opinion was taken from it — it was automatically drawn into the war. Even so, in the beginning most Indian leaders, in the hope that loyalty would be rewarded with self-government after the war, appealed for support to Britain. The princes of the princely states also gave soldiers and money.

In 1914 the Indian army was about 150,000 strong; by the end of the war recruitment had risen to about 1.4 million. Different sources give different figures for the total contribution — it is generally believed that **more than a million** Indian soldiers and labourers were sent to overseas fronts. Most recruitment was from areas such as Punjab, the North-West Frontier Province, Garhwal, Nepal and Rajputana.

## The Western Front: France and Belgium

In the autumn of 1914 the Indian Corps reached France, when the British army needed immediate help. In Belgium **Khudadad Khan** showed indomitable courage at a machine-gun position near Hollebeke in **October 1914** and became the first Indian to receive the **Victoria Cross**. **Darwan Singh Negi** and **Gabbar Singh Negi** of the Garhwal Rifles also received this honour.

Indian soldiers fought in cold, muddy trenches, often with inadequate clothing and weapons, battles such as Neuve Chapelle and Ypres in **March 1915**. By the end of 1915 the Indian infantry units were withdrawn from France and sent to other fronts, while the cavalry units stayed there until later.

## Gallipoli, Mesopotamia and Other Fronts

- **Gallipoli (1915):** In the unsuccessful campaign to seize the Dardanelles in Turkey, Sikh, Gurkha and Punjabi units fought alongside Australian and New Zealand soldiers.
- **Mesopotamia (present-day Iraq):** The largest number of Indian soldiers fought here. After a long siege at **Kut al-Amara**, the British-Indian army had to surrender in **April 1916** — this is counted among the great defeats of this war. In **March 1917** the British army, with Indian units, captured **Baghdad**.
- **Egypt and Palestine:** Indian soldiers defended the Suez Canal. In September 1918 cavalry from Jodhpur and Mysore took part in the capture of **Haifa**.
- **East Africa:** In November 1914 the attack by Indian units on **Tanga** failed.

In the war more than **74,000** Indian soldiers were killed and many more were wounded.

## The Effect inside India

The economic burden of the war was heavy. Military expenses, war loans, increased taxes and the rising prices of essential goods broke the backs of ordinary people. Complaints of forced recruitment became common, especially in Punjab.

There was also more stirring in politics. In 1915 the planned revolt of the **Ghadar Party** was foiled. In 1916 **Tilak** and **Annie Besant** began the **Home Rule movement**, and in the same year the Congress and the Muslim League came together in the **Lucknow Pact**. In August 1917 the Secretary of State Montagu announced the goal of gradual responsible government in India. **Gandhi** appealed for army recruitment in 1918, for which he was also criticised.

## Memory and Legacy

In place of the self-government that Indians had hoped for after the war, events such as the **Rowlatt Act** and **Jallianwala Bagh** came in **1919**. This disappointment prepared the ground for the Non-Cooperation Movement under Gandhi.

In memory of the fallen soldiers the **All India War Memorial** was built in New Delhi, which is today called **India Gate**. The foundation of this 42-metre-high monument, designed by Edwin Lutyens, was laid by the Duke of Connaught on **10 February 1921**, and it was inaugurated by Viceroy Lord Irwin on **12 February 1931**. On it are inscribed the names of more than 13,000 soldiers killed in the First World War and the Third Anglo-Afghan War (1919). The Neuve Chapelle Memorial in France also recalls those Indian soldiers who have no known grave.
