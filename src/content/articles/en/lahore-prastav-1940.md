## Background: 1937 to 1940

In the provincial elections of **1937** the Congress formed governments in most provinces, while the Muslim League performed poorly. The League alleged that Muslims were neglected under the Congress governments. When the Congress ministries resigned in **October–November 1939** over the question of the Second World War, the League celebrated a "Day of Deliverance" on **22 December 1939**.

In this same period **Muhammad Ali Jinnah** began to argue forcefully that Hindus and Muslims in India were two separate "nations". His article published in the British magazine *Time and Tide* on **9 March 1940** made the same point. This idea later came to be called the **"two-nation theory"**. Because of the war, the British government also needed the cooperation of both of India's major parties, which had increased the League's political importance.

## The Lahore Session

The annual session of the All-India Muslim League was held from **22 to 24 March 1940** in Lahore's **Minto Park**, which is today called **Greater Iqbal Park**. In his presidential address on **22 March**, Jinnah said that Hindus and Muslims belonged to different religious philosophies, social customs and literatures, and so binding them together in a single state as "one nation" would lead to discontent.

The draft of the resolution was prepared by a sub-committee whose members included **Sikandar Hayat Khan**, the Premier of Punjab. In the open session it was moved by **A. K. Fazlul Huq**, the Premier of Bengal. Leaders such as **Chaudhry Khaliquzzaman** of the United Provinces, **Zafar Ali Khan** of Punjab, **Sardar Aurangzeb Khan** of the Frontier Province and **Sir Abdullah Haroon** of Sindh supported it. In Pakistan **23 March** is officially regarded as the day it was passed, while some historians write that it was finally adopted on **24 March**.

## What the Resolution Said

The core of the resolution was that geographically contiguous areas should be demarcated into units so that the regions in which Muslims are numerically in a majority — such as the **north-western and eastern zones** of India — should be grouped to constitute **"Independent States"**, in which the constituent units would be autonomous and sovereign.

The resolution also said that adequate provisions would be made in the constitution to protect the religious, cultural, economic and political rights of the minorities living in these units and regions; similarly, safeguards were demanded for Muslims in the rest of India.

It is worth noting that the resolution did **not contain the word "Pakistan"**, and the word "States" was in the plural. The name had been suggested in **1933** by **Choudhary Rahmat Ali**. As soon as the resolution was passed, Hindu newspapers and leaders began calling it the **"Pakistan Resolution"**, and later the League too adopted this name.

## Reactions

Congress leaders strongly opposed the demand and said that dividing the country on the basis of religion went against India's unity. Not all Muslims agreed with it either. In **April 1940** the **Azad Muslim Conference** was held in Delhi, where several Muslim organisations supported a free and united India. According to some historians, the League also put pressure on its nationalist Muslim opponents.

Debate over the language of the resolution continued later as well. Whether **"Independent States"** meant one country or several was interpreted in different ways. People such as the Bengal leader **Abul Hashim** regarded it as a demand for two separate countries in the east and the west. In **April 1946** a convention of Muslim League legislators in Delhi passed a resolution demanding **"a sovereign independent state"**, which many historians regard as an amendment of the Lahore Resolution; while some say this convention had no authority to change the original resolution.

## Significance and Legacy

The Lahore Resolution was a decisive turning point in Muslim League politics. Before it, the League had mainly demanded safeguards and greater representation; now its demand became a separate political entity. Over the next seven years — at the **Cripps Mission (1942)**, the **Simla Conference (1945)** and the **Cabinet Mission (1946)** — this demand remained at the centre, and finally in **1947** India was partitioned.

In Pakistan this resolution is regarded as the foundation of nation-building. At the spot where it was passed, the **Minar-e-Pakistan** was built in the 1960s, and **23 March** is a national holiday there as **"Pakistan Day"**. In India it is seen as the beginning of the political process that ended in the division of the country and the tragedy that came with it.
