## The Chauhans of Ranthambore

Near **Sawai Madhopur** in Rajasthan, in the middle of today's Ranthambore Tiger Reserve, on a hill about 700 feet high, stands the fort of **Ranthambore** (*Ranastambhapura* in Sanskrit). Because of its construction and position, it was considered almost impregnable. After the fall of Prithviraj Chauhan, a branch of the Chauhans kept their kingdom here.

The last king of this branch was **Hammir Dev** (or Hammir), who came to the throne in about **1283**, after his father **Jaitra Singh**. In the early years, until about **1288**, he raided neighbouring kingdoms, the most notable being his victory over the Paramaras of **Malwa**. The later *Hammira Mahakavya* describes his "digvijaya" (conquest of all directions) at length, but the historian Dasharatha Sharma considers this exaggerated, because Hammir's own inscriptions mention only his successes in Malwa.

## The Attacks of Jalaluddin Khalji

After the Khalji dynasty was founded in Delhi, Sultan **Jalaluddin Khalji** marched against Ranthambore in **1290** and besieged the fort, but, unable to win it, returned. In **1292** he attacked again, but could not even take the fort of **Jhain**. These failed campaigns raised the reputation of Ranthambore even further.

## The Question of Shelter (1299)

In **1299**, as Alauddin Khalji's army was returning from the Gujarat campaign, some **Mongol** soldiers rebelled over the division of the loot. Their leaders **Muhammad Shah** and **Kehbru** fled and reached Hammir, and Hammir gave them **shelter**. When Alauddin's brother **Ulugh Khan** demanded that they be killed or handed over, Hammir flatly refused. This refusal became the immediate cause of the war.

## The Siege (1301)

Ulugh Khan's army attacked and killed Hammir's commander **Bhimsingh**; Hammir's brother **Bhoj** went over to Alauddin. But Hammir's army won a victory in the **Hindowat Valley**. The commander **Nusrat Khan**, who had come to help, was killed by a stone from a **manjanīq** (a stone-throwing machine) hurled from the fort, and Hammir pushed Ulugh Khan back as far as Jhain.

Then **Alauddin Khalji** himself reached Ranthambore. His army built a sloping mound of earth (*pasheb*) to reach the fort wall. The siege lasted for months. Meanwhile Alauddin's nephew **Aqat Khan** tried to kill him and there were rebellions in Delhi too, but the Sultan did not leave the siege.

Conditions inside the fort kept getting worse. Grain began to run out and a **famine**-like situation arose. The biggest blow came when two of Hammir's commanders, **Ratipal** and **Ranmal**, changed sides and joined Alauddin.

## The Last Battle and Jauhar

In **July 1301**, when no way of escape remained, Hammir chose a final battle instead of surrender. According to tradition, the women of the fort performed **jauhar** under the leadership of Queen **Rangdevi**, and Hammir died fighting with his loyal companions. According to Firishta the fort was taken on **10 July 1301**, while the Jain writer Nayachandra gives a date two days later.

Alauddin's court poet **Amir Khusrau** described this campaign. After the victory, Alauddin also punished the traitors Ratipal and Ranmal, and handed the fort over to **Ulugh Khan**. About the Mongol leader Muhammad Shah it is said that even when captured he taunted Alauddin, after which he was put to death.

## Memory and Literature

The story of Hammir's **protection of the one who sought refuge** and his holding out to the end lives in Rajasthan's folk memory as a symbol of "hath" (stubborn resolve). In the fifteenth century **Nayachandra Suri** wrote the Sanskrit *Hammira Mahakavya* about him, and later Jodhraj's *Hammir Raso* and Chandrashekhar's *Hammir Hath* were composed. Note that the *Hammira Mahakavya* was written about a hundred years after his death, so historians treat its details with caution.

Today, since **2013**, Ranthambore Fort is part of the UNESCO World Heritage Site "**Hill Forts of Rajasthan**".
