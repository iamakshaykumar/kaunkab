## From Bombay to Cambridge

**Homi Jehangir Bhabha** was born on **30 October 1909** in a prosperous Parsi family of **Bombay**. His family wanted him to become an engineer, but while studying at **Gonville and Caius College**, Cambridge, he became absorbed in theoretical physics. In **1935** he published his calculation of the scattering of electrons and positrons, which is today called **"Bhabha scattering"**. With the German scientist **Walter Heitler** he also developed the theory of the "cascade" of cosmic rays.

## The War Kept Him in India

In **1939** Bhabha had come to India on holiday when the **Second World War** broke out and he stayed here. He took up the post of Reader in Physics at the **Indian Institute of Science (IISc)** in **Bengaluru**, whose head then was the Nobel laureate **C. V. Raman**. Here he led the cosmic ray research unit. In **1941** he was elected a Fellow of the **Royal Society**.

## The Founding of TIFR (1945)

Bhabha felt that India needed a world-class institute for fundamental science. In **March 1944** he sent a proposal for such an institute to the **Sir Dorabji Tata Trust**. With the support of the Tata family, especially **J. R. D. Tata**, the **Tata Institute of Fundamental Research (TIFR)** was founded on **1 June 1945**. It began first on the IISc campus in Bengaluru and moved to Bombay the same year. Bhabha remained its director for life.

TIFR produced many leading Indian scientists in physics, mathematics and later computer science and biology. It was here that India's first digital computer, **TIFRAC**, was built in **1957**. On **15 January 1962** Prime Minister **Jawaharlal Nehru** inaugurated TIFR's new campus on the seafront at Colaba.

## The Framework of Atomic Energy

After independence there was deep trust between Nehru and Bhabha. In **1948** the **Atomic Energy Commission** was formed and Bhabha became its chairman. On **3 January 1954** the **Atomic Energy Establishment (AEET)** began at **Trombay** near Bombay, and in the same year he became secretary of the newly formed **Department of Atomic Energy**. On **4 August 1956** its **Apsara** reactor went critical, and in **1960** the **CIRUS** reactor was built with Canadian cooperation. In **1955** Bhabha also presided over the United Nations conference on the **peaceful uses of atomic energy** held in Geneva.

India has little uranium but a large reserve of **thorium**. Keeping this in mind, Bhabha outlined a **three-stage nuclear programme** — natural-uranium reactors in the first stage, fast breeder reactors in the second, and the use of thorium in the third. India adopted it in **1958**, and it remains the basis of the country's nuclear policy even today.

## The Debate on the Bomb

After China's nuclear test in **1964**, Bhabha said publicly that India could carry out a nuclear test in about a year and a half once a decision was made. This led to a sharp debate in the country. Prime Minister **Lal Bahadur Shastri** took a middle path and permitted research only into a "peaceful nuclear explosion". Historians regard this period as an important turning point in India's nuclear policy.

## A Sudden End and Legacy

On **24 January 1966**, on the way to Geneva, **Air India Flight 101** crashed into **Mont Blanc** in the Alps and Bhabha died. The official inquiry gave the cause as a misunderstanding between the pilots and air traffic control. Some suspect a foreign conspiracy, but no independent proof of this has been found.

After his death, in **1967** the Trombay establishment was renamed the **Bhabha Atomic Research Centre (BARC)**. Bhabha is regarded as the father of the Indian nuclear programme — a scientist as skilled at building institutions as at solving equations.
