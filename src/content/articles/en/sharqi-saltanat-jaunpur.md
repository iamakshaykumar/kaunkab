## The breakup of the Tughluq empire

By the end of the 14th century the Tughluq Sultanate of Delhi had been weakened by internal quarrels. In 1394 Sultan Nasiruddin Mahmud Shah Tughluq made his wazir **Malik Sarwar** governor of the eastern provinces and gave him the title **Malik-ush-Sharq** (Lord of the East). Malik Sarwar, also called **Khwaja Jahan**, made Jaunpur his centre and began what was in practice independent rule. From this title the dynasty got the name **Sharqi** ("eastern").

The city of Jaunpur itself had been founded a few decades earlier, around 1359, by Firuz Shah Tughluq on the banks of the Gomti river. Timur's attack on Delhi in 1398 weakened Delhi further, which gave regional centres such as Jaunpur the chance to rise.

## Major sultans

- **Malik Sarwar** (1394–1399): the founder; after him his adopted son **Mubarak Shah** declared himself sultan.
- **Ibrahim Shah** (about 1402–1440): the golden age of the sultanate. He extended the kingdom towards Kannauj and Bihar, patronised scholars and issued coins in his own name.
- **Mahmud Shah** (1440–1457): he led campaigns towards Bengal, Odisha and Delhi; his conflict with the newly risen **Bahlul Lodi** in Delhi began.
- **Muhammad Shah**: after a short reign he was killed in a revolt by his brother.
- **Husain Shah** (1458–1479): the last Sharqi sultan.

## The Atala Mosque and Sharqi architecture

The greatest legacy of the Sharqi sultans is their architecture. The foundation of the **Atala Mosque** was laid in the time of Firuz Shah Tughluq (about 1376–77), but it was completed in **1408** in the time of Ibrahim Shah. It was built on the site of a temple of Atala Devi, and its pillars show the use of material from the old temple.

Its hallmark is the huge **pishtaq** standing in front of the prayer hall, a tall gateway with sloping towers behind which the dome is hidden. The same style is seen later in the **Jhanjhri Mosque**, the **Lal Darwaza Mosque** and the **Jama Masjid**, completed in the time of Husain Shah. Scholars regard it as a blend of the Tughluq style of Delhi and the local Hindu craft tradition.

## 'Shiraz-i-Hind'

The city of **Shiraz** in Iran was famous for poetry and learning. At the Sharqi court, scholars of Persian and Arabic, qazis and Sufis received so much patronage that Jaunpur came to be called **"Shiraz-i-Hind"** (the Shiraz of India). Its madrasas became famous far and wide.

In the field of music the name of the last sultan, **Husain Shah Sharqi**, is notable. Tradition links him with the development of the **khayal** style of singing and with the composition of some ragas; the name of the raga **Jaunpuri** is also linked to this city, although not every detail of these claims is proven.

## Conflict with the Lodis and the end

In the mid-15th century, with the rise of **Bahlul Lodi** in Delhi (from 1451), a long struggle went on between Jaunpur and Delhi. Husain Shah marched on Delhi several times but was finally defeated. In **1479** Bahlul Lodi took Jaunpur, and Husain Shah fled to Bihar, where he spent the rest of his life under the protection of the sultan of Bengal (he died in 1505).

Although Sharqi power ended, the mosques of Jaunpur and its tradition of learning still make this city a special milestone in the medieval history of North India.
