## A Fight That Began With Wages

The story of the right to information begins not in Parliament in Delhi but in the dry villages of Rajasthan. On **1 May 1990** the **Mazdoor Kisan Shakti Sangathan (MKSS)** was formed in **Devdungri** village in Rajsamand district. Its leading founders included former IAS officer **Aruna Roy**, social activist **Nikhil Dey** and local farmer-activist **Shankar Singh**.

At first the organisation's struggle was for minimum wages. Labourers working on government relief works often did not receive their full wages. When they raised questions, officials said their attendance was recorded as lower in the register (the muster roll). But the labourers were never allowed to see those records. From this came the understanding that justice was impossible without information.

## Public Hearings and the Beawar Sit-in

From the mid-1990s the MKSS adopted the method of the **jan sunwai** (public hearing). Documents of government spending, obtained somehow, were read out at an open village meeting. Villagers would say whether the road, school or well on which money had been shown as spent had actually been built, and whether the labourers whose names were recorded had really been at work. At many places these hearings exposed fake bills and the names of people who had never worked at all. A famous slogan of the movement was **"Hamara paisa, hamara hisaab"** (Our money, our accounts).

In **1996** the MKSS held a sit-in of about **40 days** in the town of **Beawar** in Rajasthan, demanding the right to information. People came from hundreds of villages, donated, and carried the demand to ordinary people through songs and plays. In the same year journalists, lawyers and activists from across the country together formed the **National Campaign for People's Right to Information (NCPRI)**.

## From the States to the Centre

Under pressure from the movement, several states made their own information laws: **Tamil Nadu** and **Goa** (1997), then **Rajasthan** (2000), **Karnataka**, **Maharashtra**, **Delhi** and others. At the Centre the **Freedom of Information Act** was passed in 2002, but it was considered weak and was never properly implemented. The Supreme Court had also said in several judgments that the right to know is part of freedom of expression (Article 19).

The UPA government formed in 2004 under **Dr. Manmohan Singh** promised a strong information law in its Common Minimum Programme. The National Advisory Council, of which Aruna Roy was also a member, played a role in drafting it. The Lok Sabha passed the **Right to Information Bill** on **11 May 2005** and the Rajya Sabha on **12 May 2005**. It received Presidential assent on **15 June 2005**, and from **12 October 2005** the law came fully into force.

## What the Law Says

- Any Indian citizen can seek information from any **public authority** — a government department, a government company or a body substantially funded by the government — by submitting a written application. They do not need to say why they want the information.
- Every office appoints a **Public Information Officer**, who must generally reply within **30 days**; in matters of life or liberty, within **48 hours**.
- The application fee for the central government was set at only **10 rupees**, and people below the poverty line are exempt from the fee.
- Under **Section 4**, government departments must publish a great deal of information on their own, without being asked.
- The **Central Information Commission** and State Information Commissions were set up to hear appeals. **Wajahat Habibullah** became the first Chief Information Commissioner. An officer who withholds information without reasonable cause can be fined **250 rupees per day**, up to a maximum of **25,000 rupees**.
- Some information relating to national security, foreign relations, privacy and so on is exempt, and certain intelligence and security organisations are outside the law's scope — although information can be sought from them on allegations of corruption and human rights violations.

## Impact, Challenges and Changes

RTI put a powerful tool in the hands of ordinary citizens. Lakhs of applications are filed every year, and information on everything from ration cards, pensions and stalled work to major scams has come out this way. It is counted among India's most effective transparency laws.

But there have also been challenges. Complaints about vacant posts in information commissions and large numbers of pending appeals have continued. According to activists, many people who used RTI were attacked and some were even killed, so demands for stronger protection for them have continued. The **2019** amendment gave the central government the power to fix the tenure and salaries of information commissioners; the government described it as a step to rationalise the system, while critics said it weakened the independence of the commissions. A similar debate arose over the change made by the **2023** Digital Personal Data Protection Act to the provision exempting personal information.

Despite these debates, the right to information reminds us that a simple question from a village's labourers about seeing a muster roll can lead to a law that makes the democracy of a whole country more accountable.
