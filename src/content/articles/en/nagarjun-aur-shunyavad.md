## The "Second Buddha"

In the history of Buddhist philosophy, **Nagarjuna** is perhaps considered the most influential thinker after the Buddha. He was a Mahayana monk and philosopher of **South India**, who is generally placed between **about 150 and 250 CE**. He laid the foundation of **Madhyamaka** (Middle Way) philosophy, which in India was often also called **"Shunyavada"** (the doctrine of emptiness). In Tibet he is called the **"Second Buddha"**, and many Buddhist traditions of East Asia regard him as their teacher.

## Life: Few Facts, Many Legends

Very little is firmly known about Nagarjuna's life. His biographies were written centuries later — the oldest Chinese biography is linked with the translator **Kumarajiva** (early 5th century) — and they are full of miracles. According to the legends he was a Brahmin by birth, knew the art of mantras, and went to the **realm of the Nagas** and brought the Prajnaparamita sutras from there. These traditions also do not agree with one another about his birthplace and his studying at **Nalanda**.

Many scholars, such as Joseph Walser, link him with the **Satavahana** dynasty — perhaps with the time of King **Yajnashri Satakarni** — which supports his traditional date. His works the *Suhrllekha* ("Letter to a Friend") and the *Ratnavali* are in the form of advice given to a king. Tibetan historians link him with **Sriparvata** in Andhra, but this connection is uncertain.

## The Mulamadhyamakakarika

Nagarjuna's most important work is the **Mulamadhyamakakarika** ("Fundamental Verses on the Middle Way"). It has about **450 karikas** (short verses) in **27 chapters**, and its Sanskrit original is still available. The text begins with a salutation to the Buddha, who taught **pratityasamutpada** (dependent origination) in the form of eight negations — no cessation, no origination; no annihilation, no permanence; no singularity, no plurality; no coming, no going.

Besides this, many works such as the *Vigrahavyavartani*, *Shunyatasaptati* and *Yuktishashtika* are also attributed to him, although scholars disagree on his authorship of many of them.

## The Meaning of Emptiness

Nagarjuna's central idea is that nothing in the world has **svabhava** — that is, an existence resting in itself, independent and unchanging. Everything comes into existence only depending on causes, parts and other things. This very "absence of own-nature" he calls **shunyata** (emptiness).

So emptiness does not mean "nothing exists". In the twenty-fourth chapter Nagarjuna says that whatever arises in dependence is itself called emptiness, and this is the **Middle Way** — that is, the path between the two extremes of "everything exists permanently" and "nothing exists".

In his reasoning Nagarjuna uses the **catuskoti** (four alternatives) — a thing arises from itself, from another, from both, or without a cause — and rejects all four. He also distinguishes **two truths**: **samvriti satya** (conventional truth, by which the world runs) and **paramartha satya** (ultimate truth, that is, emptiness). In the twenty-fifth chapter he even says that there is not the slightest difference between samsara and nirvana.

## Disciples and Influence

Nagarjuna's disciple **Aryadeva** carried his ideas forward. Later **Buddhapalita**, **Bhavaviveka** and, in the 7th century, **Chandrakirti** wrote famous commentaries on the Mulamadhyamakakarika; Chandrakirti's *Prasannapada* is considered the most influential commentary in Tibetan Buddhism. Kumarajiva made a Chinese translation of it in 409 CE, through which this philosophy reached China, Korea and Japan.

## Significance

For about a thousand years Nagarjuna's philosophy remained at the centre of Indian philosophical debates — Nyaya, Vedanta and Jain thinkers engaged deeply with him and opposed him. Even today philosophers around the world write on his arguments. A thinker about whose life we know almost nothing is counted, because of his ideas, among India's most famous philosophers.
