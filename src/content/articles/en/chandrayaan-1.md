## The Dream of Reaching the Moon

The idea of sending a scientific mission to the Moon was raised in India in **1999** at a meeting of the Indian Academy of Sciences. After this **ISRO** set up a National Lunar Mission Task Force, which said that India had the technical capability for it. In April 2003 more than a hundred scientists considered the proposal and agreed to it. On **15 August 2003**, in his Independence Day speech, Prime Minister **Atal Bihari Vajpayee** announced the project, and in November 2003 the government formally approved it.

The mission was named **Chandrayaan** — in Sanskrit 'chandra' means the Moon and 'yaan' means vehicle. Its estimated cost was about **386 crore rupees**, much less than the lunar missions of other countries. The project director was **Mylswamy Annadurai**, and at that time the chairman of ISRO was **G. Madhavan Nair**.

## Launch and Lunar Orbit

On the morning of **22 October 2008** the **PSLV-C11** rocket lifted off with Chandrayaan-1 from the **Satish Dhawan Space Centre** at **Sriharikota** in Andhra Pradesh. At launch the spacecraft weighed about **1,380 kilograms**.

The spacecraft first reached Earth orbit. Then its engine was fired several times to enlarge the orbit gradually, so that it could move towards the Moon. On **8 November 2008** the engine was fired for about thirteen and a half minutes and the spacecraft was placed in lunar orbit. Later it was brought into a polar orbit about **100 kilometres** above the lunar surface, from where it could survey the whole Moon.

## The Moon Impact Probe and Jawahar Point

Chandrayaan-1 also carried a small device of about 35 kilograms, the **Moon Impact Probe (MIP)**. On **14 November 2008** it separated from the main spacecraft and, descending for about 25 minutes, struck as planned near **Shackleton crater** close to the Moon's south pole. As it fell it kept sending pictures and data. A picture of the tricolour was painted on its sides.

That day was the birth anniversary of **Jawaharlal Nehru**, so the impact site was named **Jawahar Point**. With this, India's space agency became the **fifth** national agency to reach the surface of the Moon, after the Soviet Union, the United States, Japan and the European Space Agency. It is said that this probe was added to the mission on the suggestion of former President **A. P. J. Abdul Kalam**.

## Eleven Instruments, International Cooperation

Chandrayaan-1 carried a total of **11 scientific instruments** — **five Indian** and **six foreign**. The foreign instruments came from the American agency **NASA**, the European Space Agency and Bulgaria. Their job was to make a three-dimensional map of the Moon, find out the chemical elements and minerals on the surface, and examine those craters at the poles where sunlight never reaches and where ice was likely to be hidden.

In this way the mission became an example not only of India's effort but also of international scientific cooperation. During its working life the spacecraft made more than 3,000 orbits of the Moon and sent thousands of pictures back to Earth.

## The Discovery of Water Molecules

Chandrayaan-1's biggest achievement was its discovery related to **water** on the Moon. NASA's instrument, the **Moon Mineralogy Mapper (M3)**, picked up signals in the light reflected from the lunar surface that indicated the presence of **water (H₂O)** and **hydroxyl (OH)** molecules. These signals were strongest in the cold polar regions, but were spread as a very thin layer over a large part of the surface.

On **24 September 2009** the discovery was published in the well-known scientific journal *Science*. The next day ISRO reported that the Moon Impact Probe's instrument **CHACE** had also recorded signs of water in the Moon's extremely thin atmosphere during its descent. Another NASA instrument on Chandrayaan-1, the **Mini-SAR** radar, gave signs of ice in the dark craters of the north pole.

For a long time the Moon was thought to be completely dry. This discovery changed the thinking of scientists and started a new discussion on the possibility of building human bases on the Moon in the future.

## End of the Mission and Legacy

Chandrayaan-1 was meant to work for two years, but after a few months technical problems began — because of excess heat the spacecraft's orbit had to be raised to 200 kilometres, and the 'star sensor' that gave its direction failed. On **28 August 2009** contact with the spacecraft was lost and ISRO declared the mission over. It lasted a total of **312 days**, but according to ISRO most of its scientific goals had already been achieved.

Chandrayaan-1 gave India confidence for deep-space missions. On the basis of this experience, **Mangalyaan** was sent in 2013, **Chandrayaan-2** in 2019 and **Chandrayaan-3** in 2023, which made history by landing successfully near the Moon's south pole. In a sense, the foundation of all of them was laid by this small but bold mission of 2008.
