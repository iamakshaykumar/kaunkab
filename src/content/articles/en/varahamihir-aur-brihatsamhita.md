## Who Was Varahamihira?

**Varahamihira** (also called simply "Varaha" or "Mihira") was a famous astronomer, mathematician and astrologer of the 6th century. His time is generally taken to be **c. 505 to 587 CE**. The year of birth is derived from a calculation based on the Shaka era, while the year of death, 587, rests on the statement of a later commentator, and some scholars doubt it.

His father's name was **Adityadasa**, meaning "servant of the Sun" — this hints that the family worshipped the Sun. "Kapitthaka" is named as his birthplace, but there is disagreement about where this place was. Varahamihira himself called himself **"Avantyaka"**, that is, a resident of Avanti. For this reason he is linked with Ujjain, although it is also possible that his connection was with the whole Avanti region rather than the city alone.

## The Story of the Nine Gems

In folk tradition Varahamihira is counted among the "Navaratnas" (nine gems) of the court of King **Vikramaditya**. But historians do not regard this story as historical, because the list comes from a much later text and places scholars of different centuries in a single court. It is fair to treat it as a memory of tradition, not an established fact.

## Panchasiddhantika: A Summary of Five Systems

In Varahamihira's time several methods of astronomical calculation were in use in India. In the **Panchasiddhantika** he presented a summary and comparison of five systems:
- **Surya Siddhanta**
- **Paulisha Siddhanta**
- **Romaka Siddhanta**
- **Vasishtha Siddhanta**
- **Paitamaha Siddhanta**

Among these he regarded the Surya Siddhanta as the most accurate. Names such as Romaka and Paulisha show that at that time Indian astronomy had also been somewhat influenced by the Greco-Roman tradition. This text is a major source of information on those older systems whose original texts are not available today.

## Brihat Samhita: An Ancient Encyclopedia

The **Brihat Samhita** is Varahamihira's most famous work. It has a little over a hundred chapters (the number varies between editions). Its basic subject is planets, stars and omens, but it also includes many practical subjects of life:
- **Weather** — clouds, rain, wind, rainbows, meteors and earthquakes.
- **Farming and trade** — crops, the care of trees and plants, and ups and downs in the prices of goods.
- **Water** — a chapter on signs for finding underground sources of water.
- **Architecture (Vastu)** — the construction of houses, palaces and temples.
- **Sculpture** — the characteristics of images of deities, including images of the Brahmanical, Buddhist and Jain traditions.
- **Gems, perfumes and more** — testing diamonds, pearls and rubies, methods of making fragrant substances, and a recipe for a strong glue (vajralepa).

It should be remembered that this text contains much about omens and predictive astrology, which is not regarded as science today. But it is a priceless source giving a glimpse of the society, art and knowledge of 6th-century India. The commentary on it by **Bhattotpala** (Utpala) is the most famous.

## Other Works and Mathematics

Varahamihira also wrote the **Brihajjataka** on horoscopes and its shorter form the **Laghujataka**, and the **Yogayatra** on auspicious times for travel. In mathematics he gave some important relations in trigonometry and refined Aryabhata's table of sines. The Brihat Samhita contains a table of combinations, which is mathematically like the later "Pascal's triangle".

## Legacy

Varahamihira's works continued to be read for centuries. In the 11th century **Al-Biruni** quoted passages from the Brihat Samhita in his book on India and regarded Varahamihira as an excellent astronomer. Later, in the time of Firoz Shah Tughlaq, it was translated into Persian. Even today his works are a main foundation for understanding the history of Indian science and culture.
