## Background: Land and Discontent

In Malabar (the northern part of present-day Kerala), land was mostly held by **Jenmi** landowners — most of them Namboodiri Brahmin and Nair families. A large number of the cultivating tenants were Muslim **Mappilas** (called "Moplahs" in English documents) and members of the Thiyya community. British courts treated the rights of the Jenmis as absolute, so there was always the fear of eviction and high rents. In the nineteenth century there had already been several small Mappila revolts here.

In 1920–21 the **Khilafat** and **Non-Cooperation** movements reached Malabar. Khilafat committees and Congress meetings became linked with peasant discontent. The government banned meetings and arrested leaders.

## The Outbreak of the Revolt

In early August 1921, a crowd foiled an attempt to arrest a Khilafat worker at Pookkottur. On **20 August 1921** the police raided Tirurangadi to arrest Khilafat volunteers. A rumour of the desecration of a mosque spread, a crowd gathered, the police fired, and within a few days the revolt spread across the Ernad and Valluvanad taluks.

Early leadership was in the hands of **Ali Musliyar**; later **Variyankunnath Kunhahammed Haji** became the chief leader. The rebels attacked police stations, treasuries, courts and registry offices, and in some areas declared their own rule. For several weeks British administration in these areas almost came to a halt.

## Violence on Many Sides

The first targets of violence were British officials, the police and Jenmi landlords. Later, in many places, **ordinary Hindus** were also attacked — murders, plunder and forced conversions were recorded. There is deep disagreement over their numbers: estimates by district officers and later researchers range from a few hundred to a few thousand. Kunhahammed Haji denied forced conversion and distanced himself from such incidents.

The British imposed martial law, sent Gurkha and British units and raised the "Malabar Special Police". The repression was extremely harsh. In official figures about **2,300** rebels were killed and more than **45,000** taken prisoner; unofficial estimates put the dead at about 10,000. Thousands of people were sent to distant places, including the Andamans. In January 1922 Kunhahammed Haji was captured and sentenced to death, and by early 1922 the revolt was suppressed.

## The Wagon Tragedy

On the evening of **19 November 1921**, about a hundred Mappila prisoners were sent from Tirur in a **closed goods wagon** to Podanur near Coimbatore. When the wagon was opened after midnight on 20 November, 64 prisoners had died of suffocation; a few more died later in hospital, bringing the total dead to about 70. The event became a symbol of the inhumanity of British repression.

## Different Interpretations by Historians

- **Peasant revolt:** Historians such as K. N. Panikkar and Robert Hardgrave regard it as a revolt of peasants against landlords and the colonial state, whose language was religious.
- **Communal aspect:** Colonial officials, and critics such as Annie Besant at the time and B. R. Ambedkar later, put the violence against Hindus at the centre and link it to religious frenzy.
- **Anti-colonial struggle:** Many Indian historians and the Kerala government (1971) regard it as part of the freedom struggle.

Many scholars, such as Conrad Wood, see it as a mixture of all these elements. At the time the Congress distanced itself from the violence, while K. P. Kesava Menon, a Congress leader of Malabar, held police repression responsible for the revolt. In 2021 a proposal to remove the names of those killed in the rebellion from the Indian Council of Historical Research (ICHR) martyrs' dictionary again led to sharp debate. The rebellion still reminds us how complexly land, religion and anti-imperialism can be entangled in a single event.
