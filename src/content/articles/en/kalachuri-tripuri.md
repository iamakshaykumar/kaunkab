## The Chedi Country and Tripuri

The region around the Narmada in central India was anciently called **Chedi** or **Dahala**. From about the seventh to the thirteenth century a branch of the **Kalachuri** dynasty ruled here, with its capital at **Tripuri**. This place is known today as the village of **Tewar** near **Jabalpur** (Madhya Pradesh). That is why they are called the **Kalachuris of Tripuri**, the Kalachuris of Chedi, or the "later Kalachuris".

The Kalachuris described themselves as belonging to the **Haihaya** dynasty and linked their genealogy to the mythical king **Kartavirya Arjuna**. Their real origin is uncertain; the historian V. V. Mirashi linked them to the older Kalachuris of Mahishmati, but there is no firm proof of this. Shaivism appears prominent in their inscriptions.

## From Subordination to Independence

At first the Kalachuris were subordinate first to the **Rashtrakutas** and then to the **Gurjara-Pratiharas**. **Kokkala I** (c. **850–890**) is regarded as the first powerful ruler of this dynasty. When the Pratihara and Rashtrakuta empires weakened, the Kalachuris gradually became independent — probably in the time of **Yuvarajadeva I** (c. **915–945**).

## Gangeyadeva — Towards Empire

**Gangeyadeva** (c. **1015 to 22 January 1041**) made the Kalachuri kingdom a great power. His inscription of 1019 has only ordinary titles, which suggests that at first he was perhaps subordinate to the Paramara king **Bhoja**. He fought the Chalukyas of Kalyani together with Bhoja and the Chola king **Rajendra Chola**, but in the end had to withdraw. According to some sources Bhoja later even defeated Gangeya.

In the 1030s Gangeyadeva campaigned in the east as far as **Utkala** (Odisha) and in the north into the territory of the weakened Chandelas. By about **1033–34** **Varanasi** had come under his control. In the **Piyavan inscription** of 1037–38 he took imperial titles such as "Maharajadhiraja" and the epithet "**Vikramaditya**".

Gangeyadeva is especially famous for his **gold coins**. On one side of them is the four-armed goddess **Lakshmi** seated cross-legged, and on the other, in three lines in Nagari script, is written "Shrimad Gangeyadeva". This design became so popular that later the **Chandela** and **Gahadavala** kings also issued coins of the same kind. According to Kalachuri inscriptions Gangeyadeva died at **Prayaga**.

## Lakshmikarna — Karna the 'Chakravarti'

Gangeyadeva's son **Lakshmikarna**, also called **Karna**, ruled from about **1041 to 1073** and is regarded as the most famous warrior of this dynasty. An inscription of his from 1042 mentions the shraddha ceremony for his father at Prayaga.

Karna attacked **Vanga** and **Anga** in the east and raided as far as **Kanchi** in the south. He first fought the Chaulukya king of Gujarat, **Bhima I**, and then the two together opposed the Paramara king of Malwa, **Bhoja**. After Bhoja's power broke around **1055**, Karna held Malwa for a short time, but within a few months Jayasimha, backed by the Chaulukyas, took it back. Around **1052–53** he took the title "**Chakravarti**"; he was also called "Trikalingadhipati".

In Bundelkhand he defeated the Chandela king **Devavarman** and for more than ten years held a part of Chandela territory, which was later recovered by **Kirtivarman**. After a conflict with the Pala king of Bengal a treaty was made, and Karna's daughter **Yauvanashri** was married to the Pala king. The sources do not agree on the outcome of this war. In a final campaign against Malwa around **1073** he was defeated by Bhoja's brother **Udayaditya**, and around the same time he handed the throne to his son **Yashahkarna**.

Karna was also a patron of scholars; the Kashmiri poet **Bilhana** has described his court in his *Vikramankadevacharita*.

## Decline

After Lakshmikarna the dynasty's power began to decline. In the time of **Yashahkarna** the northern region including **Varanasi** passed into the hands of the **Gahadavalas**, and the Kalachuri branch of **Ratanpur** (Chhattisgarh) became independent. The last known king, **Trailokyamalla**, is mentioned until at least **1212**, but how his rule ended is unknown. Later the region was divided among the Paramaras, Chandelas, the Yadavas of Devagiri and the Delhi Sultanate.
