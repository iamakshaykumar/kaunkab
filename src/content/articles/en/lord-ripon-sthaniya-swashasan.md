## The Arrival of a Liberal Viceroy

In **1880** the Liberal government of **William Gladstone** was formed in Britain, and **Lord Ripon** (George Robinson, 1st Marquess of Ripon) was sent to India as Viceroy. Before him, in the time of **Lord Lytton**, steps such as the **Vernacular Press Act**, the Delhi Durbar held in the middle of a famine and the Afghan war had caused great discontent in Indian opinion.

Like Gladstone, Ripon believed that British rule was justified only if it gradually prepared Indians to manage their own affairs. He repealed the Vernacular Press Act in **1882** and then moved to increase Indian participation in local government.

## Earlier Efforts

Municipalities already existed in India — Madras had had a municipal corporation since 1688, and in the nineteenth century several municipal laws were made in Bengal, Bombay, Punjab and elsewhere. In **1870** the government of **Lord Mayo** issued a resolution on **financial decentralisation**, giving the provinces responsibility for meeting the expenses of some departments themselves and speaking of the use of local taxes.

But most members of these bodies were nominated by the government, and the chairman was usually the district collector or some British officer. Real power remained in the hands of government officials.

## The Resolution of 18 May 1882

On **18 May 1882** the Government of India issued **Ripon's Resolution** on Local Self-Government. Its main points were:

- **A network of local boards** — **district boards** for rural areas throughout the country, with **local boards at the taluka / tehsil level** below them, and stronger municipalities in the towns.
- **Non-official majority** — these boards should have a majority of **non-official members**.
- **Elections** — wherever local conditions permitted, members should be **elected**, not merely nominated.
- **Non-official chairman** — wherever possible, the chairman of the board should also be a non-official.
- **Control from outside** — government officers should **supervise from outside** rather than sit within the boards and give orders. Government permission should be needed only for matters such as raising loans, imposing new taxes, selling municipal property or large construction works.
- **Adequate funds and duties** — local bodies should be given definite duties such as roads, sanitation, primary education, hospitals and water, and adequate sources of income for them.

The most important idea of the resolution was that the purpose of local self-government was not merely administrative efficiency but **"political and popular education"**. Ripon admitted that at first Indian members might not work as efficiently as British officers, but he believed that ability comes only through experience. In his view, only when given responsibility would educated Indians take an interest in public affairs, and trust between the government and the people would grow.

## Implementation and Limits

After this resolution, new laws were made in several provinces between **1883 and 1885**, such as the Local Boards Acts of Madras and Bombay (1884) and the **Bengal Local Self-Government Act (1885)**. Changes were also made in the constitution and powers of municipalities.

But in practice the spirit of the resolution could not be fully implemented. The provincial bureaucracy did not agree with Ripon's liberal views; many officers believed that Indians were unfit for self-government. In many places the right to vote remained very limited, the district collector often continued as chairman of the district board, and local bodies were always short of money. In this same period the **Ilbert Bill controversy (1883)** showed how strongly the European community living in India opposed giving Indians equality.

## Significance and Legacy

Despite its shortcomings, Ripon's resolution was an important step towards representative institutions in India. For this reason he is called the **"father of local self-government in India"**. Working in district boards and municipalities, many Indians gained experience of elections, budgets and public debate, which proved useful later in national politics.

During Ripon's term steps such as the **1881** census, the first **Factory Act (1881)** and the **Hunter Commission (1882)** on education were also taken. He was so popular among Indians that on his departure in **1884** meetings were held in his honour in many cities. This idea of local self-government later developed through the **Decentralisation Commission of 1909**, the **reforms of 1919**, and, in independent India, into the panchayati raj and municipal system created by the **73rd and 74th Constitutional Amendments (1992)**.
