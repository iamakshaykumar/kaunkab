## Trade Tied to the Monsoon

Trade between India and the Mediterranean world was old, but it took a sharp leap after Emperor **Augustus** added **Egypt** to the Roman Empire in **30 BCE**. From the ports on Egypt's Red Sea coast — **Myos Hormos** and **Berenike** — ships began to cross the Arabian Sea and reach India directly. This became possible through knowledge of the **monsoon winds**, for which Greek tradition credits a sailor named **Hippalus**. The Greek geographer **Strabo** wrote that in the time of Augustus about **120 ships** went to India from Myos Hormos every year.

## The Periplus: A Merchant's Guidebook

The **"Periplus of the Erythraean Sea"** (a voyage around the Erythraean Sea) is a practical handbook written in **Greek**, by an unknown author — probably a Greek merchant of Egypt. Its composition is usually placed in the **middle of the 1st century CE** (about 40–70 CE). It gives details of ports, the goods bought and sold there, and the routes. Among India's major ports it names, on the west coast, **Barbarikon** (the Indus delta) and **Barygaza** (Bharuch), and on the Malabar coast **Muziris** and **Nelkynda**; towards the east coast it also mentions **Poduke**, which most scholars identify with Arikamedu.

## The Muziris Papyrus

A document kept in the papyrus collection of Vienna — the **Muziris Papyrus** (middle of the 2nd century CE) — gives a rare glimpse of the scale of this trade. On one side is a **loan contract** taken for a trading voyage to **Muziris**, and on the other the account of customs duty on Indian goods that arrived on a ship called **Hermapollon**. These goods included **nard** (fragrant oil) from the Ganges region, **ivory**, cloth and — according to the latest reading — a huge quantity of **black pepper**. The total value of the goods ran into millions of coins; scholars' interpretations differ on the exact figures.

## Arikamedu: A Port Found in Excavation

**Arikamedu**, on the bank of the Ariyankuppam river about 4 kilometres from Puducherry, had already drawn the attention of French scholars in the 1930s. **Mortimer Wheeler** excavated here in **1945** and described it as a trading settlement connected with Rome. Here were found Italian **Arretine** pottery, **amphorae** (jars) for wine and oil, and glass and stone beads. Later the excavations of **Vimala Begley** and K. V. Raman (1989–92) showed that the settlement was inhabited from before Roman contact, from about the **2nd century BCE**, and lasted until the seventh or eighth century CE — that is, it was basically an **Indian port** where Roman goods also arrived, and not a "Roman colony".

## Coin Hoards and Pliny's Complaint

In south India — especially Tamil Nadu, Kerala and Andhra — many hoards of **Roman gold and silver coins** have been found, a large number of them coins of early emperors such as **Augustus** and **Tiberius**. The **Pudukkottai** hoard is one example. The Roman writer **Pliny the Elder** complained that India drew at least **50 million sesterces** every year from the Roman Empire. The Sangam poem **Akananuru** also describes the ships of the **Yavanas** "coming with gold and returning with black pepper".

## Significance and Decline

This trade shows that ancient India, especially the **Chera**, **Pandya** and **Chola** regions of the south, was an active part of a vast Indian Ocean network. With the crisis of the Roman Empire in the middle of the 3rd century CE this trade declined, although it partly revived in the 4th century. A statue of the Buddha found at Berenike in Egypt shows that this movement was two-way — Indian traders also reached the shores of the Red Sea.
