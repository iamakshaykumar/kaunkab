## What Neolithic Means

**Neolithic** literally means the "New Stone Age". This period is identified not only by ground and polished stone axes but by a major change in life — **farming**, **animal rearing**, pottery and permanent villages. In the Indian subcontinent this change did not come at the same time everywhere. At **Mehrgarh** in Balochistan, wheat-and-barley farming and the rearing of sheep, goats and cattle are seen from about **7000 BCE**, while Neolithic settlements in Kashmir, the Ganges valley and the Deccan belong mainly to the third and second millennia BCE. Every region had its own crops, its own houses and its own traditions.

## Burzahom: The Pit-Dwellings of Kashmir

At **Burzahom**, on the Karewa plateau near Srinagar, Helmut de Terra and T. T. Paterson made a small investigation in the 1930s, and then the Archaeological Survey of India under **T. N. Khazanchi** carried out extensive excavations from **1960 to 1971**. The settlement here is broadly believed to begin from **about 3000 BCE**.

- In the **first phase** people lived in round or oval **pit-dwellings** dug into the ground; some had steps for going down. In Kashmir's harsh winter these pits must have given warmth.
- In the **second phase** houses began to be built at ground level, with floors plastered with clay.
- Bone and deer-antler tools — needles, awls, spear points — have been found in large numbers.
- In human graves, skeletons of **dogs** and deer were found, and some skulls showed marks of **holes** (trepanation).
- A **hunting scene** is engraved on a stone slab.

The **rectangular stone harvesters** (sickles) of Burzahom and some other items resemble the Neolithic cultures of northern China, so many scholars see the possibility of trans-Himalayan contacts. Also, on some pots here, horned designs similar to those of **Kot Diji** in the Indus region are found.

## The Ganges Valley: Chirand and Koldihwa

At **Chirand**, on the northern bank of the Ganga in Bihar's Saran district, excavations were carried out by the Bihar State Archaeology Directorate in **1962–63** and again in the 1970s. The Neolithic phase here is placed between about **2500 and 1345 BCE**. People lived in round huts of mud and reeds, and marks of **rice husk** on pottery show that rice was their main crop; wheat, barley and pulses were also grown. Most notable are about **400 bone and horn tools**.

**Koldihwa** (Belan valley) in Uttar Pradesh was once famous for its evidence of the "world's oldest rice", but its very old radiocarbon dates are **disputed**. New studies show that clear Neolithic occupation here dates to **after about 1900 BCE**.

## The Ash Mounds of the South

The most unusual mark of the Neolithic (c. **3000 – 1200 BCE**) of Karnataka and Andhra-Telangana is the **ash mounds**. In the nineteenth century **Robert Bruce Foote** recorded them. **F. R. Allchin**, in the excavation of **Utnur** (report 1961), found **enclosures** made with wooden posts and hoofprints of cattle, which strengthened the interpretation that these mounds were formed by **repeated burning of dung** collected in cattle pens. Later studies confirmed this by identifying microscopic plant remains of dung in the ash. Sites such as **Kupgal-Sanganakallu** and **Budihal** also belong to this tradition. It is clear that cattle rearing had a large role in the economy here.

## Importance of These Discoveries

The picture of Neolithic India is not one story but a combination of many regional stories. Pit-dwellings suited to the cold in Kashmir, rice farming in the Ganges valley, and a life based on animal rearing in the Deccan — all these show that the road to farming was different in every place. Many dates are still under debate, and new radiocarbon tests are repeatedly re-examining older conclusions. Even so, it was these very villages that laid the foundation of the later **Chalcolithic** cultures and the settlements of the historical period.
