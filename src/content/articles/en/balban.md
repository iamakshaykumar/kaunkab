## Background: From Slave to Sultan

Balban's original name was Baha-ud-din, and he was born around 1216 into an **Ilbari Turk** family of Central Asia. As a child the Mongols captured him and sold him as a slave. Eventually Sultan **Iltutmish** bought him in Delhi. By his own ability he entered the group of Turkish slave-nobles chosen by Iltutmish, which later historians call the **"Chalisa"** (Turkan-i-Chihalgani, the Forty).

Under Sultan **Nasir-ud-din Mahmud** (1246–1266), Balban became the naib (deputy ruler) with the title "Ulugh Khan" and the real centre of power. After Nasir-ud-din's death, he became sultan himself in 1266 and took the title "Ghiyas-ud-din".

## The Theory of Kingship

Since the time of Razia, there had been a tug-of-war for power between the sultan and the Turkish nobles. Balban answered this by raising the prestige of the sultan very high. According to Barani's account, his ideas were:
- The sultan is **"Zill-i-Ilahi"** (the shadow of God) on earth, and his authority comes from divine grace.
- Distance between king and subjects is necessary; the sultan should not mix freely with ordinary people.
- Power should go only to people of high lineage. Balban himself claimed to be a descendant of **Afrasiyab**, the Turanian hero of the Iranian epic.

## Sijda, Paibos and Court Splendour

To display this theory, Balban made the court highly disciplined and grand. He adopted rituals inspired by Iranian tradition — **sijda** (bowing to the ground before the sultan) and **paibos** (kissing the sultan's feet). The Persian New Year, **Nauroz**, also began to be celebrated with great pomp. Barani writes that Balban was never seen laughing or joking in court and was always in dignified dress.

## Harsh Administration: Nobles, Spies and Rebellion

Balban broke the power of the "Chalisa" nobles; many influential nobles were given severe punishments. **Barids** (spies) were appointed across the kingdom, who reported directly to the sultan. The army department (Diwan-i-Arz) was also strengthened.

Roads around Delhi were unsafe because of raids by the **Mewatis**. Balban had forests cleared, built outposts and forts, and took harsh military action in these areas. Around 1275, **Tughril Khan**, the governor of Bengal, rebelled and declared himself independent; after several failed attempts, Balban himself led a campaign and crushed the rebellion, and made his son **Bughra Khan** governor of Bengal. Historians often call this harsh policy the "policy of blood and iron".

## Defence against the Mongols, the Last Years and Assessment

The Mongols remained a constant threat in the north-west. Balban had the frontier forts repaired and stationed a strong army in the Multan-Lahore region, led by his elder son **Prince Muhammad**. In **1285**, Prince Muhammad was killed fighting the Mongols. Balban never recovered from this blow and died in **1287**.

He had chosen his grandson Kaikhusrau as his successor, but the nobles placed **Qaiqubad** on the throne. Because of weak rule, the dynasty came to an end by 1290 and the Khaljis came to power.

Balban is seen as a ruler who gave stability to a crumbling sultanate and made the sultan's authority supreme. But his strictness, his rule based on fear and his suppression of the nobles also wiped out a whole generation of capable leadership.
