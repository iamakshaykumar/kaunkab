## From Chaos to Kingship

In the seventh century, after Shashanka, the ruler of Gauda, Bengal went through a long period of political instability. Later Pala inscriptions call this period **"matsya nyaya"** — that is, just as a big fish swallows a small one, the powerful were oppressing the weak.

Around 750 CE a local chieftain named **Gopala** came to power. The Khalimpur copper plate of Dharmapala says that the people chose Gopala as king to restore order. Historians believe this may mean the consent of the leading chiefs; even so, this episode is unusual for that age.

## Dharmapala, Devapala and the Tripartite Struggle

During the time of Gopala's son **Dharmapala** (c. 770–810 CE), the Pala kingdom expanded greatly. The prestigious city of North India in that age was **Kanauj**, and a long struggle was fought among three powers to control it:
- the **Palas** in the east,
- the **Gurjara-Pratiharas** in the west and central India,
- the **Rashtrakutas** of the Deccan.

In history this is called the "tripartite struggle". Dharmapala for a time placed a ruler who supported him on the throne at Kanauj, but this position did not last. In the time of his son **Devapala** too, Pala power remained impressive. Later, after ups and downs, rulers such as Mahipala I and Ramapala restored the kingdom. In the twelfth century, with the rise of the Sena dynasty, Pala rule came to an end.

## The Vikramashila Mahavihara

The Pala rulers were patrons of Buddhism, although their inscriptions also record grants made to Brahmins. Dharmapala is credited with the founding of the **Vikramashila Mahavihara**. Its remains have been found today near the village of Antichak in Bhagalpur district of Bihar.

Vikramashila was mainly a great centre for the study of Buddhist philosophy and especially the Tantrayana (Vajrayana) tradition. Grammar, logic and philosophy were also taught here. Excavations have revealed a large cross-shaped central stupa with monks' cells around it. It had a close relationship with other mahaviharas such as Nalanda, Odantapuri and Somapura.

According to Tibetan sources, admission to Vikramashila was difficult and scholars were given titles such as "pandit". The mahavihara was run by a council of leading teachers. Some parts of these accounts were written later, so they should be read with caution, but they give an idea of the reputation of this institution.

## Influence That Reached Tibet

Among the most famous teachers of Vikramashila was **Atisha Dipankara Shrijnana**. In the eleventh century (around the 1040s) he went to Tibet and played a large role in the reorganisation of Buddhism there. He still holds a high place in the Tibetan tradition. Many Buddhist texts of the Pala period were translated into Tibetan, because of which many texts survive today only in Tibetan form.

## Art and Legacy

The black stone and bronze sculptures of the Pala period are known as the "Pala style". The influence of this style is seen in the art of Nepal, Tibet, Myanmar and Southeast Asia as well. Illustrated manuscripts on palm leaf are also a gift of this age.

Around 1200 CE, during the campaigns of Bakhtiyar Khalji, Vikramashila was destroyed. Today the Archaeological Survey of India looks after its remains, and these ruins remind us that eastern India was once a centre of knowledge for students from all over Asia.
