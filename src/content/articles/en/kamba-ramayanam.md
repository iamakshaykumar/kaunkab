## The Poet Kambar

**Kambar** (or **Kamban**) is counted among the greatest poets of the Tamil language. According to most sources his period is **c. 1180–1250**, and he was born at **Therezhundur** in present-day Tamil Nadu. However, some writers place him earlier, in the Chola period of the **ninth century** — that is, scholars differ about his date.

Kambar received the patronage of **Sadaiyappa Vallal**, the chieftain of Tiruvennainallur. In gratitude, Kambar named his patron in the epic **once in every thousand verses**. According to tradition, the Chola king **Kulottunga Chola III** gave him the title "**Kavi Chakravarti**" (emperor of poets).

There are different stories about the end of his life. According to one, he settled at **Nattarasankottai**, where his tomb stands and a festival is held every year. Another tale speaks of a quarrel with the king, exile and a sad end. These stories rest on tradition and have no solid historical proof.

## Ramavataram — The Structure of the Epic

The name of Kambar's famous work is **Ramavataram**, but common people call it the **Kamba Ramayanam** (*Kambaramayanam*). It is based on Valmiki's Sanskrit Ramayana, but it is not a translation — it is an independent poetic work steeped in Tamil culture and devotion.

It has **six books**, divided into **113 padalams** (chapters), with a total of about **10,569 verses**. The seventh book, the **Uttara Kanda**, is not considered Kambar's; it is said to be the work of the Tamil poet **Ottakkuttar**. Over the centuries many later verses had been added to the poem; an organisation called the **Kamban Kazhagam**, led by T. P. Meenakshisundaram, separated these out, and its **1976** edition is regarded as the standard today.

Tradition says that the first public recitation of this poem took place in the temple at **Srirangam**, on an island of the Kaveri.

## How It Differs from Valmiki

Kambar took the outline of the story from Valmiki but changed many episodes in his own way:

- **The abduction of Sita:** In Valmiki, Ravana seizes Sita and carries her off, whereas in Kambar, Ravana **does not touch** Sita, but lifts up the piece of earth (in some accounts the whole hut) on which Sita was standing.
- **The divinity of Rama:** Valmiki shows Rama mainly as an ideal human being. Kambar gradually brings him out as **God** — this is believed to be the influence of the devotional tradition of the Tamil **Alvar** saints of that time.
- **The episode of Tara:** In Valmiki, after Vali's death his wife Tara becomes Sugriva's queen, whereas in Kambar Tara remains a widow — this change is thought to fit the Tamil ideal of fidelity.
- **Tamil ideals:** In many episodes the conduct of the characters has been shaped to suit the values of Tamil society, such as loyalty and propriety.

The story of Rama appears in Tamil even before Kambar — in **Sangam** literature, the *Silappadikaram*, the *Manimekalai* and the hymns of the Alvars. But it was Kambar who gave the whole story the form of a grand epic.

## Language and Poetic Beauty

The Kamba Ramayanam is famous for its **variety of metres**, its descriptions of nature and its moving dialogues. Kambar wrote other works too, such as the *Sadagopar Antadi* and the *Saraswati Antadi*.

## Legacy Today

The Kamba Ramayanam is still a living part of Tamil culture. Many families read the whole epic in the month of **Aadi**, it is recited in temples, and the **Sundara Kanda** is the most popular part. It was translated into English by **C. Rajagopalachari** (1961) and by P. S. Sundaram, and has also been translated into Odia.

Valmiki's Sanskrit Ramayana, Tulsidas's Awadhi *Ramcharitmanas* and Kambar's Tamil *Ramavataram* — these three show how the Rama story found its own form in the different languages of India.
