## Who Was Kalhana

**Kalhana** was a Kashmiri Brahmin poet of the twelfth century. His father **Champaka** had been a minister of **Harsha** (reigned 1089–1101 CE), king of the Lohara dynasty of Kashmir. Kalhana had therefore heard of and understood at close quarters court politics, conspiracies and the rise and fall of kings. He was probably born in the early twelfth century, and some scholars link him with Parihasapura.

Kalhana was a worshipper of Shiva — every taranga of the Rajatarangini begins with a praise of Shiva — but respect for Buddhism too is seen in his writing. At the time he wrote this work Kashmir was ruled by King **Jayasimha**; but Kalhana was not his court poet.

## The Composition of the Work

Kalhana began composing the **Rajatarangini** ("River of Kings") in **1148 CE** and completed it in **1149 CE**. It is a poem in **Sanskrit**, divided into eight parts called **tarangas**. It contains thousands of verses.

The contents of the work are roughly as follows:
- **Tarangas 1–3** — ancient and mythical kings, such as the Gonanda dynasty; in these the chronology is weak and the stories are mostly legend.
- **Taranga 4** — the Karkota dynasty, including the story of Lalitaditya Muktapida.
- **Tarangas 5–6** — the Utpala dynasty and the period of Queen Diddā.
- **Tarangas 7–8** — the Lohara dynasty, down to Kalhana's own time; these are the most detailed and reliable parts.

## His Way of Writing History

Kalhana does not merely repeat stories; he tells what sources he used. According to him, he consulted **eleven** dynastic works of earlier scholars and the **Nilamata Purana**. Among them he names some works such as **Kshemendra**'s 'Nripavali' and **Helaraja**'s 'Parthivavali', which are now lost. In addition he examined **inscriptions** on temples and buildings, grant charters, prashastis and coins.

At the start of the work Kalhana says that a true poet-historian should describe the past free of love and hatred, like a judge. He also openly criticised the weaknesses, greed and cruelty of the kings of his time. M. A. Stein pointed out that his writing lacks the excessive praise of patrons often found in Indian court literature.

## How Reliable Is It

Modern historians read the Rajatarangini with care:
- The dates and long reigns of the kings of the early tarangas are not trustworthy, and many kings are not known from any other source.
- In the Karkota period the chronology is better, but descriptions such as Lalitaditya's conquests seem exaggerated.
- The seventh and eighth tarangas, in which Kalhana described his recent past and his own time, are considered the most authentic.

Even so, such a long, chronological and source-based history of a single region is rare in ancient and medieval India.

## The Later Tradition

Kalhana's Rajatarangini began a tradition of history writing in Kashmir. Later **Jonaraja**, his pupil **Shrivara**, and **Prajyabhatta** and **Shuka** carried it forward, so that the history of Kashmir was written down to the end of the sixteenth century (around 1597). In the time of Sultan **Zain-ul-Abidin** it was translated into Persian.

In the modern period the Hungarian-British scholar **M. A. (Aurel) Stein** edited the manuscripts and in 1900 published a detailed English translation, which is still regarded as the standard. Translations have also been made in Hindi, Urdu and many other languages.

## Significance

It is often said that the ancient Indians had no sense of history. The Rajatarangini is the strongest answer to this idea. Kalhana stressed the examination of sources, impartiality and an understanding of cause and effect — qualities still expected of a good historian today. Most of our knowledge about the temples, cities and kings of Kashmir is a gift of this work.
