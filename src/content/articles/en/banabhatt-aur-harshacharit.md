## From Wandering Youth to Court Poet

**Banabhatta** (or Bana) was born in the early 7th century in the village of **Pritikuta**, on the bank of the **Son** river, in a learned Brahmin family. His father's name was **Chitrabhanu** and his mother's name was **Rajadevi**. His mother died in his childhood, and at about **fourteen years** of age his father too passed away. After this the young Bana lived a wandering life for some years — roaming through pilgrimage places, royal courts and centres of learning, keeping company with poets, artists and scholars.

We learn these things not from any outside source but from the early chapters of Bana's own work, the **Harshacharita**, in which he describes his lineage and life. Later, at the call of a relative of Emperor **Harshavardhana** (reigned about **606–647 CE**), he met the emperor, who was then camped at a place called **Manitara**. Bana tells us that Harsha was at first suspicious about his conduct and treated him coldly, but within a few days, impressed by his talent, he honoured him. In this way Bana became Harsha's **court poet**.

## Harshacharita: The First Historical Biography in Sanskrit

The **Harshacharita** ("Deeds of Harsha") is a biography of Harshavardhana written in Sanskrit prose. It is considered the **first historical biography** in Sanskrit and the beginning of historical writing in verse-prose literature. It is divided into **eight ucchvasas** (chapters):

- In the first two ucchvasas Bana describes his lineage, his childhood and his meeting with Harsha.
- After this comes the description of the **Pushyabhuti** (Vardhana) dynasty and Harsha's father **Prabhakaravardhana**, who faced the Hunas.
- After Prabhakaravardhana's death a rain of troubles follows: Devagupta, the king of Malwa, killed **Grahavarman**, the Maukhari king of Kannauj and husband of Harsha's sister **Rajyashri**, and took Rajyashri captive. Harsha's elder brother **Rajyavardhana** conquered Malwa, but **Shashanka**, the king of Gauda (Bengal), killed him by treachery.
- At the age of sixteen Harsha became king. In the last chapter he finds Rajyashri, wandering in the **Vindhya forest**, just at the moment when she was about to enter the fire.

The work ends with Harsha taking the throne and rescuing his sister. Some scholars consider it incomplete, since it does not describe Harsha's later life, his victories and defeats; others believe that Bana deliberately stopped the story at the height of Harsha's rise.

## As a Source of History

The Harshacharita was written in praise of the king, so its picture of Harsha is very bright and many events (such as Shashanka's role) are seen from one side only. The episode of the Vindhya forest is also poetic and is not confirmed by other sources. So historians read it together with the account of the Chinese traveller **Xuanzang** and with inscriptions.

Even so, its value is very great. Bana has described villages, fields, irrigation, army camps, the decoration of the royal palace, wedding preparations, clothes and the everyday life of people so vividly that a glimpse of 7th-century north India is clearly visible. The Harshacharita also contains one of the early clear mentions of **chaturanga** — the ancestor game of chess. In 1897 **Cowell** and **Thomas** published its English translation.

## Kadambari: An Unfinished Novel

Bana's other great work, **Kadambari**, is a fictional love story, counted among the world's early novels. Its story runs in several layers: a **parrot** tells his story to King **Shudraka**, and within that story other characters tell their own tales. The main story is the love of Prince **Chandrapida** and the gandharva maiden **Kadambari**, and alongside it runs the tale of **Mahashveta** and **Pundarika**. The characters return over many births — as humans, gods and animals or birds. Its plot is believed to be taken from the story of King Sumanas in Gunadhya's lost **Brihatkatha**.

Bana died before completing it. His son **Bhushanabhatta** completed it by writing its later part according to his father's plan. So Kadambari is considered to have two parts — the **Purvabhaga** (Bana's) and the **Uttarabhaga** (Bhushanabhatta's).

## Style and Legacy

Bana's prose style is full of long compounds, figures of speech, similes and elaborate descriptions. This style is difficult to read, but in the Sanskrit tradition it was regarded as the peak of prose poetry. The **Chandishataka**, a hymn in praise of Chandi, is also believed to be Bana's, while the play 'Parvatiparinaya' is linked to him but this is doubtful. His works remain a compulsory part of the Sanskrit literature syllabus even today and continue to be the most vivid source for understanding the age of Harsha.
