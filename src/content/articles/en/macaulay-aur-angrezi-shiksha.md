## Background: The One Lakh Rupee Question

The **Charter Act of 1813** directed the East India Company to spend at least one lakh rupees every year on the education and literature of Indians. But it was not clear what kind of education the money should go to. The **General Committee of Public Instruction**, formed in 1823, split into two camps.

- **Orientalists** — such as Henry Thoby Prinsep — wanted traditional education in Sanskrit, Arabic and Persian to be promoted and institutions like the Calcutta Madrasa and the Sanskrit College to continue.
- **Anglicists** — were in favour of teaching Western science and literature through the English medium.

Indians were not outside this debate either. **Raja Ram Mohan Roy** had advocated education in modern science as early as 1823.

## Macaulay's Minute (2 February 1835)

**Thomas Babington Macaulay** came to India in 1834 as the first Law Member of the Governor-General's Council and also became president of the education committee. On **2 February 1835** he wrote his 'Minute on Indian Education'.

In it Macaulay viewed the Indian and Arabic traditions of knowledge with extreme contempt. His famous remark was that **"a single shelf"** of a good European library was worth the whole native literature of India and Arabia. He also set the aim of creating a class that would be **"Indian in blood and colour, but English in taste, in opinions, in morals and in intellect"** and would act as interpreters between the rulers and the people. Behind this was the idea of 'downward filtration' — teach a few at the top, and knowledge will reach the common people by itself.

## The English Education Act, 1835

Governor-General **Lord William Bentinck**, in a short resolution of **7 March 1835**, accepted Macaulay's opinion almost entirely. It was decided that the government funds set aside for education would be spent mainly on teaching European literature and science through the English medium. This decision is generally called the **'English Education Act 1835'**, although technically it was a government resolution. Later Governors-General, especially Lord Auckland, showed some leniency towards Oriental institutions, but the direction had changed.

## Wood's Despatch (1854) and the Universities (1857)

The next big step was the **Wood's Despatch of 1854**, sent by **Charles Wood**, President of the Board of Control. It is often called the 'Magna Carta of English education in India'. Its main proposals were:

- establishment of an education department in every province;
- government aid (grant-in-aid) to private schools;
- use of vernacular languages at the primary level and English at the higher level;
- universities on the model of the University of London.

As a result the **universities of Calcutta, Bombay and Madras** were established in **1857** — the same year that the great revolt broke out in North India.

## Results and Debate

English education created a new educated middle class — lawyers, teachers, journalists and government employees. The irony was that leaders like Dadabhai Naoroji, Gokhale and Tilak later emerged from this very class and criticised British rule with the help of its own language and ideas. Modern ideas of science, democracy and equality also spread by this route.

On the other hand, critics believe that this policy pushed Indian languages and the traditional system of knowledge to the margins, created a deep gulf between the educated and the uneducated, and neglected mass education — the theory of 'downward filtration' did not succeed in practice. The word 'Macaulayism' is still used today to mean a colonial mindset. At the same time, some Dalit thinkers also regard English education as a means of freedom from caste-based barriers to education. In this way Macaulay's Minute remains one of the most controversial documents in Indian history.
