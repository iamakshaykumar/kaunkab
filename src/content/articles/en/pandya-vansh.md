## The Earliest Mentions

The Pandyas, along with the **Cheras** and **Cholas**, were one of the three ancient dynasties of Tamilakam. Their region was present-day southern Tamil Nadu — around Madurai, Tirunelveli and Ramanathapuram. Mentions of the Pandyas are very old:

- The Greek ambassador **Megasthenes** of the 4th century BCE spoke of the kingdom of a queen named "Pandaia" in the south, which many scholars take to be the Pandya kingdom;
- In the 3rd century BCE, **Ashoka's** Second and Thirteenth Rock Edicts describe the Pandyas, along with the Cholas and Keralaputras, as neighbours outside the borders of the Mauryan empire;
- The Hathigumpha inscription of **Kharavela**, king of Kalinga, claims that pearls were brought from the Pandya kingdom.

## The Pandyas of the Sangam Age

Sangam poems describe the Pandya kings as patrons of the poets' assemblies of Madurai. The famous king **Nedunjeliyan** defeated the combined army of the Cheras, Cholas and several chieftains in the battle of **Talaiyalanganam** — the poem *Maduraikkanci* is in praise of this very king. In the epic *Silappadikaram* the Pandya banner is described as the **fish (meen)**, and it contains the story of Kannagi burning Madurai, which is literary, not historical.

A major basis of Pandya prosperity was **maritime trade**. The port of **Korkai**, situated at the mouth of the **Tamiraparani** river, was famous for pearl fishing. Roman coins have been found in excavations in the Pandya region, which are evidence of trade with Rome.

## The Kalabhra Period and Revival

After the Sangam age, roughly between the 3rd and 6th centuries, the Tamil country was dominated by rulers called the **Kalabhras**, about whom very little is known. In the latter half of the 6th century King **Kadungon** defeated the Kalabhras and re-established Pandya power — this is recorded in the 8th-century **Velvikudi copper plates**. Scholars differ slightly on the date of Kadungon's rule.

After this the Pandyas had a long struggle with the **Pallavas** of Kanchi. **Arikesari Maravarman** (about 640–670 CE) was the main king of this period. According to tradition, under the influence of the Shaiva saint **Tirugnana Sambandar** a Pandya king gave up Jainism and adopted Shaivism, but the details of this story come from later religious texts. In about the 8th century, under Pandya patronage, the unfinished rock-cut **Vettuvan Koil** temple was built at **Kalugumalai**.

## Under the Cholas

At the end of the 9th century Chola power grew rapidly. In about 910 CE the Chola king **Parantaka I** took Madurai and assumed the title "Maduraikonda" (conqueror of Madurai). In the time of Rajaraja I and Rajendra I the Pandya region became fully a part of the Chola empire, and Chola princes continued to rule there as viceroys.

## The Golden Age of the 13th Century

As the Cholas weakened, the Pandyas made a strong comeback in the 13th century. **Jatavarman Sundara Pandya I** (from about 1251) extended his power north to Nellore, and to Kerala and northern Sri Lanka. **Maravarman Kulasekhara I** defeated the Hoysala-Chola alliance. At this time the Venetian traveller **Marco Polo** visited the Pandya coast in 1288 and 1293 and described its pearls and the king's jewellery.

## Decline and Legacy

After Kulasekhara's death a civil war broke out over the succession. Meanwhile in 1310–11 **Malik Kafur**, the general of Alauddin Khalji, invaded the south and plundered as far as Madurai. In the 1330s a separate sultanate came into being at Madurai and the main Pandya power came to an end; their minor branches continued for some time afterwards in the southern areas.

Over a history of more than fifteen hundred years, the Pandyas deeply influenced Tamil literature, temple architecture and maritime trade. Madurai is still remembered today as the "city of the Pandyas".
