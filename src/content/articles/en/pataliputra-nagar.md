## From Pataligrama to Pataliputra

In the 5th century BCE King **Ajatashatru** of Magadha built a small **fort** on the bank of the Ganga near a village called **Pataligrama**. According to tradition this fort was built to face the **Vajji confederacy** (the Licchavis) who lived to the north of the Ganga. By the traditional chronology its date is taken as about **490 BCE**, although many historians place this whole chronology of Magadha a few decades later.

The Buddhist text *Mahaparinibbana Sutta* says that in the last days of his life the Buddha passed through here while this fort was being built. In this text the Buddha is made to prophesy that this place would later become a great city — this can also be considered a tradition added later.

## Becoming the Capital

According to Jain and Puranic traditions, Ajatashatru's successor **Udayin** (Udayabhadra) founded the city near the confluence of the Ganga and **Son** rivers and brought the capital of Magadha here from **Rajagriha**. Surrounded by rivers, this place was suitable for both defence and trade.

After this, for about a thousand years Pataliputra remained the centre of the politics of North India. The **Nanda**, **Maurya** and later **Gupta** kings made it their capital. The Greeks called it **Palibothra**.

## Through the Eyes of Megasthenes

After about 303 BCE, **Megasthenes**, the ambassador of Seleucus, came to the court of Chandragupta Maurya. His original work *Indica* is lost, but his description survives in the quotations of later writers:
- The city was in the shape of a parallelogram — about **80 stadia** long and **15 stadia** wide (roughly 15 × 2.75 kilometres).
- The city was surrounded by a **wooden rampart** with holes made for shooting arrows.
- According to Arrian the rampart had **570 towers** and **64 gates**.
- Outside the rampart was a **moat** about 600 feet wide and 30 cubits deep.

By the estimate of modern scholars the population here in Ashoka's time must have been between about 150,000 and 400,000, which makes it one of the largest cities in the world at that time.

## The Excavations at Kumhrar and Bulandibagh

In 1892 **Laurence Waddell** identified ancient Pataliputra beneath modern Patna. Later, excavation at **Bulandibagh** brought to light remains of a thick wooden rampart, which match Megasthenes' description.

The most famous discovery is that of **Kumhrar**. In 1912–13 the American archaeologist **D. B. Spooner** found here the remains of a pillar of brightly polished stone and the pits of 72 pillars. In 1951–55 the excavation of the K. P. Jayaswal Research Institute (A. S. Altekar and V. Mishra) found 8 more pits. In this way it came to be called the **"80-pillared hall"** — the pillars stood in 8 rows of 10, at a distance of about 4.57 metres. Each pillar was about 9.75 metres high. Some scholars see the influence of the Achaemenid palaces of Iran in its design. Remains of an **Arogya Vihara** (a healing centre) have also been found at Kumhrar.

## Decline and Patna

Pataliputra was prosperous in the Gupta period too — in about 400 CE **Faxian** praised its hospitals and grand buildings. But after the 6th century its decline began; among the reasons given are changes in the course of the Ganga, floods and the Huna invasions. Around 637 CE **Xuanzang** found it almost deserted.

In the 16th century **Sher Shah Suri** founded a city here again, which later became modern **Patna**. Today the remains of ancient Pataliputra lie buried beneath this modern city, so large-scale excavation here is difficult.
