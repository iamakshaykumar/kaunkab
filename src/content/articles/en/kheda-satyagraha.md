## Background: Gujarat After Champaran

After the success at Champaran in 1917, Gandhi returned to Ahmedabad, where his ashram was. In early 1918 two separate crises in Gujarat came before him — that of the textile mill workers in the city and that of the farmers in the nearby district of Kheda. In both he adopted the method of satyagraha.

## The Ahmedabad Mill Strike

During the plague days the mill owners had given a "plague bonus" to keep the workers from leaving, which they now wanted to stop. The workers, troubled by wartime price rises, were demanding a permanent wage increase instead. The social worker **Anasuya Sarabhai** brought their case to Gandhi, while the chief of the owners was her brother **Ambalal Sarabhai**.

After examining the conditions, Gandhi considered the demand for a **35 percent** increase reasonable; the owners stood firm at 20 percent. Around 22 February 1918 the owners declared a lockout and the workers remained on strike. Gandhi held meetings every day on the bank of the Sabarmati and asked the workers to remain non-violent and stand by their pledge. When the strike began to weaken, Gandhi began a fast on **15 March 1918** — this is considered his first public fast in India. Three days later, on 18 March, the owners agreed to arbitration, and by the arbitrator's decision the workers got a 35 percent increase.

Gandhi himself later admitted that the fast also put moral pressure on the owners, so it cannot be called a wholly pure satyagraha.

## Kheda: Crops Ruined, Yet Revenue Demanded

In 1917, because of excessive rain and an infestation of rats, the crop in Kheda was very poor. According to the revenue rules, if the yield was **less than one-fourth (four annas)** of normal, that year's land revenue should have been remitted. The farmers said that this was exactly the situation, but the officials, overestimating the yield, insisted on collecting the full revenue. Petitions went up to the Governor of Bombay but were of no use.

On **22 March 1918**, from Nadiad, Gandhi told the farmers not to pay the revenue. Thousands of farmers took a pledge that they would not pay that year's revenue and would bear the hardship of confiscation. Organisation work in village after village was handled by workers such as the young lawyer **Vallabhbhai Patel**, **Indulal Yagnik**, Shankarlal Banker, Mahadev Desai and Mohanlal Pandya.

## Confiscation and the Settlement

The government confiscated bullocks, utensils, household goods and, in some villages, even the standing crop. **Mohanlal Pandya** got the name "onion thief" for pulling up an onion crop from a confiscated field, which raised the farmers' morale.

In early June 1918 a middle path was found: the government indicated that only those farmers who could afford it should pay the revenue, and that collection from poor farmers would be deferred. On 3 June at Uttarsanda the mamlatdar accepted this, and then the Collector applied the same condition to the whole district. Gandhi did not regard the result as a complete victory, but the movement ended with honour.

## Significance

Champaran, Ahmedabad and Kheda — these three made Gandhi a trusted leader among farmers and workers and tested satyagraha in Indian conditions. Kheda turned **Vallabhbhai Patel** from his legal practice towards public life; ten years later this same experience served in the Bardoli Satyagraha. The very next year, 1919, Gandhi launched the first all-India satyagraha against the Rowlatt Act.
