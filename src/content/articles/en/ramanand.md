## The Puzzle of the Dates

The dates of Ramananda's life are one of the tangled puzzles of Indian history. Different sources give different periods:

- Tradition often gives dates like **1299–1410** (The Sikh Encyclopedia writes "1300–1410?", with a question mark);
- the *Encyclopaedia of Indian Literature* gives **1366–1467**;
- many modern scholars place him at **about 1400–1475**.

So the safe thing to say is that Ramananda was active between the **end of the 14th century and the 15th century** — that is, in the later part of the Delhi Sultanate.

## From Prayag to Kashi

According to tradition Ramananda was born at **Prayag** (today's Prayagraj) in a Kanyakubja Brahmin family. He came to **Kashi** to study and spent a large part of his life there. According to the *Bhaktamal* his guru was **Raghavananda**, who is linked with the Sri Vaishnava tradition of Ramanujacharya.

This connection too is not entirely straightforward. Tradition links Ramananda with Ramanuja's Vishishtadvaita, but many scholars also see the influence of **Nathpanthi yoga** in his thought. Later the Ramanandi sect itself gave itself an identity distinct from the Sri Vaishnava tradition of the south.

## The Language of the People and Devotion for All

Ramananda's greatest contribution was that he opened up bhakti **to all**. He preached in the **language of the people, Hindi**, instead of Sanskrit, so that those who did not know Sanskrit — that is, most of the people — could also understand religion. He stressed devotion to **Rama and Sita**.

Among his disciples were people of different castes and backgrounds, and women too. For this reason he is often called the **bridge between the bhakti movements of the south and the north** — there is even a saying: "Bhakti Dravid upji, laye Ramanand" (Bhakti arose in the Dravida land; Ramananda brought it north). Modern scholars regard only two works — the *Vaishnava Matabja Bhaskara* and the *Ramarchana Paddhati* (in some sources 'Ramachand Paddhati') — as his authentic compositions; many other works were later attached to his name.

## The Line of Disciples

Nabhadas's *Bhaktamal* lists **fourteen** chief disciples of Ramananda — twelve men and two women. The main ones are:

- **Kabir** — a weaver of Kashi, the most famous poet of nirguna bhakti;
- **Raidas** (Ravidas) — a saint-poet from a leatherworker family;
- **Pipa** — a Rajput king who gave up his kingdom and took to devotion;
- **Sain** — a devotee from a barber family;
- **Dhanna** — a Jat farmer of Rajasthan;
- **Anantananda**, **Sursurananda**, **Narharidas** and others;
- the women disciples **Sursuri** and **Padmavati**.

## Was Kabir Really His Disciple?

It is this list of disciples that is the subject of the biggest debate among historians. If Ramananda died around 1410, it seems difficult for saints like Kabir and Raidas, who were active in the 15th century, to have been his direct disciples. The Sikh Encyclopedia too accepts this tradition "despite chronological impossibilities". Some modern scholars believe that the Ramanandi sect later linked these popular saints to its own tradition; other scholars defend the tradition on historical grounds.

## Legacy

Ramananda is regarded as the originator of the **sant tradition** of north India. The **Ramanandi sect** (Ramavat) that took his name is today one of the largest Vaishnava ascetic communities of India, whose Bairagi sadhus spread devotion to Rama especially in the middle Gangetic plain. The Sikhs' **Guru Granth Sahib** (page 1195) also includes a hymn of his, which speaks not of idol worship but of the all-pervading Brahman, revealed through the 'shabad' of the true guru. This shows that his influence was not limited to the Vaishnava sect but reached the nirguna saints and later the Sikh tradition. Even if his dates and the links with his disciples are disputed, it is undisputed that Ramananda opened the way for taking bhakti beyond the limits of temple and caste.
