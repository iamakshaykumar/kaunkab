## What Are the Jatakas

The word 'Jataka' means 'a story connected with birth'. Buddhist tradition holds that before becoming the Buddha, **Siddhartha Gautama** was born countless times, and in each birth practised some virtue — generosity, moral conduct, patience, truth, compassion. A being moving towards Buddhahood in this way is called a **Bodhisattva**. The Jataka tales are the stories of these previous births.

In these tales the Bodhisattva appears sometimes as a **deer**, sometimes as the **king of the monkeys**, an **elephant** or a **hare**, and sometimes as a king, merchant, Brahmin or ordinary labourer. So the Jatakas show the whole life of ancient India — forests, rivers, markets, royal courts and villages. Historians also treat them as one source for understanding the trade, society and customs of the time.

## Jatakas in the Pali Tipitaka

In the **Pali Tipitaka**, the scriptural collection of Theravada Buddhism, the Jatakas form part of the **Khuddaka Nikaya** of the **Sutta Pitaka**. The collection counts **547** Jatakas. The part originally regarded as canonical is their **gathas** (verses); the prose part of the stories is found in the commentary **Jatakatthavannana**, compiled around the **5th century CE**. According to scholars, the tales themselves took shape and were added gradually between about **300 BCE and 400 CE**; some scholars consider the verse portion even older.

Most Jatakas share the same structure:

- **The story of the present** — some event in the Buddha's time, on which the Buddha tells an old story.
- **The story of the past** — the actual story of one of the Bodhisattva's previous births, with gathas in between.
- **Samodhana (the connection)** — at the end the Buddha explains which character in the old story is who today, for example, "The deer of that time was I myself."

The Jataka tradition also continued in Sanskrit. The **Jatakamala** of the poet **Aryashura**, of about the **4th century CE**, retells 34 Jatakas in a refined poetic style.

## Some Famous Tales

- **Vessantara Jataka** — Prince Vessantara, following the ideal of generosity, gives away everything, even his own family. It is the longest tale in the collection, and still the most recited and performed in Theravada countries.
- **Ruru Jataka** — The golden deer (the Bodhisattva) saves a man drowning in a river, but the same man, greedy for a reward, reveals the deer's whereabouts to the king. In the end the deer's truthfulness changes the king's heart.
- **Mahakapi Jataka** — The king of the monkeys makes his own body a bridge to carry the whole troop across a river.
- **Shasha Jataka** — As a hare, the Bodhisattva is ready to offer himself for a hungry guest.
- **Chhaddanta Jataka** — The six-tusked elephant who forgives his own hunter.
- **Mahajanaka Jataka** — The story of a prince who does not lose courage even after being shipwrecked at sea.

## Jatakas on Stone and Walls

The Jatakas were not only read and heard; they were also **seen**. Around the **2nd–1st century BCE**, Jataka scenes were carved on the railings and gateways of the stupas of **Bharhut** and **Sanchi** in Madhya Pradesh. These are among the oldest known depictions of the tales. The round medallions of Bharhut often show several moments of a story together in a single frame — as in the Ruru Jataka in this article's picture, where the deer saves the man below and the hunter and king appear above. Many scenes also carry the name of the story inscribed in Brahmi script. On the gateways of Sanchi are scenes such as the **Chhaddanta**, **Vessantara** and **Sama (Shyama) Jataka**.

A few centuries later, around the **5th century CE**, the Jatakas came alive in colour on the walls of the **Ajanta** caves in Maharashtra. The paintings of the **Mahajanaka** and **Champeyya Jataka** in Cave 1 are famous, and many Jataka tales are painted in Cave 17. Some Ajanta paintings are also accompanied by quotations from Aryashura's Jatakamala. Outside India, at the 9th-century temple of **Borobudur** in Java (Indonesia), all 34 tales of the Jatakamala are carved in stone; Jataka paintings are also common in the temples of Sri Lanka, Myanmar and Thailand.

## A Journey Around the World and a Legacy

Many Jataka stories resemble those of other Indian story traditions, such as the **Panchatantra**, and some also resemble Aesop's fables; but who borrowed from whom is a matter of debate among scholars. One Bodhisattva story, after reaching Arabic and then European languages, became the medieval Christian tale of **'Barlaam and Josaphat'**.

At the end of the 19th century, an English translation of the entire Pali Jataka collection was published in six volumes (1895–1907) under the editorship of **E. B. Cowell**, bringing these tales to modern readers. Even today, the Vessantara Jataka is recited and staged in countries such as Sri Lanka, Thailand and Myanmar, and in India, from children's books to museums, the Jataka tales teach that **compassion and sacrifice** are the fruit not of a single birth, but of continuous practice.
