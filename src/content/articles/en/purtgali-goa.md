## Background: The Portuguese Claim on the Sea

After **Vasco da Gama** reached Calicut in 1498, Portugal began trying to seize control of the spice trade of the Indian Ocean. In 1505 **Francisco de Almeida** came as the first viceroy, and the Portuguese outposts in Asia were joined together as the **Estado da India** ("State of India"). At first its centre was **Cochin**.

Almeida relied mainly on the navy, but his successor **Afonso de Albuquerque** (governor, 1509–1515) believed that Portugal needed a few strong, permanent bases in Asia — where ships could be built and repaired and soldiers could be stationed.

## Why Goa

The **island of Goa**, between the Mandovi and Zuari rivers, was at that time a rich port of the Adil Shahi Sultanate of **Bijapur**. From here came the trade in **war horses** from Persia and Arabia, which both the Deccan sultanates and Vijayanagara needed. Being surrounded by rivers, it was also easy to defend.

## Two Conquests: February and November 1510

With the help of the local chief **Timoji**, Albuquerque's fleet entered the Mandovi river in February 1510 and on 17 February took Goa almost without a fight. But the Sultan of Bijapur, **Ismail Adil Shah**, soon sent a large army. In May 1510 this army crossed the river onto the island, and on 31 May the Portuguese had to leave the city. Because of the monsoon they remained stuck at the river mouth for months.

After receiving additional ships and soldiers, Albuquerque returned and on **25 November 1510** attacked from three sides and won the city again. After this the city was plundered for several days and the Muslim residents who remained there were massacred — this was the cruel side of the conquest, which even Portuguese historians record. Over the following year Bijapur tried to take Goa back, but did not succeed.

## The Estado da India and the Cartaz

Albuquerque went on to conquer Malacca in 1511 and Hormuz in 1515, which gave the Portuguese a hold on the main sea routes of the Indian Ocean. In **1530** the viceroy's court moved from Cochin to Goa, and Goa became the capital of the Estado da India.

The main weapon of Portuguese power was the **cartaz** — a sea permit in force from 1502. Indian, Arab and other merchant ships had to take this pass from Portuguese officers and pay duty at Portuguese bases such as Goa, Malacca or Hormuz. A ship caught without a cartaz could have its cargo confiscated and could even be sunk. Every year in the monsoon season Portuguese fleets patrolled the coast to check this.

## Rule, Society and Religion

In the early years Albuquerque let the Hindu residents live by their own customs, banned the practice of **sati**, and encouraged Portuguese soldiers to marry local women by giving them land and dowries. But from the middle of the sixteenth century the policy became harsh. Christian missionary work intensified, many temples were demolished, and in **1560** the **Inquisition** (a religious court) was established in Goa, which imposed severe restrictions on Hindus, Muslims, Jews and New Christians.

In this period Old Goa filled up with grand churches and convents and came to be called the "Rome of the East". Because of disease, the capital was moved to Panjim (Nova Goa) in 1843.

## Legacy

The conquest of Goa was the first permanent territorial rule of a European power in India. Later the Dutch, English and French companies broke the Portuguese monopoly of the sea, but Portuguese rule over Goa, Daman and Diu lasted until **1961**, when India liberated them. Goa's architecture, food, music and mixed Konkani-Portuguese culture still bear witness to this history of four and a half centuries.
