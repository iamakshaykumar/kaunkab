## The Crisis in East Pakistan

Pakistan, created in 1947, was divided into two parts, with about 1,600 kilometres of Indian territory between them. East Pakistan had the larger population, but political and military power was dominated by the western part, and there had long been discontent over language and economic discrimination. In the general election of December 1970, **Sheikh Mujibur Rahman's** **Awami League** won a majority in the National Assembly, but the military government of President **Yahya Khan** put off the transfer of power.

## The Military Crackdown and the Refugee Crisis

On the night of **25 March 1971**, the Pakistani army began a wide military crackdown in East Pakistan under the name "Operation Searchlight", and Mujib was arrested. The next day, 26 March, Bangladesh's independence was declared. Estimates of the number of civilians killed in the months of violence that followed vary greatly — from about **300,000 to 3 million** — and it remains a subject of sharp dispute even today; Pakistan's official figures are much lower. Accounts of large-scale sexual violence are also recorded.

To escape the violence, about ten million refugees reached West Bengal, Tripura, Assam and Meghalaya, placing a heavy economic and social burden on India. Prime Minister **Indira Gandhi** drew the attention of the world to this crisis. On 9 August 1971 India signed a Treaty of Peace, Friendship and Cooperation with the Soviet Union. India gave training and support to the Bangladeshi freedom fighters — the **Mukti Bahini**; Pakistan called this interference in its internal affairs.

## The War: 3–16 December 1971

On the evening of **3 December 1971**, the Pakistan Air Force attacked several of India's western airfields. After this India declared war and fighting began on both the eastern and western fronts. On 6 December India recognised Bangladesh as an independent country.

In the east, the joint command of the Indian Army and the Mukti Bahini advanced rapidly toward Dhaka from several directions; the Indian Navy blockaded the Bay of Bengal and also attacked Karachi port. The United States gave diplomatic support to Pakistan and sent its aircraft carrier toward the Bay of Bengal, while the Soviet Union used its veto in the Security Council in India's favour.

## The Surrender in Dhaka

On **16 December 1971**, at the Ramna Race Course ground in Dhaka, the chief of Pakistan's Eastern Command, Lieutenant General **A. A. K. Niazi**, signed the instrument of surrender before the chief of India's Eastern Command, Lieutenant General **Jagjit Singh Aurora**. About 93,000 Pakistani soldiers and personnel became prisoners of war, and East Pakistan became **Bangladesh**. The next day, 17 December, India declared a unilateral ceasefire on the western front.

Estimates of losses differ by source: neutral sources estimate about 2,500 to 3,800 Indian and about 9,000 Pakistani soldiers killed. In Pakistan, the causes of the defeat were investigated by the **Hamoodur Rahman Commission**, whose report was made partly public decades later.

## The Shimla Agreement (1972)

On **2 July 1972** at Shimla, Indira Gandhi and Pakistan's President **Zulfikar Ali Bhutto** signed the Shimla Agreement, which came into force on 4 August 1972. The two countries pledged to settle differences peacefully through bilateral talks; the ceasefire line of 17 December 1971 in Jammu and Kashmir became the **Line of Control**, which neither side may change unilaterally; and India returned most of the territory it had won on the western front. The return of the prisoners of war took place after the Delhi Agreement of 1973, and in February 1974 Pakistan recognised Bangladesh.

## Significance

The 1971 war was an event that changed the map of South Asia: a new country, Bangladesh, was born. In India 16 December is celebrated as 'Vijay Diwas' and in Bangladesh too this day is 'Bijoy Dibos'; in Pakistan it is remembered as the 'Fall of Dhaka'.
