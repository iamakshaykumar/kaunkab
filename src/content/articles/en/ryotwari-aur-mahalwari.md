## Background: After the Permanent Settlement

In **1793** Lord Cornwallis introduced the **Permanent Settlement** in Bengal, Bihar and Orissa, under which zamindars were treated as owners of the land and a fixed revenue was settled with them for ever. As Company rule spread over south, west and north India, officials felt this model did not suit every place. In many areas there were no big zamindars like those of Bengal, and the Company also did not want the gains of expanding cultivation to go to intermediaries instead of itself. From this thinking came two new systems: **Ryotwari** and **Mahalwari**.

## Ryotwari: A Direct Settlement with the Cultivator

After the Treaty of Seringapatam in **1792**, the Company obtained the **Baramahal** region from Mysore. Here **Captain Alexander Read** and his assistant **Thomas Munro** carried out a new experiment in the 1790s: the land was surveyed, every field was assessed and the revenue was fixed directly with the **ryot** (cultivator). Munro later adopted the same method in the "Ceded Districts" of Andhra as well.

Munro became **Governor of Madras** in **1820** and held the post until his death in **1827**. During this period Ryotwari became the main system over a large part of the Madras Presidency. Later it was extended to the **Bombay Presidency** as well, in the time of Mountstuart Elphinstone and then with the surveys of the 1830s. Later on, a similar system was introduced in parts of Assam and Burma too.

Its main features were:

- Revenue was settled **directly** between the government and the cultivator; there was no zamindar as intermediary.
- As long as the cultivator paid the revenue, he could not be evicted from the land; he could sell or mortgage it.
- The revenue was **not permanent**; it was revised from time to time (usually every 20 to 30 years).
- Revenue had to be paid in **cash**.

At first the state's demand was up to about **half** of the produce, which Munro tried to reduce gradually to about **one-third**. Even so, historians consider it heavy.

## Mahalwari: Collective Responsibility of the Village

In the **Ganga-Yamuna Doab** of north India and nearby areas, which later came to be called the **North-Western Provinces**, there was a strong tradition of collective landholding in villages. Here the Company official **Holt Mackenzie** put forward a plan in **1819**, which was given legal form through **Regulation VII of 1822**. In it the unit of revenue was the **"mahal"**, that is, a village or a group of villages. The village headman or **lambardar** paid the revenue on behalf of the whole mahal, and the village co-sharers were jointly responsible for it.

Under the 1822 arrangement the state's demand was set very high: in some cases up to about **95 per cent** of the rental. This proved hard to enforce and many villages sank into arrears.

In **1833**, in the time of Governor-General **Lord William Bentinck**, the system was reformed through a new regulation. **Robert Merttins Bird** devised a method of assessment based on field maps and surveys, and with the state demand fixed at about **two-thirds (66 per cent)** of the rental, a **30-year** settlement was made. Later **James Thomason** carried it forward. This demand too was considered heavy, so the **Saharanpur Rules** of **1855** reduced it to **50 per cent**. The system was also adopted in Punjab and parts of Central India.

## Comparison of the Two Systems and Their Impact

The difference between the three systems (the Permanent Settlement, Ryotwari and Mahalwari) lay in who was responsible for the revenue: the zamindar, the individual cultivator or the whole village. In both Ryotwari and Mahalwari the revenue was **temporary** and could be raised from time to time, so that the gains of expanding cultivation reached the government directly.

In practice the results of both were hard on the peasants:

- Because of high revenue rates payable in **cash**, peasants became dependent on **moneylenders** in years of bad harvest.
- Land became a commodity that could be sold and mortgaged, so that on failure to repay debts land began passing into the hands of moneylenders and traders.
- Complaints of arbitrariness by government staff and revenue officials were common; critics said that under Ryotwari the government itself became the "biggest zamindar" in place of the zamindar.
- In Mahalwari areas, arrears and auctions of land increased discontent among old landholding families, which many historians regard as one reason for rural participation in north India in the **Revolt of 1857**.

In the Deccan region of Bombay, the tension between peasants and moneylenders surfaced as the **Deccan Riots of 1875**.

## Legacy

Ryotwari and Mahalwari laid the foundation over a large part of India for the land records, surveys, khasra-khatauni and revenue administration that continued in some form even after independence. By rough estimates the largest share of the cultivable land of British India was under Ryotwari. In independent India, the **abolition of zamindari** and land reform laws changed these colonial systems, but the questions of revenue, debt and land rights that they raised remained an important part of Indian agrarian history.
