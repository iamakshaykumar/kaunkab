## The Rise of the Gahadavalas

At the end of the eleventh century the politics of north India was fragmented. The Pratihara empire had already broken up and the grip of the Kalachuris was weakening. In this vacuum **Chandradeva** (c. 1089–1103) laid the foundation of the **Gahadavala** kingdom. The dynasty is held to have had two capitals, **Varanasi** and **Kannauj**. The inscriptions suggest that Varanasi was their preferred capital, while Kannauj, which for centuries had been a symbol of prestige in north India, was something like a 'capital of honour'.

## The Main Rulers

- **Chandradeva** (c. 1089–1103): the founder
- **Madanapala** (c. 1104–1113)
- **Govindachandra** (c. 1114–1155): the most powerful ruler
- **Vijayachandra** (c. 1155–1169)
- **Jayachandra** (c. 1170–1194): the last great ruler
- **Harishchandra** (c. 1194–1197): in a limited area after Chandawar

These dates are **approximate**, based on inscriptions.

## The Golden Age of Govindachandra

In **Govindachandra**'s rule of about forty years the Gahadavala kingdom reached its peak. He faced the **Ghaznavid** invasions coming from the west and also fought the **Pala** rulers in the east. A large number of grants (copper plates) issued in his name have been found, most of them from the Varanasi region.

At his court **Lakshmidhara** composed the **'Krityakalpataru'**. His queen **Kumaradevi**, who came from a Buddhist family of Bodh Gaya, built the **Dharmachakra-Jina-Vihara** at **Sarnath**; it is mentioned in her inscription found at Sarnath. A Hindu king's queen building a Buddhist vihara shows the religious tolerance of the time.

## Turushkadanda: A Mysterious Tax

A tax named **'Turushkadanda'** occurs repeatedly in the Gahadavala grants. 'Turushka' means Turk. Historians are **not unanimous** about its purpose: in one view it was a tax to raise the tribute paid to the Ghaznavids, in another the cost of fighting the Turks. The older view that it was a tax imposed on Muslims is now considered less acceptable.

## Jayachandra and the Battle of Chandawar

In **Jayachandra**'s time the power of **Muhammad Ghori** was rising rapidly from the north-west. After Prithviraj Chauhan's defeat in the Second Battle of Tarain in 1192, the way to the Ganga valley was open. In **1194**, at **Chandawar** (near today's Firozabad) on the Yamuna, the army of Ghori, among whose commanders was **Qutb-ud-din Aibak**, and the army of Jayachandra faced each other. The fight was fierce, but after Jayachandra was killed his army scattered. According to the contemporary writer Hasan Nizami, Varanasi was plundered after this.

**Legend versus history:** The 'Prithviraj Raso' says that Prithviraj carried off Jayachandra's daughter **Samyogita** from her swayamvara and that out of this enmity Jayachandra sided with Ghori. According to historians there is no solid historical evidence in favour of this story; it is a creation of later poetry. From this very story the word 'Jaychand' came into popular speech meaning 'traitor', which is not considered historically fair.

## Significance

The Gahadavala dynasty symbolises the last great native power of the Ganga valley, which stood just before the establishment of the Delhi Sultanate. After the defeat at Chandawar the region of Varanasi and Kannauj passed under Ghori and then the sultans of Delhi. Their grants, coins and the inscription at Sarnath give valuable information on the administration, tax system and religious life of that period.
