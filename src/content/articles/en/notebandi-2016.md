## The Announcement

At about eight o'clock on the night of **8 November 2016**, Prime Minister **Narendra Modi**, in a televised address to the nation, announced that from **12 midnight** that same night the Mahatma Gandhi Series notes of **500 and 1000 rupees** would cease to be legal tender. People were given until **30 December 2016** to deposit or exchange these notes at banks and post offices. At the same time the issue of a new **2000 rupee** note and a newly designed **500 rupee** note was announced.

Preparations for this decision were kept secret. At that time **Urjit Patel** was Governor of the Reserve Bank of India and **Arun Jaitley** was Finance Minister. According to the government, the decision was taken after a recommendation by the Reserve Bank's central board.

## The Government's Stated Aims

The government gave three main aims for demonetisation:

- **Black money**: bringing out unaccounted wealth hidden in the form of cash.
- **Counterfeit notes**: ending the circulation of fake notes.
- **Financing of terrorism and Naxalism**: striking at the cash used in these activities.

In the following weeks the government also described promoting a **less-cash and digital economy** and widening the tax base as major goals.

## The Cash Crunch and Everyday Life

About **86 per cent** of the value of all currency then in circulation was in these two notes, worth about **15.41 lakh crore rupees**. Printing new notes and recalibrating ATMs for them took time, so for many weeks **long queues outside banks and ATMs** persisted across the country. Limits on withdrawals and note exchange were changed repeatedly.

Small traders, farmers, daily-wage labourers and the informal sector, all dependent on cash, were the worst affected. Media reports also carried news of deaths of people standing in queues, although the government did not officially confirm such figures. The government and its supporters said that this short-term hardship was necessary for long-term benefits.

## RBI Data and the Economic Debate

In **August 2018** the Reserve Bank's annual report stated that about **15.31 lakh crore rupees** of the demonetised notes, or about **99.3 per cent**, had returned to the banks. Critics said this showed that black money held as cash was far less than expected, or that it too had been absorbed into the banking system. The government's position was that money coming into banks created an accounting trail, made it possible to investigate suspicious deposits, and increased the number of people filing income tax returns.

Former Prime Minister and economist **Manmohan Singh** called it "organised loot and legalised plunder" in the Rajya Sabha and warned of a fall of about two percentage points in GDP. Many economists, including **Amartya Sen**, criticised it, while some industrialists and commentators supported it at first. Economic growth slowed in the quarters of 2016–17; economists disagree on how much of this was due to demonetisation. On the other hand, after demonetisation the use of digital payments, especially **UPI**, rose sharply, although after some time the use of cash also grew again, rising above its earlier level.

## The Supreme Court Verdict

On **2 January 2023** a five-judge Constitution Bench of the Supreme Court delivered its verdict on several petitions challenging the legality of demonetisation. By a **4:1 majority** the court upheld the decision. The majority judgment written by Justice **B. R. Gavai** said there was no flaw in the decision-making process, since there had been consultation between the government and the Reserve Bank, and that there was a reasonable nexus between the measure and its objectives.

Dissenting, Justice **B. V. Nagarathna** said that demonetisation on such a large scale should have been done through a law of Parliament, not through a notification, and held it "unlawful"; but she too said that the earlier position could not now be restored. The court made clear that it was examining only the legal validity of the policy, not its economic consequences.

## Legacy

Demonetisation is counted among the biggest and most debated economic decisions of independent India. Its supporters see it as a bold step against corruption and a spur towards digital payments, while critics regard it as a step with a heavy social and economic cost whose declared aims were not achieved. Research and political debate on its effects continue to this day.
