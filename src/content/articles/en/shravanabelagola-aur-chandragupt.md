## A town between two hills

In Hassan district of Karnataka, **Shravanabelagola** lies between two hills: **Chandragiri** (the smaller hill) and **Vindhyagiri** or Indragiri (the larger hill). The name is usually understood to mean "the white pond of the shramanas", pointing to the pond in the middle of the town. The place is the centre of two different stories: a **tradition** that links it with the Mauryan emperor Chandragupta, and a **documented history** connected with the huge 10th-century Gommateshwara statue.

## Tradition: Bhadrabahu and Chandragupta

According to **Digambara Jain tradition**, the acharya **Bhadrabahu**, regarded as the last "shrutakevali", that is, the knower of all the Jain scriptures, predicted a **twelve-year famine** in North India. He set out towards the south with thousands of disciples. The tale says that **Chandragupta Maurya** gave up his kingdom, took monastic initiation and came along with them. Bhadrabahu performed **sallekhana** (giving up the body by gradually renouncing food) on Chandragiri, and Chandragupta, after serving him for years, did the same there, at about **298 BCE**.

The monuments on Chandragiri named the **Bhadrabahu Cave** and the **Chandragupta Basadi** are linked to this tradition.

## What does the evidence say?

Historians view this story with caution:

- The **oldest inscriptions** that link Bhadrabahu and Chandragupta together date from about **600 CE** or later, that is, about nine hundred years after the event.
- In one old inscription the name of Bhadrabahu's disciple is "**Prabhachandra**". Lewis Rice took this to be Chandragupta's initiation name, but scholars such as J. F. Fleet rejected this identification.
- The **Shvetambara** tradition tells a different story; according to Hemachandra of the 12th century, Chandragupta became a Jain in the latter part of his life at Chanakya's urging and gave up his body in meditation, an account quite different from the Digambara tale.
- Greek sources describe Chandragupta as a king who hunted and performed sacrifices, and are silent about his last years.

Some scholars regard the tradition as basically correct, while according to others the tale developed later. One suggestion is also that the memory of **Samprati**, a descendant of Ashoka famous as a Jain patron, may have become attached to Chandragupta. The conclusion is that this is an **old and respected tradition**, but independent contemporary evidence is not available.

## Gommateshwara: history recorded in stone

The statue of **Bahubali**, called **Gommateshwara** here, standing on the summit of Vindhyagiri, is about **57 feet (17 metres)** high and is carved out of a single granite rock. It was built by **Chavundaraya**, minister and commander of the Western **Ganga dynasty**. On the basis of the inscription at the base, its construction is placed at **about 981–983 CE**.

According to Jain stories, Bahubali, a son of the first Tirthankara Rishabhadeva, gave up the kingdom even after winning a war against his brother Bharata, and performed such a long **standing penance** (kayotsarga) that creepers climbed over his body and anthills formed near his feet. In the statue these creepers and anthills are finely carved.

Every **twelve years** the **Mahamastakabhisheka** is held here, in which the statue is anointed with milk, saffron, sandalwood and so on. The last anointing took place in **2018** and the next is expected in **2030**.

## Importance

Shravanabelagola is a symbol of the long and deep history of Jainism in South India. Its hundreds of inscriptions are also an invaluable source for the development of the Kannada language and script. The site also teaches that, in studying history, it is necessary to distinguish **tradition** and **evidence**: both have their own importance, but they are not the same.
