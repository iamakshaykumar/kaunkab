## How the Act Came About

The proposals that emerged from the **Simon Commission** (1927), which came to review the 1919 reforms, and from the three **Round Table Conferences** (1930–1932) held in London afterwards, were presented by the British government in **March 1933** as a **White Paper**. A **Joint Select Committee** of Parliament chaired by **Lord Linlithgow** then considered them from April 1933 to November 1934.

The Secretary of State for India, **Sir Samuel Hoare** (August 1931 – June 1935), steered this long bill through Parliament. In Britain, the diehard wing of the Conservative Party, including **Winston Churchill**, opposed it strongly because they felt it gave India too much power. Finally, on **2 August 1935**, it received royal assent. Even after the parts concerning Burma were moved into a separate law, it had **321 sections and 10 schedules** — that is, it was a very long and detailed law.

## Main Provisions

- **Provincial autonomy:** The **dyarchy** of 1919 in the provinces was **abolished**. All departments of a province now came into the hands of Indian ministers responsible to the legislature. This part came into force on **1 April 1937**.
- **Special powers of the Governor:** Even so, the Governor retained "special responsibilities" and emergency powers. If the constitutional machinery failed, he could take over the administration of the province.
- **Dyarchy at the Centre:** The system removed from the provinces was proposed for the Centre — defence and foreign affairs would remain reserved to the Governor-General.
- **All-India Federation:** There was a plan to form a **Federation** from the provinces of British India and the princely states, which would come into being only when enough states joined it.
- **Three lists:** The subjects were divided between the Centre and the provinces into **Federal, Provincial and Concurrent** lists.
- **Federal Court:** A **Federal Court** was created to settle disputes between the Centre and the provinces, and it began work on **1 October 1937**; its first Chief Justice was **Sir Maurice Gwyer**.
- **Franchise:** The number of voters rose from about **5 million to 35 million**, but it was still bound by conditions of property and education.
- **Separate electorates:** Separate electorates were retained in accordance with the **Communal Award** of 1932.
- **New provinces and Burma:** **Burma** was separated from India, and new provinces were formed by separating **Sind** from Bombay and **Orissa** from Bihar.

## Indian Reaction

Almost all Indian parties criticised the Act. **Jawaharlal Nehru** called it **"a machine with strong brakes but no engine"** and a "new charter of slavery". The Congress said that real power still lay with the Governors and the Governor-General, and that in the proposed federation there was an attempt to suppress the nationalist voice through the nominated representatives of the princely states. The **Muslim League** also opposed its central scheme, while most **princely states** hesitated to join the federation, fearing a reduction of their autonomy.

Despite the opposition, almost all the major parties took part in the **provincial elections of 1937**, and the Congress formed governments in several provinces.

## The Unfinished Federation

The federal part of the Act **never came into force**, because enough princely states did not join, and when the **Second World War** began in 1939 the plan was shelved. The system of 1919 therefore continued at the Centre, with a few changes.

## Long-Term Impact

Historians look at this Act from two viewpoints. On one hand, it was a tool for maintaining imperial control — the powers of Governors, reserved subjects and separate electorates are examples. On the other hand, it gave India practical experience of a federal structure and of provincial government.

After independence in **1947**, both India and Pakistan adopted this Act, with some amendments, as their **interim constitution**. The framers of India's Constitution also took over many of its structures in altered form — such as the **three lists**, the **office of Governor**, the **emergency provisions** and the **federal judiciary**. For this reason the Act of 1935 is regarded as an important ancestor of the Indian Constitution.
