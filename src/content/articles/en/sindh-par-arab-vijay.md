## Background

At the end of the seventh century **Sindh** was ruled by the Brahmin **Chach dynasty**. The king of Sindh was **Dahir**, whose capital was **Aror** (near present-day Rohri). Both Hindus and Buddhists lived in his kingdom in large numbers.

Meanwhile, in the west, the Arab **Umayyad Caliphate** was expanding rapidly. After reaching Iran and Makran, Sindh lay on its eastern border. Some small Arab expeditions had already reached the western coast of India in the seventh century, but they had not succeeded.

## Reason for the Invasion

According to Arab sources, pirates near the coast of Sindh plundered Arab ships, and the powerful governor of Iraq, **Al-Hajjaj ibn Yusuf**, demanded compensation from Dahir. Dahir replied that the pirates were not under his control. This account rests mainly on later Arab-Persian sources, and many historians also regard it as a pretext for a policy of expansion. After this Hajjaj sent his young relative **Muhammad bin Qasim**, whose age is said to have been about 17 at the time, with an army.

## From Debal to Aror

- **Debal (711–712 CE):** The Arab army, which came by way of Makran, first besieged the port city of **Debal**. With the help of stone-throwing machines the city was captured and there was heavy destruction there.
- **Crossing the Indus:** After this the army subdued several cities and crossed the Indus.
- **Death of Dahir (712 CE):** In the decisive battle near Aror, King Dahir was killed fighting bravely. Many sources give its date as June 712, although some give 711.

## Brahmanabad and Multan

Resistance continued even after Dahir's death. His son **Jaisiya** held the front at **Brahmanabad**, and fighting also took place at the capital Aror. After Brahmanabad and Aror, the Arab army reached **Multan** in the north, which was captured by about 713 CE.

The *Chach Nama* has many touching tales about Dahir's queen and daughters, such as the revenge of Dahir's daughters Suryadevi and Parimaladevi. Historians regard these as legend, because they are not confirmed by any contemporary source.

## The Nature of Arab Rule

Temples were destroyed and plundered during the war, but after the victory Muhammad bin Qasim kept the local administrative arrangements largely in place in order to govern. Hindus and Buddhists were given the status of "dhimmi" (protected subjects) and allowed to follow their religion in return for the jizya tax, and many local officials were kept in their posts.

After the death of Caliph Al-Walid in 715 CE, the new Caliph Sulayman recalled Muhammad bin Qasim, and he died in captivity in that same year.

## Historical Significance

Sindh was the first region of the Indian subcontinent where lasting Arab rule was established. Even so, this conquest remained limited to Sindh and Multan. Later Arab expeditions were stopped by the **Gurjara-Pratiharas**, the **Chalukyas** and other Indian kingdoms. Through Sindh, trade and the exchange of knowledge between the Arab world and India also increased, and Indian books on mathematics and astronomy later reached Baghdad.
