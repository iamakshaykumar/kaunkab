## The Pratihara Dynasty and Bhoja's Inheritance

In the 8th-9th centuries, three powers — the **Gurjara-Pratiharas**, the **Palas** of Bengal and the **Rashtrakutas** of the Deccan — fought for a long time to control the prosperous northern Indian city of **Kannauj**. This is called the "tripartite struggle". The Pratihara king **Nagabhata II** had conquered Kannauj, but the kingdom weakened in the time of his son **Ramabhadra**.

Ramabhadra's son **Mihira Bhoja** (Bhoja I) ascended the throne around **836 CE**. He inherited a weak kingdom in difficult circumstances. In a reign of about fifty years he made it the greatest power of northern India.

## Expansion of the Empire

Mihira Bhoja first brought the rebellious feudatories of Rajasthan back under his control. A grant of his found at Daulatpura in Dausa district is believed to date from **843 CE**, which is proof of his control over this region.

At its peak his empire is said to have extended:
- to the **Narmada River** in the south,
- to the **Sutlej** in the north-west,
- and to the borders of **Bengal** in the east.

The capital was **Kannauj**, which was then also called "Mahodaya". The Gwalior region was also under him.

## Struggle with the Palas and Rashtrakutas

The tripartite struggle continued in Mihira Bhoja's time too. During the reign of the Pala king **Devapala** the Palas were powerful, but after Devapala, Mihira Bhoja defeated the Pala king **Narayanapala** and extended his influence eastward.

His clashes with the Rashtrakutas in the south continued. One of his cavalry campaigns in the territory of the Rashtrakutas of Gujarat failed, and around **880 CE** the Rashtrakuta king **Krishna II** defeated him near Ujjain. That is, not all his battles turned into victories; even so, his hold on northern India remained.

## Clash with the Arabs and Sulaiman's Account

From the 8th century Sindh was under Arab rule, and the Pratiharas became a great wall against their eastward advance. The struggle with the Arabs continued in Mihira Bhoja's time too.

The Arab merchant **Sulaiman**, whose account is believed to date from about **851 CE**, wrote about the king of "Juzr" (Gurjara). According to translations, he said that this king has a large army, that no other Indian king has cavalry as good as his, that he is an enemy of the Arabs, and that his kingdom is very safe from thieves and robbers. Historians identify this king as Mihira Bhoja.

## Adivaraha: Coins and Religion

Mihira Bhoja was a devotee of Vishnu and took the title **"Adivaraha"**. This title and the image of the Varaha avatar are found on his silver coins. These coins are found over a large area, which shows the economic strength of the empire. Some scholars also link the **Teli ka Mandir** in Gwalior Fort with the Pratihara period, and some with the time of Mihira Bhoja, but there is disagreement on its exact date.

## Succession and Significance

Around **885 CE** Mihira Bhoja was succeeded by his son **Mahendrapala I**, in whose time the Pratihara empire expanded further. The famous poet **Rajashekhara** was associated with Mahendrapala's court.

Mihira Bhoja's reign was a period of political stability in 9th-century northern India. Because of his long rule, large empire and defence of the western frontier, he is considered the most powerful king of the Pratihara dynasty. Note that the Paramara **King Bhoja** of Dhar (11th century) was a different king; the similarity of the two names often causes confusion.
