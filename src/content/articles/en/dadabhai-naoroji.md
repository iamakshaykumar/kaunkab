## Early Life

**Dadabhai Naoroji** was born on **4 September 1825** in **Navsari**, Gujarat, in a Parsi family. After studying at Elphinstone Institution in Bombay, in December 1855 he became professor of mathematics and natural philosophy there — the first Indian to reach this post. He was also active in social reform: his name is linked with the start of the Parsi reform body 'Rahnumae Mazdayasnan Sabha' in 1851 and the Gujarati paper **'Rast Goftar'** in 1854.

In 1855 he went to England as a partner in a Parsi trading firm, and in 1859 he opened his own cotton-trading firm. After this his life was divided between Bombay and London. In 1874 he was also, for a short time, Diwan of the princely state of Baroda.

## The Theory of the Drain of Wealth

Naoroji's greatest contribution is the theory of the **'Drain of Wealth'**. He had a role in founding the **East India Association** in London in 1867, and began presenting this idea in a speech given that same year. In 1876 came 'Poverty of India' and finally, in **1901**, his famous book **'Poverty and Un-British Rule in India'** was published.

His argument was that the cause of India's poverty was not just famine or backwardness but the wealth that went to England every year without any return — the salaries and pensions of British officials, the 'Home Charges', military expenditure and profit on foreign capital. By estimating per capita income he showed that the income of the ordinary Indian was inadequate even for the basic necessities of life. According to Wikipedia, in his 1901 book the total drain was estimated at 200 to 300 million pounds.

The word 'Un-British' in the book's title was chosen deliberately: Naoroji said that the rule in India was itself against the ideals of British justice and fairness.

## The Congress and National Politics

Naoroji was involved in the founding of the Indian National Congress in Bombay in 1885. He was Congress president three times — **1886** (Calcutta), **1893** (Lahore) and **1906** (Calcutta). He was a Moderate leader who believed in constitutional methods, petitions and influencing British public opinion.

The Calcutta session of 1906 took place when, after the Partition of Bengal, the tug-of-war between the Moderates and the Extremists was at its peak. Because he was acceptable to both sides, the elderly Naoroji was made president. In his presidential address the Congress's goal was stated as **'swaraj'** — a system of self-government like the British colonies. The Moderates read this as limited self-government and the Extremists as full independence; but this declaration of swaraj from the Congress platform is regarded as historic.

## The First Indian in the British Parliament

Naoroji believed that India's case had to be put directly in the British Parliament. In the general election of **1892**, as a Liberal Party candidate, he won from the **Finsbury Central** constituency in London by only **5 votes** and became the first Indian to reach the House of Commons. In Parliament he raised issues such as holding the Indian Civil Service examination in India also and reducing the burden of expenditure on India.

In the election of 1895 he lost this seat. In the same period he was a member of the Royal Commission on Indian expenditure (the Welby Commission), in whose formation his arguments on the drain are also believed to have played a part.

## Legacy

He died in Bombay on **30 June 1917** at the age of 91. Leaders such as Gokhale and Gandhi regarded him with deep respect; in a letter written to him from South Africa in 1894, Gandhi described him as being like a father.

Historians believe that the economic analysis of thinkers such as Naoroji, Ranade and R. C. Dutt laid the foundation of **economic nationalism** — that is, the understanding that British rule was not in India's interest. It is also noteworthy that his figures and estimates have been debated among later economists, but his argument played a decisive role in setting the ideological direction of the national movement.
