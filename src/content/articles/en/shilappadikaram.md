## The work and its author

**Silappatikaram** (in Tamil *Cilappatikaram*) means "the story of the anklet". It is regarded as the first epic of Tamil. This poem of about 5,700 lines is mainly in the **akaval** metre and is divided into three **kandams** (books), named after the three Tamil capitals: **Puhar Kandam** (Chola), **Madurai Kandam** (Pandya) and **Vanji Kandam** (Chera).

According to tradition, its author was **Ilango Adigal**, a Chera prince, the younger brother of King **Senguttuvan**, who became a Jain ascetic. But modern scholars doubt this tradition, because this information comes mainly from the poem's own preface. There is also deep disagreement over the date of composition: estimates range from the 2nd to the 6th century CE, and many scholars place it at about the 5th century. The poem is later than the Sangam poems but is deeply connected with the same tradition.

## Puhar: Kovalan, Kannagi and Madhavi

The story begins at the Chola port of **Puhar** (Kaveripattinam). **Kovalan**, the son of a rich merchant, is married to **Kannagi**. After some time Kovalan falls in love with the dancer **Madhavi**, abandons Kannagi and squanders all his wealth. After a misunderstanding at a festival, he leaves Madhavi and returns to Kannagi. To begin a new life the two set out on foot for the Pandya capital **Madurai**; Kannagi has only her pair of valuable **anklets** left.

## Madurai: the demand for justice

In Madurai Kovalan goes to the market to sell one anklet. A goldsmith of the royal palace, who had himself stolen the queen's anklet, accuses Kovalan of theft. The Pandya king **Nedunjeliyan** sentences Kovalan to death without an inquiry.

Hearing the news, Kannagi goes to the royal court with the other anklet. She breaks it and shows that it contains **rubies**, while the queen's anklet had **pearls**. Realising his injustice, the king gives up his life and the queen dies with him. According to the poem, by Kannagi's curse the city of Madurai bursts into flames, but Brahmins, women, children, the old and the virtuous are spared. This is a literary tale, not a historical account of a real event.

## Vanji: the goddess Pattini

Immersed in grief, Kannagi goes west to the Chera country, where, according to the poem, she ascends to heaven with Kovalan in a celestial chariot. Hearing this story, the Chera king **Senguttuvan** brings a stone from the Himalaya, bathes it in the Ganga, has an image of Kannagi made from it, and installs a temple to her as **Pattini** (the chaste goddess). Many kings attend the inauguration, including **Gajabahu** of Lanka. The description of the Himalayan expedition is regarded as poetic exaggeration.

## Importance for history

The Silappatikaram is not merely a story of love and justice; it is a detailed picture of ancient Tamil society. It contains descriptions of the port of Puhar, Yavana traders, markets, festivals, music and dance, and is an important source for the history of Tamil music and drama. In the poem the Jain, Buddhist and Vedic-Puranic religious streams appear side by side. It is also distinctive for joining all three Tamil kingdoms, Chola, Pandya and Chera, in a single story.

## Legacy

The Silappatikaram is counted as the first of the "Five Great Epics" of Tamil. Sattanar's epic **Manimekalai** is linked to this same story; its heroine is the daughter of Kovalan and Madhavi, who becomes a Buddhist nun. In Tamil Nadu, Kerala and Sri Lanka, Kannagi is still worshipped and remembered as a symbol of justice and the power of women. The first English translation of the poem was made by V. R. Ramachandra Dikshitar in 1939.
