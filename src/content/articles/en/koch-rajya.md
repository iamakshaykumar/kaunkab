## A New Kingdom on the Land of Kamata

After the ancient kingdom of Kamarupa broke up, the **Kamata kingdom** arose in present-day north Bengal and western Assam. At the end of the fifteenth century the Sultan of Bengal, Alauddin Husain Shah, conquered Kamata, but because of the distance his control there could not last. The region was divided among many small Bhuyan chieftains.

In this vacuum a young leader of the **Koch** and **Mech** communities, **Bisu**, rose. He was born near Chikna hill, in the house of the Mech chieftain Hariya Mandal. He organised the Koch, Mech and neighbouring communities and defeated the Bhuyan chieftains one by one.

## Biswa Singha: The Founder (1515–1540)

Around **1515** Bisu took the Hindu name **Biswa Singha** and declared himself king. Brahmin priests put about a legend linking his lineage to Lord Shiva, which gave the new dynasty Kshatriya status — this was a common way in that period of giving legitimacy to rule. He built a system based on a council of twelve ministers (karji), a standing army and a census of able-bodied men (paik), and moved the capital from Chikna to Hingulabas. His relations with the **Ahom** kingdom rising in the east were sometimes of friendship, sometimes of subordination.

## Naranarayan and Chilarai: The Zenith

After Biswa Singha, around 1540, his son **Naranarayan** (Malladev) became king and made his brother **Shuklabdhaj** the crown prince and chief commander. Shuklabdhaj's army moved so fast that people began to call him **"Chilarai"** — the prince as swift as a kite (chil).

In late 1562 Chilarai launched a major campaign against the Ahom kingdom; by April 1563 the Ahom capital had fallen, and in the treaty Koch supremacy was accepted. After this the Koch armies reached Cachar, Manipur, Jaintia, Tripura and Sylhet, and many kings agreed to pay tribute. These details come mainly from Koch genealogies, in which some exaggeration is possible. This period of expansion halted when conflict arose with the Karrani Sultan of Bengal, **Sulaiman Karrani**; according to traditional accounts, Chilarai was also held captive for a time in this war.

Chilarai died in **1571** of smallpox on the bank of the Ganga.

## Kamakhya, Sankardev and Culture

The most lasting legacy of the Koch kings is cultural. The **Kamakhya Temple** on Nilachal Hill in Guwahati was rebuilt in **1565**. An inscription in the temple is believed to mention its construction by Chilarai in Shaka year 1487 (1565 CE). Sources do not agree on who destroyed the earlier temple.

The great Vaishnava saint of Assam, **Srimanta Sankardev**, found refuge in the Koch kingdom after leaving the Ahom kingdom. Under Chilarai's patronage his *Ekasarana* faith could spread. Naranarayan had scholars make Assamese translations of the Mahabharata, the Puranas and the Gita, and issued silver **Narayani** coins. He also issued an order recognising tribal forms of worship.

## Partition and Cooch Behar

Chilarai's son **Raghudev** received authority over the eastern part of the kingdom around 1581. After Naranarayan's death in 1587 the kingdom was permanently divided into two parts — **Cooch Behar** in the west (under Naranarayan's son **Lakshminarayan**) and **Koch Hajo** in the east. Koch Hajo later became a battleground of the Mughals and the Ahoms, while Cooch Behar accepted Mughal suzerainty and later, in the British period, became a princely state. After independence, the Cooch Behar princely state merged with India in 1949 and became a district of West Bengal in 1950. Even today the memory of the Koch kings and the heroic Chilarai is deep in Cooch Behar, Assam and north Bengal.
