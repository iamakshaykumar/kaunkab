## A Kingdom of the Brahmaputra Valley

**Kamarupa** was an ancient kingdom of Assam, centred in the Brahmaputra valley. In the epics and Puranas this region is also called **Pragjyotisha**, and its capital Pragjyotishpura is generally identified with present-day **Guwahati**. The first historical mention of the name "Kamarupa" occurs in the fourth century in the **Prayag Prashasti of Samudragupta**, where it is described as a tribute-paying kingdom on the frontier of the Gupta empire.

Historians date the Kamarupa kingdom from about **350 to 1140 CE**. Over this long period, three main dynasties ruled.

## The Varman Dynasty (c. 350–650)

The Varman dynasty was founded by **Pushyavarman**. The kings of this dynasty claimed descent from the legendary kings Narakasura and Bhagadatta — this genealogy rests on tradition, not on historical evidence. The kings of this line remained connected with the politics of northern India; Bhaskaravarman's father **Susthitavarman** had to suffer defeat at the hands of the Later Gupta king Mahasenagupta.

## Bhaskaravarman and Harsha

The most famous king of the Varman dynasty was **Bhaskaravarman** (c. 600–650 CE). He ascended the throne after his brother Supratishthitavarman died childless. At that time **Shashanka**, the king of Bengal (Gauda), was the great power of eastern India. When Rajyavardhana, the king of Thanesar, was killed at the hands of Shashanka, Bhaskaravarman sent his envoy **Hamsavega** to Rajyavardhana's younger brother **Harshavardhana** with an offer of friendship. This is described in Banabhatta's "Harshacharita". The alliance hemmed Shashanka in from two sides.

Bhaskaravarman's **Nidhanpur** and **Dubi** copper plates are the main inscriptions for his history. He issued the Nidhanpur copper plate from the victory camp at **Karnasuvarna** (Shashanka's old capital) — this suggests that after Shashanka, part of Bengal came under his influence.

## Xuanzang's Journey

When the Chinese Buddhist traveller **Xuanzang** was at Nalanda, Bhaskaravarman invited him to Kamarupa. According to Chinese sources, Harsha also summoned him, and in the end Bhaskaravarman took him along to meet Harsha. The seal of Bhaskaravarman found at Nalanda (c. 643 CE) is regarded as archaeological evidence of these relations. Xuanzang described the king of Kamarupa as belonging to a Brahmin line and wrote that most of the people there were not Buddhists, yet the king respected scholars.

After Harsha's death (around 647 CE), at the time of the expedition of the Chinese envoy **Wang Xuance**, Bhaskaravarman supplied provisions to his army. According to a Chinese account, he also asked China for an image of Laozi, the founder of Taoism. The **Bhaskarabda** era, used in Assam, is counted from his accession.

## The Mlechchha and Pala Dynasties

After Bhaskaravarman's death (c. 650 CE), the Varman dynasty came to an end. From about 655 CE, **Salastambha** established a new dynasty, which in inscriptions calls itself the **"Mlechchha" dynasty**. Its capital was **Haruppeshvara**, which is identified with Dah Parbatia near Tezpur. Around 900 CE, after the last king Tyagasimha died childless, the feudatories chose **Brahmapala** as king. It was **Ratnapala** of this **Kamarupa Pala dynasty** (which is distinct from the Pala dynasty of Bengal) who founded the capital **Durjaya** (near Guwahati).

## The End and the Legacy

In the eleventh and twelfth centuries, the Pala king Ramapala of Bengal took over western Kamarupa, and gradually the kingdom broke up into small parts. By the thirteenth century, the Kamata kingdom, and in the east the **Ahom kingdom**, had taken the place of Kamarupa. Ruins such as the Dah Parbatia doorframe near Tezpur and Madan Kamdev near Guwahati still bear witness to this long history.
