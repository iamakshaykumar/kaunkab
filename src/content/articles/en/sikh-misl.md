## Background: Decades of Struggle

After the martyrdom of **Banda Singh Bahadur** in 1716, the repression of the Sikhs of Punjab by the Mughal governors increased. The Sikhs, divided into small **bands (jathas)**, kept up the struggle from forests and remote areas. In the 1730s, under the leadership of **Nawab Kapur Singh**, the work of organising these bands into larger groups — the Budha Dal and the Taruna Dal — began.

The invasions of **Nadir Shah** in 1739 and of **Ahmad Shah Abdali** (Durrani) from 1748 hollowed out Mughal authority in Punjab. It was in this political vacuum that Sikh power arose.

## 1748: The Formation of the Dal Khalsa

In **1748**, at the **Sarbat Khalsa** (assembly of the whole community) held in Amritsar, a **gurmata** (collective resolution) was passed. All the bands were reorganised into eleven **misls**, and their joint army was named the **Dal Khalsa**. Its supreme command was given to **Jassa Singh Ahluwalia**. There is disagreement about the date of this assembly — some sources place it on Vaisakhi (29 March 1748), others on Diwali.

The main misls were — **Ahluwalia, Bhangi, Kanhaiya, Ramgarhia, Sukerchakia, Singhpuria, Nakai, Nishanwalia, Dallewalia, Karorsinghia (Panjgarhia) and Shaheedan**. Counting the **Phulkian** misl of Malwa, many speak of twelve misls, but it was not part of the formation of 1748.

## How Did the Misls Work?

Each misl had its own **sardar** and was almost independent in its own territory. Even so, to decide common matters, a Sarbat Khalsa was generally held in Amritsar twice a year — on Vaisakhi and Diwali — where gurmatas were passed.

A major source of income was the **rakhi** system: villages gave a share of their produce, and in return the misl protected them from plunder and attacks. Fast detachments of horsemen were the real strength of the misls.

## Clash with Abdali

In Ahmad Shah Abdali's repeated invasions the Sikhs paid a heavy price. On **5 February 1762**, at **Kup** near Malerkotla, Abdali's army attacked a large convoy of Sikh families. Estimates of the number killed in this **Vadda Ghallughara** (Great Massacre) vary widely — from about 5,000 to 30,000 or even more.

Even so, the Sikhs reorganised quickly. As soon as Abdali went back, they took back the territories. In **1765** the Sikhs took control of **Lahore** and struck coins in their own name from its mint — a clear declaration of their sovereignty.

## Expansion and Mutual Rivalry

In the following decades the misls began raiding from the Sutlej to the Indus and into the Yamuna-Ganga doab. On **11 March 1783** the joint army of Baghel Singh, Jassa Singh Ahluwalia and Jassa Singh Ramgarhia reached **Delhi** for a short time.

But as the external threat lessened, rivalry among the misls over land and influence began to grow. After the death of Jassa Singh Ahluwalia in 1783, the unity of the Dal Khalsa also weakened. Misls such as the Bhangi, Kanhaiya, Ramgarhia and Sukerchakia began fighting one another.

## Ranjit Singh and the End of the Misl Era

In this rivalry the **Sukerchakia misl** came out ahead. Its young sardar **Ranjit Singh** took control of Lahore on **7 July 1799** and in the following years merged most of the misls into his state. The Phulkian states south of the Sutlej — Patiala, Nabha, Jind — came under British protection.

The misl era showed that even amid oppression and invasions, a community could become a political power through collective decisions and shared leadership. This very foundation later became the basis of the **Sikh Empire**.
