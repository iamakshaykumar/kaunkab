## The Hour of Crisis

After the martyrdom of Chhatrapati Sambhaji in 1689, his half-brother **Rajaram** took up the command of the Maratha struggle and carried on the war in the south from **Jinji**. But on **3 March 1700** Rajaram died of illness at **Sinhagad**. At that time **Aurangzeb** himself was camped in Maharashtra with a huge army, besieging the Maratha forts one by one. Sambhaji's son **Shahu** was in Mughal captivity. It seemed that the Maratha kingdom, without a king, would fall apart.

## Who Tarabai Was

Tarabai (born about **1675**) was the daughter of Shivaji's famous commander-in-chief **Hambirrao Mohite** and the wife of Rajaram. Even in her husband's lifetime she had become known for her knowledge of matters of administration and the army. The Mughal historian **Khafi Khan** — who had no sympathy for the Marathas — also described her as a clever woman, skilled in civil and military affairs.

After Rajaram's death, Tarabai declared her four-year-old son **Shivaji II** Chhatrapati and took charge of the government herself as regent.

## Strategy Against Aurangzeb

Between 1700 and 1707 the Mughal army captured many forts such as Satara, Parli, Panhala, Vishalgad, Sinhagad, Rajgad and Torna — often through long sieges, heavy expense or bribery. But Tarabai's strategy was not to save forts at any cost. Instead of standing and fighting in one place, the Maratha armies split into fast cavalry bands that attacked Mughal supply routes and territories, and as soon as the Mughal army moved on, many forts were taken back.

Tarabai kept the chiefs bound to the war by giving them the right to collect taxes from the territories they won. Under commanders such as **Dhanaji Jadhav**, Maratha bands advanced beyond the Deccan and began raiding as far as **Malwa** and **Gujarat**. Khafi Khan wrote that the Maratha armies had begun entering the old territories of the empire and plundering.

## Aurangzeb's Death and Shahu's Return

After fighting in the Deccan for about twenty-five years, Aurangzeb died on **3 March 1707**. The Maratha state had not been wiped out — it had spread more than before. That is why the struggle from 1689 to 1707 is often called the **"Maratha War of Independence"**, and its last phase was led by Tarabai.

After Aurangzeb, the Mughals released **Shahu**, perhaps so that the Marathas would split — and so it happened. Tarabai did not accept Shahu's claim. On **12 October 1707** the two sides came face to face at **Khed**, near Pune; most of the chiefs, including Dhanaji Jadhav, went over to Shahu's side, and Shahu was victorious. In early 1708 Shahu became Chhatrapati at Satara.

## Kolhapur and Later Life

Tarabai did not accept defeat and established a separate seat at **Kolhapur**. But in **1714** the faction of Rajaram's second wife **Rajasbai** removed her and her son from power and imprisoned them. Later Tarabai reached an agreement with Shahu and lived at Satara. After Shahu's death in 1749 she put **Ramraja**, whom she presented as her grandson, on the throne, but soon, in a clash with the Peshwa **Balaji Bajirao**, she too was imprisoned. In the end she accepted the Peshwa's authority. She died at Satara in **1761**.

## Significance

Tarabai took up leadership at a time when the Maratha state had neither a king nor a capital, and faced the full might of the Mughal Empire. Under her leadership the Maratha resistance held, and Aurangzeb's Deccan campaign was left unfinished. Her later political manoeuvres remain disputed, but historians widely accept her role in the decisive years 1700–1707.
