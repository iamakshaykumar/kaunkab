## The Eastern Ganga Dynasty and Narasimhadeva I

From the eleventh to the fifteenth century, Odisha (ancient Kalinga) was ruled by the **Eastern Ganga dynasty**. It was in the time of Anantavarman Chodaganga of this dynasty that the construction of the Jagannath Temple at Puri had begun.

**Narasimhadeva I** (reigned c. 1238–1264 CE) was a powerful ruler of this dynasty. In his time there was conflict with the sultans of Bengal, and inscriptions and Persian accounts show that around 1243–44 he conducted a successful campaign against the Bengal army. The Sun Temple is believed to have been built in his reign, around 1250 CE. According to one tradition it took 12 years and about 1,200 craftsmen to build, but this figure rests more on popular memory than on a historical document.

## A Chariot of Stone

The Konark temple is conceived as a huge **chariot** of the Sun God:
- On both sides of the high platform, a total of **24 wheels** are carved, with fine carving on them.
- In front, stone horses are shown pulling the chariot.
- The spokes of the wheels are made in such a way that many people link them with estimating time like a sundial.
- The numbers of wheels and horses are often regarded as symbols of the cycle of time — months, fortnights and days of the week.

## Kalinga-Style Architecture

The temple is built in the **Kalinga style** of Odisha, in which a tall spire (deul or rekha deul) rises over the main sanctum, and in front is an assembly hall (**jagamohana**) with a pyramid-like roof. In front of the temple there is also a separate **natya mandapa** (dance hall).

The walls have countless sculptures of gods and goddesses, musicians, dancers, elephants, scenes of war and daily life. They give a glimpse of the society, dress and entertainment of the time.

Large images of the Sun God made of green chlorite stone have also been found around the temple, which are linked with the forms of the Sun in the morning, noon and evening. The temple faces east, so that the first rays of the rising sun fall on the entrance. Many sculptures found in the temple complex are now kept in the Konark Archaeological Museum and in other museums of the country.

## The Fallen Spire and Conservation

The magnificent structure seen today is mainly the jagamohana. The tall spire of the main sanctum collapsed over time. When and why it fell is debated among scholars; causes such as a weak foundation, the coastal environment and later invasions are discussed.

In the early twentieth century (around 1903) the British administration filled the jagamohana with sand and sealed it, to save it from collapsing. The Archaeological Survey of India looks after it. In 1984 UNESCO declared it a World Heritage Site.

## Significance

The Konark temple is an example of the blending of science, religion and beauty in Indian art. It is a lasting monument to the tradition of sun worship, to the prosperity of the Eastern Ganga kings and to the wonderful skill of the craftsmen of Odisha. The Konark Dance Festival held here every year keeps this heritage alive.
