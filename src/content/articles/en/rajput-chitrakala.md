## Background

Rajput painting is the name for the miniatures made from about the end of the sixteenth century to the middle of the nineteenth century at the Hindu royal courts of Rajasthan (the plains) and the hills of Punjab-Himachal-Jammu (Pahari). Their subjects were mostly religious and literary — the **Ragamala** (pictorial form of ragas), Krishna's lila in the **Bhagavata Purana** and **Gita Govinda**, the Ramayana, the classification of heroes and heroines, and the **Barahmasa**. Portraits of kings, hunts and court scenes were also made. Contact with the Mughal court influenced these styles, but they kept their own identity — bright colours, flat backgrounds and heartfelt devotion.

Most paintings were made not in a court workshop but in painter families, where the skill passed from father to son.

## Mewar

Among the earliest dated works of Mewar is the **Chawand Ragamala of 1605**, made by Nisardin (Nasiruddin) — this was the time when Chawand was Mewar's capital. In the time of Rana Jagat Singh I (reigned 1628–1652) **Sahibdin** was the leading painter; under his leadership huge illustrated volumes of the Ramayana were prepared between 1649 and 1653. The Mewar style is known for its deep reds and yellows and its simple, direct figures.

## Bundi, Kota and Marwar

In **Bundi** the Chunar Ragamala of 1591 is an early sign of Mughal contact. In the paintings of Bundi and **Kota**, which became a separate state in 1624, lush nature, rain and later lively hunting scenes were prominent. In **Marwar** the Pali Ragamala of 1623 is important, and the style of **Bikaner** came closest to Mughal refinement.

## Kishangarh and Bani Thani

In the small state of Kishangarh a distinctive style developed in the middle of the eighteenth century. King **Sawant Singh** (reigned from 1748), who wrote poems of Krishna devotion under the pen name 'Nagaridas', was its patron, and the painter **Nihal Chand** is regarded as its chief artist. These paintings have figures with long, bow-like eyebrows, lotus-petal eyes, pointed chins and long necks. According to tradition the model for the paintings of Radha was **Bani Thani**, the singer-poet beloved of Sawant Singh — many scholars regard this identification as based on tradition.

## The Pahari Styles: From Basohli to Kangra

The first major centre of Pahari painting was **Basohli** (today in Kathua district of Jammu and Kashmir). In its paintings of the late seventeenth century, blazing reds and yellows were used, and even pieces of the shining wings of beetles were applied to bring out jewellery. The **Rasamanjari** (c. 1694–95), linked with a painter named Devidas, is considered its famous example.

In the eighteenth century the sons of Pandit Seu of **Guler**, **Manaku** and **Nainsukh** (c. 1710–1778), gave the style a new direction. From about 1740 Nainsukh worked for Raja **Balwant Singh** of Jasrota and made informal, intimate pictures of his everyday life — reading, listening to music, warming himself at a fire in winter — which are rare in Indian art. From the workshop of his family came the gentle, rhythmic style that later flowered at **Kangra** under the patronage of Raja Sansar Chand — soft colours, wide natural scenes and moods of love and devotion.

## Significance

Rajput and Pahari paintings are a visual record of India's religious poetry, music and court life. In the nineteenth century, with the political weakness of the Rajput states and the spread of Western painting styles and photography, this tradition declined, but today these paintings are treasures of the world's major museums.
