## Ullal and the Chowta Dynasty

The **Tulunadu** region of coastal Karnataka was famous in the sixteenth century for the trade in black pepper, cardamom and rice. **Ullal**, on the mouth of the Netravati river near Mangalore, was a small but prosperous port of this trade, visited by Arab and Indian merchants.

It was ruled by a Jain dynasty called the **Chowtas**, whose capital was Puttige (near Moodbidri). This dynasty followed the **Aliyasantana** tradition, that is, matrilineal succession — power passed to sisters' sons and daughters. According to this tradition, her maternal uncle **Tirumala Raya** handed over the rule of Ullal to **Abbakka**.

## Portuguese Pressure

After the arrival of Vasco da Gama in 1498, the Portuguese captured Goa in 1510 and began to build their naval dominance on the western coast. They demanded tribute from the coastal kingdoms and forced merchant ships to take their permit (cartaz).

Abbakka refused to accept the Portuguese terms and kept up relations with Arab merchants and the **Zamorin of Calicut**. She was married to **Lakshmappa Arasa** of the Banga dynasty of Mangalore, but the marriage did not last; according to later accounts he went over to the side of the Portuguese.

## Years of Struggle

According to the available accounts, the Portuguese attacked Ullal several times:

- In **1555**, Abbakka forced a fleet led by Admiral Dom Álvaro da Silveira to retreat — many accounts say so.
- Further attacks followed in the 1550s and 1560s; once the Portuguese even succeeded in occupying the town of Ullal.
- According to folk tradition, around 1568 Abbakka made a sudden night raid and inflicted heavy damage on the Portuguese camp.
- Around **1570**, in alliance with the Zamorin and Bijapur, she attacked the Portuguese fort at Mangalore, but this campaign failed.

Tradition says that in the end she was captured through betrayal and died in prison. The exact dates and details of these events differ in different sources, and many thrilling episodes (such as burning ships with fire arrows) come from folk tales.

## One Name, Many Queens?

A big problem for historians is that there was more than one queen named **Abbakka** in Ullal — in the matrilineal tradition the name continued from generation to generation. Some writers speak of as many as four Abbakkas. The Italian traveller **Pietro Della Valle**, in December 1623, described meeting a queen of Ullal in the Mangalore region, who could not have been the queen of the 1550s–60s. It therefore seems that in the "Rani Abbakka" of folk memory the struggles of several queens have been joined together.

Most of the outside information about her comes from Portuguese sources, while local tradition keeps her story alive in Yakshagana, Bhuta Kola and folk songs.

## Legacy

Rani Abbakka is often counted among the earliest women freedom fighters of India — this is a modern view, but it is true that, as the ruler of a small kingdom, she rejected the demands of a powerful naval empire. Her administration was diverse, including Jains, Hindus and Muslims alike.

Today the **Veera Rani Abbakka Utsava** is celebrated in Ullal every year, an equestrian statue of her stands there, a Coast Guard vessel was named after her in 2012, and in 2023 the Indian postal department issued a stamp on her.
