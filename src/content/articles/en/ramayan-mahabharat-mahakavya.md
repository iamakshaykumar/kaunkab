## History or Literature?

Indian tradition calls the **Ramayana** and the **Mahabharata** "**itihasa**" (meaning "so it happened"). But from a historian's point of view they are first of all works of **literature and tradition** — there is no independent archaeological or inscriptional evidence for the dates or historicity of the events they describe, and scholars differ deeply on this. The subject of this article is not the story but the **texts** — how they were formed and grew, and how modern scholars tried to recover their original wording.

## The Valmiki Ramayana

Tradition regards **Valmiki** as the author of the Ramayana and the "**Adikavi**" (first poet). According to the story, he saw a hunter's arrow kill one of a pair of krauncha birds, and out of his grief the first **shloka** metre burst forth. The Valmiki Ramayana has **seven kandas** and about **24,000 shlokas**.

Scholars believe that:

- Kandas **2 to 6** (Ayodhya to Yuddha Kanda) are the oldest part.
- The **Balakanda** and the **Uttarakanda** were added later — these contain more passages that clearly describe Rama as an avatar of Vishnu.
- Robert Goldman placed the original oral composition at about **750–500 BCE**; other estimates put it somewhat later, and the final layers are thought to run up to the third century CE.

## Vyasa and the Mahabharata

Tradition regards the **Mahabharata** as the work of **Krishna Dvaipayana Vyasa**, who is also a character in the story. It has **18 parvas**, with the **Harivamsha** added as an appendix. The longest text has more than a hundred thousand shlokas — it is counted among the longest poems in the world. The **Bhagavad Gita** is a part of its **Bhishma Parva** (chapters 25–42).

The text itself points to its three layers — **Jaya**, **Bharata** (24,000 shlokas) and the full **Mahabharata**. However, scholars such as John Brockington regard the idea of an "8,800-verse Jaya" as a misreading of a single verse. Most scholars place the main period of its composition at about the **third century BCE to the third century CE**, with its final form reached by the early Gupta period (about the fourth century CE).

## The Critical Editions: The Search for the Original Text

Through centuries of hand copying, both texts developed different **versions** (recensions) in different regions — for example northern and southern ones. In every region verses were added and altered.

- **Mahabharata:** The **Bhandarkar Oriental Research Institute** in Pune began the work of comparing manuscripts from India and abroad in **1919**. The first general editor was **V. S. Sukthankar**. The work was completed in **1966** — 19 volumes of about 13,000 pages, followed by two volumes on the Harivamsha.
- **Ramayana:** The **Oriental Institute** in Baroda (Vadodara) published a seven-volume critical edition between **1958 and 1975**, edited by **G. H. Bhatt** and later **U. P. Shah**.

The aim of these editions was to find "the oldest recoverable common text", not the word-for-word text of a single "original author". Passages that appear to have been added later were given in footnotes and appendices.

## A Tradition That Kept Moving On

The two epics did not remain limited to Sanskrit. The story of Rama was written again and again in forms such as **Kamban**'s Tamil version (12th century), the Ranganatha Ramayana in Telugu and **Tulsidas**'s Ramcharitmanas in Awadhi (around 1576). Versions of these tales were also created across Southeast Asia. That is why historians read them not as the "events" of a single time but as **a living tradition** — an almost thousand-year-long mirror of the values, disputes and imaginations of Indian society.
