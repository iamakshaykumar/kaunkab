## Early Life

Very little is certain about Todar Mal's early life. According to tradition he was born around 1523–24 at **Laharpur** (in present-day Uttar Pradesh) in the Awadh region, in a Khatri family, but both the birthplace and the year are disputed.

He began as a writer-clerk and rose in the service of **Sher Shah Suri**. It is said that he also had a role in supervising the building of the **Rohtas Fort** in Punjab. His experience of the land revenue system based on measurement that Sher Shah had begun was of great use to Todar Mal.

## In Akbar's Service

After the fall of the Sur dynasty, Todar Mal entered Mughal service around **1562**. Besides being an administrator he was also a capable commander and took part in the campaigns of Gujarat, Bengal and Bihar. On **3 March 1575**, at the battle of **Tukaroi** against the Afghan sultan of Bengal, Daud Khan Karrani, when the Mughal commander Munim Khan was wounded, Todar Mal took charge of the front and played a big role in the Mughal victory. After the conquest of Gujarat he was also entrusted with a survey of the land system there.

In the 1570s he was given charge of the revenue department, and in **1582** he was made the chief diwan (finance minister) of the empire.

## The Zabt and Dahsala System

In Akbar's early years there were many experiments in fixing revenue, but fixing crop prices every year was difficult and full of corruption. The lasting solution came in the form of the **Dahsala** ("ten-year") system introduced in **1580**. It was part of the **zabt** system, that is, one based on measurement:

- For the ten years from 1570–71 to 1579–80, the **average yield** and **average price** of each crop were worked out.
- About **one-third** of the average yield was taken as the state's share and converted, on the basis of prices, into a **cash** rate per bigha.
- A list of crop-wise rates was made for each region, called the **"Dastur-ul-Amal"**.
- Land was divided into four classes on the basis of continuity of cultivation: **polaj** (cultivated every year), **parauti** (left fallow for a time), **chachar** (fallow for three to four years) and **banjar** (uncultivated for five years or more).
- For measurement the **jarib**, a bamboo rod joined by iron rings, and the standard **Ilahi gaz** were used.

It is worth noting that this system was not the gift of Todar Mal alone. **Khwaja Shah Mansur**, who was diwan from 1578 to 1581, also played an important role in the surveys. Even so, in tradition the system is known by Todar Mal's name.

## Limits of the Dahsala

The system was applied mainly in the central provinces such as Lahore, Multan, Delhi, Agra, Awadh, Allahabad and Malwa. In Bengal, Kashmir, Sindh and some parts of the Deccan other methods like batai (crop sharing) or kankut continued. With cash revenue, peasants became dependent on the market to sell their crops, and relief in times of bad weather depended on the officials.

## Religious Life and Death

Todar Mal was a devout Hindu. According to tradition, around 1585 he helped in the rebuilding of the **Kashi Vishwanath temple** at Varanasi. A Persian translation of the Bhagavata Purana and a Sanskrit collection called "Todarananda" are also linked with him.

He died at **Lahore** on **8 November 1589**. Popular tradition counts him among Akbar's "Navaratnas" (nine jewels), although the list of the Navaratnas is believed to have been formed by later tradition.

## Legacy

Todar Mal's system gave the Mughal state a regular and predictable income, which made the mansabdari and jagir systems possible. The administrative units of suba, sarkar and pargana, and many terms connected with revenue, remained in use for a long time afterwards.
