## The Port of the Pallavas

**Mamallapuram** (Mahabalipuram) lies on the Coromandel coast of Tamil Nadu, about 60 kilometres south of Chennai. In the seventh century it was the main port of the **Pallava** kings of Kanchipuram. The city is believed to be named after the title **"Mamalla"** (great wrestler) of King **Narasimhavarman I** (c. 630–668 CE) — the same king who defeated the Chalukya king Pulakeshin II and captured Vatapi.

The building of the monuments here is mainly linked to three kings — **Mahendravarman I**, his son **Narasimhavarman I**, and later **Narasimhavarman II "Rajasimha"** (c. 700–728 CE). Scholars disagree about which monument was built in the time of which king; some historians place most of the work in Rajasimha's period.

## The Pancha Rathas: Temples Made from Single Rocks

The **Pancha Rathas** are five **monolithic** (carved from a single rock) temples shaped like chariots. They are popularly named after the Pandavas and Draupadi — **Dharmaraja Ratha**, **Bhima Ratha**, **Arjuna Ratha**, **Nakula-Sahadeva Ratha** and **Draupadi Ratha** — but they are not actually dedicated to the Pandavas; these names came later. The Dharmaraja Ratha is the largest (about 13 × 11 metres). Near the rathas there are also figures of a **Nandi**, a **lion** and an **elephant** made from single stones.

In these rathas different roof styles can be seen — hut-like, stepped pyramid-like and gaja-prishtha (like an elephant's back) — which became the basis for the later development of the **Dravidian temple style**. It is believed that work stopped after the death of Narasimhavarman I and that these rathas were never completed or consecrated.

## The Descent of the Ganges or Arjuna's Penance

The most famous work at Mamallapuram is the **relief sculpture** made on two huge adjoining rocks, about 29 metres long and 13 metres high — one of the largest open-air reliefs in the world. It has more than a hundred figures — gods, gandharvas, sages, hunters, monkeys, nearly life-size elephants and their young.

In the natural **cleft** between the two rocks, nagas and nagins have been carved, making the cleft look like a river. It is believed that water was once made to flow down it from a tank above. The meaning of the scene is disputed: according to one view it is the story of the Ganga descending to earth through the **penance of Bhagiratha**, according to another it is **Arjuna's penance** in the Mahabharata to obtain the Pashupata weapon from Shiva.

## The Cave Mandapas

There are also several **mandapas** (pillared cave temples) dug into the rocks here. The **Varaha Mandapa** shows Vishnu's boar incarnation lifting the earth. In the **Mahishasuramardini Mandapa**, the battle of Goddess Durga with Mahishasura on one side and the calm scene of Vishnu reclining on Shesha on the other face each other. The **Krishna Mandapa** has scenes of Krishna lifting Mount Govardhan and of cowherd life.

## The Shore Temple and Legacy

In the early eighth century, in Rajasimha's time, the **Shore Temple** was built on the seashore by joining cut granite stones, believed to have been completed by about 725 CE. It has three shrines — two of Shiva and, between them, a small shrine of reclining Vishnu. It is counted among the oldest structural (built by joining) stone temples of South India. In the tsunami of 2004 this temple escaped major damage, while when the waves withdrew some old structures near the shore were briefly visible.

From the rock-cut rathas to the Shore Temple built by joining stones, Mamallapuram shows the whole story of the development of South Indian temple architecture in one place. For this reason, in **1984** this group of monuments was declared a UNESCO World Heritage Site.
