## Background: The Question of Turkey After the War

In the First World War (1914–18) Ottoman Turkey fought on Germany's side against Britain. The Sultan of Turkey was regarded by many Sunni Muslims around the world as the **Caliph**, that is, the religious and political head of the Islamic world. After Turkey's defeat in the war, there was a fear that the victorious European powers would break up the Ottoman empire into pieces and that holy places such as Mecca and Medina would pass out of the Caliph's control.

Indian Muslims felt that the assurance of a generous attitude towards Turkey given by the British government during the war was being broken. In 1919 there was already anger across the country because of the Rowlatt Act and the Jallianwala Bagh massacre.

## The Beginning of the Movement

In September 1919, at a Muslim conference in Lucknow, it was decided to launch an organised movement on the Khilafat issue, and on **17 October 1919** the first **"Khilafat Day"** was observed across the country — shops remained closed, prayers were offered in mosques and meetings were held. In November 1919 an All-India Khilafat Conference was held in Delhi, in which a boycott of British goods and non-cooperation with the government were raised. (Sources differ about who presided over this conference — some name Gandhi, while Banglapedia names A. K. Fazlul Huq of Bengal.)

The main leaders of the movement were **Maulana Muhammad Ali** and **Maulana Shaukat Ali** — who were called the "Ali Brothers" — along with **Maulana Abul Kalam Azad**, **Hakim Ajmal Khan**, **Dr. M. A. Ansari** and **Hasrat Mohani**. In March 1920 a delegation led by Muhammad Ali went to England, but it received no assurance.

## The Alliance with Non-Cooperation

The **Treaty of Sèvres** of August 1920 put its seal on the partition of the Ottoman empire. Gandhi saw the Khilafat question as an opportunity for Hindu-Muslim unity and proposed non-violent non-cooperation in its support. The Khilafat Committee began non-cooperation from 1 August 1920; at the Calcutta special session of September 1920 and the Nagpur session of December 1920 the Congress also adopted the programme of non-cooperation, which combined three demands — redress of the Punjab wrongs, Khilafat and swaraj.

After this, in 1920–22, Hindus and Muslims were seen on one platform in meetings, boycotts and national educational institutions. In July 1921, at the Khilafat conference in Karachi, a resolution was passed that it was religiously improper for Muslims to serve in the British army. After this, in September 1921, the Ali Brothers were arrested and, after a trial at Karachi, were sentenced to two years.

## Weaknesses and Controversies

Some serious weaknesses of the movement also came to light. The **hijrat** (migration) of thousands of Muslims towards Afghanistan in 1920 failed sadly. In August 1921 the **Moplah rebellion** of Malabar, which was linked both to peasant discontent and to Khilafat propaganda, took the form of communal violence in many places. In February 1922, after the **Chauri Chaura** incident, Gandhi called off non-cooperation, which angered many Khilafat leaders.

Historians debate how appropriate it was to link a religious question with the national movement. Leaders such as **Muhammad Ali Jinnah** had opposed this mixing of religion in politics. On the other hand, many historians regard it as the first major example of broad Muslim participation in an anti-imperialist mass movement.

## The End

In Turkey, the nationalist movement under **Mustafa Kemal (Atatürk)** itself ended the old order — the sultanate was abolished in November 1922, the Turkish Republic was formed in 1923, and on **3 March 1924** the Turkish parliament abolished the office of the Caliphate itself. With the very institution for whose defence the movement was run no longer existing, the basis of the Khilafat movement in India ended.

## Significance

The Khilafat movement played a big role in making the Non-Cooperation Movement a truly all-India mass movement and linked Muslims, from cities to villages, with national politics. At the same time, after its end, communal tension rose in the 1920s, which made this movement an important chapter in the complex relationship of religion and nationalism in Indian politics.
