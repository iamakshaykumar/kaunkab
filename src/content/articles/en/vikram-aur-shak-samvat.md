## What Is a Samvat?

To write the date of an event, a fixed 'starting year' is needed — just as the Christian era is counted from the birth of Jesus Christ. In ancient India many such **samvats** (eras) were in use. Kings wrote their dates in these in their inscriptions and copper plates. Today historians fix the age of old inscriptions by relating these eras to the Christian era. The two most important of them are the **Vikram Samvat** and the **Shaka Samvat**.

## Vikram Samvat (57 BCE)

The Vikram Samvat is taken to begin in **57 BCE**, so its year number is about 57 more than the Christian year (56 more in the months before Chaitra). It is a **lunisolar** calendar: the months follow the moon, and by adding an **extra month** (adhika masa) 7 times roughly every 19 years it is kept in line with the seasons. In North India the new year is celebrated from Chaitra Shukla Pratipada, while in Gujarat it is celebrated from Kartika Shukla Pratipada (the day after Diwali).

Tradition links this era with **Vikramaditya**, the king of Ujjain, who defeated the Shakas. A Jain tale — the story of **Kalakacharya** — also tells of the Shakas and Vikramaditya. But historians do not regard the historicity of this king as proven. In older inscriptions this same era appears under the names **Krita**, **Malava** or simply 'Samvat' — for example, the Mandasor inscription of the time of Yashodharman, of 532 CE, gives its date in 'Malava Samvat 589'. The oldest known inscription bearing the name 'Vikrama' is from **Dholpur**, whose date works out to **842 CE**.

## Shaka Samvat (78 CE)

The Shaka Samvat begins in **78 CE**, so its year number is 78 less than the Christian year (79 less in the early months of the year). Who started it is disputed. It was earlier linked with the Kushan emperor **Kanishka**, but newer research places Kanishka's accession around 127 CE. Today many scholars link it with **Chashtana**, the Shaka satrap of Ujjain. The Western Kshatrapas were its earliest known users — from the time of Rudrasimha I (known dates about 178–197 CE) the year of this era is found on their silver coins. Later it was also called the 'Shalivahana Shaka', but the historian D. C. Sircar regarded its connection with the Satavahana king Shalivahana as historically wrong.

The Shaka Samvat spread very far. In the inscriptions of South India it was the most common era — the Aihole inscription of 634–635 CE gives its date in Shaka Samvat 556. It reached Southeast Asia, and the traditional calendar of the Indonesian island of Bali is still based on it today.

## The Gupta Era and Other Eras

- **Gupta era:** counted from 319–320 CE; most scholars link it with the accession of **Chandragupta I**. A pillar inscription of Chandragupta II at Mathura is dated 'Gupta Samvat 61'. The Maitraka kings of Saurashtra adopted it as the **Valabhi era**. In the 11th century Al-Biruni related it to Shaka Samvat 241.
- The **Kollam era** (825 CE) was used in Kerala and the **Bhaskarabda** in Assam.

## The National Calendar (1957)

After independence, to remove the confusion of different panchangs in the country, the Government of India formed the **Calendar Reform Committee** under the scientist **Meghnad Saha**. On its recommendation, from **22 March 1957** (Chaitra 1, Shaka 1879), the **national calendar based on the Shaka Samvat** came into force. It is a solar calendar: the year is of 365 days (366 in a leap year) and the lengths of the months are fixed. All India Radio, the Gazette of India and government notices give its date along with the Gregorian date.

## Reckoning Today

In 2026 CE, from Chaitra, Vikram Samvat 2083 is running, and from 22 March the national Shaka Samvat 1948. Although the Gregorian calendar dominates in government work, fasts and festivals, wedding auspicious times and the panchang still run by these ancient eras — proof of the long continuity of India's tradition of time-reckoning.
