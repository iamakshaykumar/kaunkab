## The Tangled Old Tax System

Before GST, India's web of indirect taxes was very complicated. The Central Government levied **central excise duty** on the manufacture of goods and **service tax** on services, while the states collected taxes such as **VAT** on sales, entertainment tax, entry tax and **octroi**. Every state had different rates and rules. Sending goods from one state to another also attracted **central sales tax**.

The biggest flaw of this system was "tax on tax" — tax paid at one stage was added to the price at the next stage and was taxed again. Long queues of trucks formed at check-posts on state borders. Traders had to file many kinds of returns. Economists had long argued that a uniform tax was needed to make the whole country one common market.

## From Idea to Constitutional Amendment

The idea of GST was under discussion for nearly two decades. In **1999–2000** the government of Prime Minister **Atal Bihari Vajpayee** began considering it, and a committee of state finance ministers chaired by West Bengal's finance minister **Asim Dasgupta** was given the task of designing a model. A task force headed by the economist **Vijay Kelkar** also recommended a comprehensive GST. In **2006** Finance Minister **P. Chidambaram** set a target of introducing GST by 2010, but no agreement could be reached between the Centre and the states over the sharing of revenue, and a bill brought in 2011 also failed to move forward.

On **19 December 2014** Finance Minister **Arun Jaitley** introduced a new constitutional amendment bill in the Lok Sabha. The Lok Sabha passed it in **May 2015**, but in the Rajya Sabha it went to a select committee and the debate dragged on. Finally, on **3 August 2016** the Rajya Sabha and on **8 August 2016** the Lok Sabha passed the amended bill. Ratification by at least half of the state legislatures was required — **Assam** was the first to approve it. After the assent of President **Pranab Mukherjee** on **8 September 2016**, it became the **Constitution (101st Amendment) Act, 2016**.

## The Structure of GST

This amendment for the first time gave both the Centre and the states the power to tax the same transaction simultaneously. That is why India's GST is called a **"dual GST"**:

- **CGST** — the Centre's share on a sale within a state.
- **SGST / UTGST** — the state's or union territory's share on the same sale.
- **IGST** — an integrated tax levied when goods or services move from one state to another, which is later divided between the Centre and the destination state.

The most important institution created was the **GST Council**, chaired by the **Union Finance Minister**, with the finance ministers of all states as members. It is this council that recommends tax rates, exemptions and rules. The Centre holds one-third and the states two-thirds of the voting weight in it, so it is regarded as an example of **cooperative federalism**.

At the start there were five main rate levels — **0%, 5%, 12%, 18% and 28%** — and on goods such as luxury items and tobacco a **compensation cess** was levied on top of 28%. The states feared that their revenue would fall, so the Centre promised to make good their losses for five years. Petrol, diesel, aviation fuel, natural gas, alcohol and electricity were kept outside GST. A technology platform called the **GST Network (GSTN)** was built to run the whole system online.

## The Midnight Launch

At midnight between **30 June and 1 July 2017**, GST was formally launched in the **Central Hall** of Parliament in the presence of President Pranab Mukherjee and Prime Minister **Narendra Modi**. The government called it a historic step towards **"One Nation, One Tax"**. Some opposition parties, including the Congress, boycotted the function; they said the tax structure was complex and the preparation incomplete. Jammu and Kashmir passed its own GST law a few days later, on **7 July 2017**. Every year **1 July** is observed as **GST Day**.

## Early Difficulties and Changes

In the first years small traders had to struggle with filing returns online, frequently changing rules and technical problems on the GSTN portal. Critics called the multi-rate structure complex and said that this change, coming only a few months after demonetisation, increased the pressure on the unorganised sector. The government said these were teething troubles and that the council was continuously making improvements. The council cut the rates on many items, brought simpler schemes for small taxpayers and introduced the **e-way bill** system for the movement of goods. Check-posts on state borders were removed on a large scale.

During the COVID-19 pandemic, when revenue fell, the question of paying compensation to the states became a major dispute between the Centre and the states. Later the demand to simplify the structure further grew stronger. On **3 September 2025** the GST Council approved a major rate reform, which came into force on **22 September 2025**. It removed the 12% and 28% rates, keeping mainly two rates of **5% and 18%**, and set a special rate of **40%** for some luxury and harmful goods.

## Significance and Legacy

GST created a new balance between the powers of the Centre and the states in India's tax history. The states gave up their independent right to levy many of their taxes and in return gained a system of shared decision-making. According to its supporters, it made the country one common market, increased oversight of tax evasion and widened the formal economy. Critics say that keeping petroleum products outside it, multiple rates for a long time and the burden of compliance have kept it from becoming a fully "simple tax". Even so, the **101st Amendment** and the **GST Council** are regarded as an important chapter in the history of economic reforms in independent India.
