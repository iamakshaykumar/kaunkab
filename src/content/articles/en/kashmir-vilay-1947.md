## An Undecided Princely State

When British rule ended in 1947, the princely states were given the option of joining India or Pakistan. The Dogra ruler of **Jammu and Kashmir**, **Maharaja Hari Singh**, was a Hindu, while most of the state's population was Muslim, and its borders touched both new countries. The Maharaja made no decision up to 15 August 1947. An arrangement was made to continue services such as post, telegraph and rail with Pakistan, which was called the "Standstill" agreement, although no formal document was signed for it. In August 1947 a revolt against the Maharaja's rule broke out in **Poonch**, and the supply of essential goods to the state began to be disrupted.

## The Tribal Attack and the Instrument of Accession

On **22 October 1947**, Pashtun tribal fighters from the North-West Frontier region entered the state and advanced towards Srinagar via **Muzaffarabad**. India said they had the support of Pakistani officers; Pakistan at first described it as a spontaneous action by the tribesmen. The attackers stayed for several days at **Baramulla** in looting and violence, which gave India time to respond.

The Maharaja asked India for military help, and India made accession a condition for it. The **Instrument of Accession** bears the date 26 October 1947, although historians differ about the actual date of its signing. Under it the state handed over **defence, foreign affairs and communications** to India. On 27 October, the Governor-General **Lord Mountbatten** accepted the accession and said in an accompanying letter that, after order was restored, the question of accession would be settled by "the will of the people". The same day Indian soldiers began arriving in Srinagar by aircraft. **Sheikh Abdullah**, leader of the National Conference, supported the accession. Pakistan's Governor-General **Jinnah** rejected the accession, calling it based on "fraud".

## Expansion of the War

The Indian Army defended Srinagar and pushed the attackers back to Baramulla and **Uri**. In November 1947 **Mirpur** fell into the attackers' hands. In the same month, in **Gilgit**, the Gilgit Scouts, led by the British officer **Major William Brown**, removed the Maharaja's governor and the region joined Pakistan. In May 1948 Pakistan's regular army also officially entered the war. In Baltistan the garrison at **Skardu** fell on 14 August 1948 after a long siege, while the Indian Army crossed the **Zoji La** pass, linked up with Kargil and Leh, and brought relief to **Poonch**, which had been besieged for over a year.

## The United Nations and the Ceasefire

India raised the matter in the **UN Security Council** on 1 January 1948. The UN Commission (UNCIP), in its resolution of 13 August 1948, set out the sequence of a ceasefire, withdrawal of forces and a plebiscite. The ceasefire came into effect on **1 January 1949**, and its terms were recorded in the resolution of 5 January 1949. The **Karachi Agreement** of July 1949 fixed the ceasefire line, and a UN Military Observer Group (UNMOGIP) was later formed to monitor it.

Casualty figures differ by source: on the Indian side about 1,100–1,500 soldiers were killed and 3,150–3,500 wounded, while about 6,000 Pakistani Army and tribal fighters are estimated to have been killed and 14,000 wounded.

## Outcome and Dispute

At the end of the war about two-thirds of the former princely state — the Kashmir Valley, Jammu and Ladakh — remained under Indian administration, and about one-third — today's "Azad Jammu and Kashmir" and Gilgit-Baltistan, which India calls "Pakistan-occupied Kashmir" — under Pakistan's control. The plebiscite was never held: each country kept blaming the other for not fulfilling the conditions for it. India's official position is that the accession is valid and complete, while Pakistan regards it as disputed and cites the UN resolutions. Many analysts regard the military outcome of the war as a stalemate.

## Significance

This was the first war between independent India and Pakistan, and it laid the foundation of the Kashmir dispute, which remained the central issue in relations between the two countries through the conflicts of 1965 and 1999. The ceasefire line of 1949, with some changes in the Shimla Agreement of 1972, became the **Line of Control**.
