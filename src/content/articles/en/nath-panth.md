## From Adinath to the Guru Lineage

The **Nath Panth** (Nath Sampradaya) is the tradition of Shaiva yogis that regards Lord Shiva as **Adinath** — the first Nath, that is, the first master. 'Nath' means master or protector. In this tradition, after Shiva, the **nine Naths** (Navnath) and the **eighty-four Siddhas** are spoken of, and the living guru is regarded as the most essential basis of practice.

According to scholars, this sect emerged as a recognisable order between about the 11th and 13th centuries. In it, Shaiva, Tantric, Buddhist Siddha and yoga currents appear blended together.

## Matsyendranath and Gorakhnath

Tradition regards **Matsyendranath** (Machhandarnath) as the first human guru and **Gorakhnath** (Goraksanath) as his disciple. Matsyendranath is linked with the 'Kaula' Tantric tradition, and a text like the 'Kaulajnana Nirnaya' is found under his name.

There is very little solid historical information about the lives of these two; most details are found in folk tales. In one famous tale, Gorakhnath brings his guru back from the 'Kadali Desh', a land of pleasure and luxury — lines such as "Wake up, Machhandar, Gorakh has come" became popular from this tale. The Encyclopaedia Britannica takes Gorakhnath to be probably of the 11th century and also says that Matsyendranath probably lived considerably before him; other scholars place Gorakhnath in the 12th–13th centuries. So their dates are only estimates.

## The Kanphata Yogis and the Twelve Panths

Gorakhnath is regarded as the founder of the organised, monastery-based form of the Nath yogis. The mark of these yogis is the large earrings (mudra or darshan) worn after piercing the middle part of the ear at initiation. For this reason they are called **Kanphata** yogis.

Tradition describes the sect as divided into **twelve panths**, although according to the scholar David Gordon White this was not a strict division of a single organisation. Among the Naths there are wandering ascetics as well as householder followers, who in many regions are called 'Jogis'.

## The Tradition of Hatha Yoga

The name of the Nath yogis is deeply connected with **hatha yoga** — the method of disciplining the mind through purification of the body, postures, breath control and mudras. Texts like the 'Goraksha Shataka', probably from 13th-century Maharashtra, are linked to the name of Gorakhnath. In the 15th century, Svatmarama's **Hatha Yoga Pradipika** began its list of masters with Matsyendra and Goraksha.

Even so, historians are cautious. The oldest known text of hatha yoga, the 'Amritasiddhi' (c. 11th century), comes from a Buddhist Tantric setting, so it is not right to regard all the methods of hatha yoga as only Gorakhnath's gift; scholars debate this.

## The Vernacular 'Bani' and Influence on the Saints

The Nath yogis expressed their ideas in the vernacular as well as in Sanskrit. The verses and sabads found under the name **Gorakhbani** are regarded as important specimens of early Hindi, and some works of the Naths are counted among the early works of Punjabi.

Their language, symbols and criticism of outward ritual are seen later in saints such as **Kabir** and **Namdev** too. The Sikh tradition's 'Siddha Gosht' describes the dialogue between Guru Nanak and the Siddha yogis.

## Legacy Today

The city of **Gorakhpur** in Uttar Pradesh and its **Gorakhnath Math** are a major centre of this tradition. In Nepal the Gorkha district and the name 'Gorkha' are linked to Gorakhnath, and there Matsyendranath is also worshipped as the Buddhist Avalokiteshvara. There are Nath monasteries and yogi communities in Rajasthan, Maharashtra and Punjab too. In this way the tradition remained a bridge between yoga, folk religion and devotional literature.
