## The Crossroads of Gandhara

Taxila lies in the Punjab province of present-day Pakistan, in Rawalpindi district. In ancient times it was the chief city of the **Gandhara** region. It stood at the meeting point of the routes that connected the Gangetic plain with Central Asia, Afghanistan and Iran, so merchants, soldiers, scholars and religious teachers kept coming and going here.

Archaeology shows that three cities were settled here over time:
- **Bhir Mound** — the oldest city, settled from about the sixth century BCE.
- **Sirkap** — a city laid out in a planned way by the Indo-Greek rulers in the second century BCE.
- **Sirsukh** — a city founded in the Kushan period.

## Among Empires

In the sixth-fourth centuries BCE this region was under the influence of the Achaemenid empire of Iran; an inscription in the Aramaic language has also been found here. In **326 BCE** **Ambhi**, the king of Taxila, accepted Alexander's overlordship without a war.

After this it became part of the **Mauryan empire** and was a provincial centre of the north-west. According to Buddhist tradition, the young **Ashoka** was sent here to quell a revolt. After the Mauryas, the Indo-Greek, Shaka, Parthian and Kushan rulers ruled here. In the Kushan period Buddhist monasteries such as the **Dharmarajika Stupa** and **Jaulian** flourished around Taxila, and many sculptures of Gandhara art were made.

## A Centre of Learning

Taxila's fame was especially due to its teachers. The **Jataka tales** repeatedly mention young men who, at about the age of sixteen, went from far and wide to Taxila to study under "world-renowned" (disapamokkha) teachers. Instruction in the Vedas, grammar, archery, medicine, law and other crafts is described here.
- According to the Pali Vinaya Pitaka, the famous physician **Jivaka**, a contemporary of the Buddha, studied medicine at Taxila for several years.
- According to tradition the grammarian **Panini** was from this region, and **Chanakya** (Kautilya) is also linked with Taxila, but these stories are not confirmed by contemporary evidence.

Historians hold that it is not right to call Taxila a "university" in the modern sense. No evidence has been found here, as at Nalanda, of a single organised campus, an admission system or a central administration; teachers generally taught students independently.

## Decline

In about 400 CE the Chinese traveller **Faxian** passed through this region. In the fifth century the invasions of the **Huns** did heavy damage to the Buddhist centres of Taxila and Gandhara. When **Xuanzang** came in the seventh century, he found it ruined and far less populated than before.

## Discovery and Taxila Today

In 1863–64 **Alexander Cunningham** identified this site as ancient Taxila. From 1913, for about twenty years, **John Marshall** carried out large-scale excavations here, in which the streets, houses, stupas, monasteries, coins and ornaments of the cities were found. In 1980 Taxila was declared a UNESCO World Heritage Site. Taxila reminds us how rich the knowledge tradition of ancient India became through trade and the blending of cultures.
