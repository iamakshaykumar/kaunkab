## Birth and Early Life

Guru Nanak was born in 1469 at **Rai Bhoi di Talwandi** near Lahore, now called **Nankana Sahib** (Pakistan). His father Mehta Kalu was the village patwari (accountant) and his mother's name was Tripta. His elder sister **Bebe Nanaki** held a special place in his life.

According to tradition his birth anniversary (Gurpurab) is celebrated on Kartik Purnima, while many early Janamsakhis and scholars mention April 1469. Much of the information about his life comes from the later-written **Janamsakhis**, which mix history and devotion.

## Sultanpur Lodhi and the Spiritual Experience

In his youth Nanak went to **Sultanpur Lodhi** to the house of Nanaki's husband Jai Ram and began working in the granary (modikhana) of Daulat Khan Lodhi. He married Sulakhani and had two sons, Sri Chand and Lakhmi Das.

According to Sikh tradition, after bathing in the Bein river at Sultanpur he disappeared for three days, and on returning began to give the message of the oneness of God. This experience is regarded as the beginning of his religious life.

## The Udasis: Long Journeys

After this Guru Nanak made journeys lasting many years, called **Udasis**. Tradition usually describes four major Udasis:
- in the east towards Haridwar, Kashi and Bengal-Assam,
- in the south as far as Sri Lanka,
- in the north towards the Himalayan region,
- in the west to Mecca, Medina and Baghdad.

On these journeys his companion was **Bhai Mardana**, who played the rabab while the Guru's hymns were sung. On the way he held discussions with yogis, sannyasis, Sufis, pandits and qazis. The exact route and order of the journeys are a matter of discussion among historians.

## Main Teachings

Guru Nanak's teachings were simple and practical:
- **Ik Onkar** — God is one, formless and present in all. His composition the 'Mool Mantar' begins with this idea.
- **Naam japna, kirat karna, vand chhakna** — remembering God, earning by honest labour and sharing it with others.
- Opposition to caste, high and low, and discrimination between men and women; stress on inner truth rather than outward show and ritual.
- A spiritual life is possible even while living as a householder — it is not necessary to renounce the world.

He also wrote about the political violence of his time; the suffering caused by Babur's invasions in the 1520s is described in that part of his hymns known as the 'Babarvani'.

## Kartarpur: Sangat and Langar

After his journeys Guru Nanak settled at **Kartarpur** on the bank of the Ravi River. Here he farmed himself, and a community took shape in which people sang kirtan together, worked together and ate in a shared kitchen (**langar**) sitting in one row. This tradition of sangat (collective gathering) and pangat (eating together) is still the hallmark of gurdwaras.

In his own lifetime he chose, instead of his sons, the devoted disciple **Bhai Lehna** as his successor, who came to be called **Guru Angad Dev**. Guru Nanak died at Kartarpur in 1539.

## Legacy

The line of Gurus that began with Guru Nanak continued through ten Gurus and developed into the Sikh religion. His hymns became the basis of the literary tradition of the Punjabi language and the Gurmukhi script. His message of equality, service and co-existence, while part of the wider stream of the Bhakti movement, has its own distinct identity.
