## 'Sitting Near' — The Meaning of the Word

The word "Upanishad" is traditionally linked with **upa** (near) + **ni** (down) + **shad** (to sit) — that is, knowledge received by sitting near a teacher. Some modern scholars take its original meaning to be "connection" or "equivalence". Shankaracharya called it **Atmavidya** or **Brahmavidya**.

The Upanishads are the last part of the Vedas, which is why they are also called **Vedanta** ("the end of the Veda"). Today more than 200 texts are found under the name "Upanishad". The Muktika Upanishad gives a list of 108 Upanishads, of which about 10 to 13 are considered the **principal Upanishads** — such as Brihadaranyaka, Chandogya, Taittiriya, Aitareya, Katha, Isha, Kena, Mundaka, Mandukya and Prashna.

## When Were They Composed?

The dates of the Upanishads are not certain. The oldest Upanishads are considered to be the **Brihadaranyaka** and **Chandogya**. The scholar Patrick Olivelle places them in about the seventh-sixth century BCE, while some estimates place them earlier and some later. Roughly, the period of the early Upanishads is taken to be **about 800 to 300 BCE** — the same period when the mahajanapadas were emerging in the Gangetic plain and thinkers such as the Buddha and Mahavira were raising new questions.

## Brahman and Atman

The centre of the Vedic Samhitas and Brahmana texts was **yajna** (sacrifice). The Upanishads go beyond this and ask — what is the fundamental truth of this world, and who am "I" really? They have two central ideas:

- **Brahman** — the supreme, unchanging basis of the whole universe.
- **Atman** — the true self within a person.

The conclusion of many Upanishads is that Atman and Brahman are fundamentally one. The Brihadaranyaka has the statement **"Aham Brahmasmi"** (I am Brahman), and Brahman is described as **"neti neti"** (not this, not this), because it is not bound by any word or quality. In the Chandogya the sage **Uddalaka Aruni** repeatedly explains to his son **Shvetaketu** — **"Tat tvam asi"** (That thou art). Later, different schools of Vedanta gave different interpretations of these statements.

Many Upanishads also criticise sacrificial ritual; the Chandogya even has a satirical description of a procession of dogs imitating priests. The ideas of karma, rebirth and moksha also begin to take clear shape in these texts.

## Janaka's Assembly: Yajnavalkya and Gargi

According to the Brihadaranyaka Upanishad, **Janaka**, king of Videha, performed a great sacrifice and announced that the greatest knower of Brahman would receive cows as a prize. The sage **Yajnavalkya** claimed the prize, and many scholars debated with him.

Among them the questions of **Gargi Vachaknavi** are the most famous. She asked: this world is woven (like warp and weft) in water, so in what is water? Then air, space, the worlds, the sun, the moon, the gods, Indra, Prajapati — one question after another. At the end she asked in what **space** is woven. Yajnavalkya answered — in the **Akshara** (the imperishable). In one episode Yajnavalkya even warns her not to question further. At the end Gargi admitted before the assembly that no one could defeat Yajnavalkya on the subject of Brahman.

The same Upanishad also has a dialogue between Yajnavalkya and his wife **Maitreyi**. Before taking up renunciation Yajnavalkya wants to divide his property, but Maitreyi asks whether wealth can give immortality — and then asks for knowledge about the Atman.

## Influence and Legacy

The Upanishads later became the basis of **Vedanta philosophy**. Badarayana's Brahma Sutra systematised their ideas, and teachers such as Shankara, Ramanuja and Madhva wrote commentaries on them. The **Bhagavad Gita** too bears a deep imprint of the Upanishads.

India's national motto **"Satyameva Jayate"** is taken from the Mundaka Upanishad, and the famous prayer "Asato ma sad gamaya" is in the Brihadaranyaka. Through the Persian translation of **Dara Shikoh** in 1657 and then the Latin translation (1801–1802) by the French scholar **Anquetil-Duperron**, the Upanishads reached Europe, where philosophers such as Schopenhauer greatly admired them.
