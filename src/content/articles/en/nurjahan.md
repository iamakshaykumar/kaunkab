## Born in Kandahar, Raised in the Mughal Court

**Mehr-un-Nisa** was born in **1577** in Kandahar, when her Iranian family was coming from Iran towards India. Her father **Mirza Ghiyas Beg** entered service in Akbar's court and gradually rose to a high post. Mehr-un-Nisa received an education in Persian literature, poetry and the arts.

In 1594 she was married for the first time to an Iranian military officer, **Ali Quli Istajlu**, who had received the title **Sher Afgan**. They had a daughter — **Ladli Begum**. In 1607, in a clash with Mughal officers in Bengal, Sher Afgan was killed, and the widowed Mehr-un-Nisa was sent to the royal harem at Agra.

## 1611: From Nur Mahal to Nur Jahan

In **1611** Emperor **Jahangir** married Mehr-un-Nisa and gave her the title **Nur Mahal** ("Light of the Palace"); a few years later, about **1616**, she came to be called **Nur Jahan** ("Light of the World").

Her whole family also rose with her. Her father received the title **Itimad-ud-Daula** ("Pillar of the State") and the post of wazir, and her brother **Asaf Khan** was counted among the most powerful nobles of the court. Asaf Khan's daughter **Arjumand Banu** (later Mumtaz Mahal) was married to Prince **Khurram**. In this way an influential group formed of Nur Jahan, her father, her brother and Khurram, which some historians call the "Nur Jahan junta" — although modern historians also question the overstating of its unity.

## Coins, Farmans and Power

Because of Jahangir's failing health and his habit of drinking and opium, Nur Jahan's role in governance kept growing. She received powers that no Mughal begum had received before:

- **Farmans** (royal orders) were issued in her name and the royal seal remained with her.
- In the 1620s, from mints such as Agra, Lahore, Patna, Surat and Ahmedabad, **coins** were struck bearing, along with Jahangir's, the name "Nur Jahan Padshah Begum". She is the only Mughal empress whose name appeared on coins.
- She was also active in trade — her ships and goods were engaged in foreign trade, and the English ambassador **Sir Thomas Roe** also recognised the power of her faction in the court.

Jahangir in his memoirs praises her marksmanship — he mentions her killing tigers while sitting on an elephant during hunts. The miniature with a musket given here is also linked to this image of hers.

## The Tomb of Itimad-ud-Daula

After her father's death in 1622, Nur Jahan had his tomb built on the eastern bank of the Yamuna in Agra, which was built between about **1622 and 1628**. It is regarded as the first Mughal tomb covered almost entirely in **white marble** and with such extensive use of fine **inlay work (pietra dura)** of coloured stones. Because it is small and delicate, it is also called the **"Baby Taj"**; it predates the Taj Mahal and many scholars regard it as a forerunner of the Taj's style. Nur Jahan also had caravanserais and gardens built.

## Conflict, Decline and Lahore

In the 1620s the struggle for succession divided the court. Nur Jahan supported Prince **Shahryar**, the husband of her daughter Ladli Begum, while Khurram rebelled in 1622–23. In **1626** the general **Mahabat Khan** took Jahangir captive near the Jhelum; Nur Jahan first made a military attempt and then cleverly helped in getting the emperor released.

After Jahangir's death in **1627**, Asaf Khan supported his son-in-law Khurram, who became emperor as **Shah Jahan**; Shahryar was killed. Nur Jahan's political role ended and she spent the rest of her life in **Lahore**, where she died in **1645**. Her tomb is at Shahdara near Lahore, close to Jahangir's tomb.

In Mughal history Nur Jahan is an extraordinary example — a woman who, though remaining behind the veil, left her indelible mark on the empire through coins, farmans and buildings.
