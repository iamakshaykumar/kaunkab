## The end of the Mauryas

After Ashoka's death the Maurya empire gradually grew weaker. In about **185 BCE** the last Mauryan king **Brihadratha** was killed by his own commander **Pushyamitra Shunga**. According to Banabhatta's *Harshacharita*, this happened while Pushyamitra was showing the king a review of the whole army. With this the rule of the **Shunga dynasty** began in Magadha, with **Pataliputra** as its main capital.

According to the Puranas Pushyamitra ruled for about 36 years, and according to the Vishnu Purana ten Shunga kings ruled for a total of 112 years.

## Pushyamitra and the Ashvamedha

According to the **inscription of Dhanadeva** found at Ayodhya, Pushyamitra performed **two Ashvamedha sacrifices**. Kalidasa's play *Malavikagnimitram* says that Pushyamitra's grandson **Vasumitra** defeated Yavana (Greek) cavalry on the banks of the "Sindhu" river, after which the Ashvamedha was completed. Whether this river was the Indus of Punjab or some river of central India is disputed among scholars. It is believed that in this period **Patanjali** wrote the *Mahabhashya*, the famous work on Sanskrit grammar.

Pushyamitra's son **Agnimitra** was the ruler of Vidisha and is the hero of this very play of Kalidasa.

## Persecution of Buddhists: how true?

The Buddhist text *Divyavadana* has a story that Pushyamitra destroyed Buddhist monasteries and put a bounty on the heads of monks. But many historians regard this as exaggerated. There is also evidence to the contrary: in the Shunga period the stupa at **Sanchi** was enlarged and the stupa at **Bharhut** was decorated. However, it is also possible that these monuments were built not by kings but through donations from merchants, monks and ordinary people.

## The art of Bharhut

The stupa at Bharhut in **Satna district** of Madhya Pradesh was perhaps first built in Ashoka's time. Its stone **railing** is dated to about 125–100 BCE and the eastern **gateway** (torana) to about 100–75 BCE. In 1873 **Alexander Cunningham** reached the site and excavated it the next year. Today a large part of its railing and gateway has been reassembled and is kept in the **Indian Museum, Kolkata**.

In the carvings of Bharhut the Buddha is not shown in human form but through symbols such as the Bodhi tree, the wheel of the dharma, an empty seat or footprints. There are figures of yakshas and yakshis and scenes from Jataka tales. On one pillar of the gateway is the donation inscription of **Dhanabhuti**, in which "Sugana raje" (in the reign of the Shungas) is written; this is why Bharhut is linked with the Shunga period, although some scholars read this phrase differently.

## The Heliodorus pillar

At **Besnagar** near Vidisha stands a stone pillar, which local people used to call "Kham Baba". According to the inscription on it, in **Brahmi script** and the **Prakrit** language, **Heliodorus, son of Dion**, a resident of Taxila, had it set up as a **Garuda standard** in honour of **Vasudeva** (God of gods). Heliodorus was the ambassador of the Indo-Greek king **Antialcidas**, who came to the court of King **Kashiputra Bhagabhadra** in the fourteenth year of his reign. Its date is believed to be about 110 BCE.

Whether Bhagabhadra was a king of the Shunga dynasty or a local ruler is disputed. Even so, the pillar shows that a Greek had adopted the Bhagavata (Vaishnava) faith, a unique proof of the cultural blending of India and the Greek world.

## End and importance

After Agnimitra the Shunga kingdom gradually began to break up. The last Shunga king, **Devabhuti**, was killed in about 73 BCE by his minister **Vasudeva Kanva**, who founded the Kanva dynasty. The Shunga period is an important link between the Mauryan and Gupta ages: in this period Vedic traditions rose again, Sanskrit received encouragement, and art such as that of Bharhut and Sanchi laid the foundation of later Indian sculpture.
