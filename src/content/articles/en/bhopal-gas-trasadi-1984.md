## The Factory and the Poisonous Gas

In 1969 **Union Carbide India Limited (UCIL)** set up a pesticide factory in **Bhopal**, the capital of Madhya Pradesh. The majority shareholding in this company was held by the American firm **Union Carbide Corporation (UCC)**. The plant made a pesticide called 'Sevin' (carbaryl), and for this an extremely poisonous chemical — **methyl isocyanate (MIC)** — was stored in large tanks.

The factory stood close to densely populated settlements. In the early 1980s the company was making losses as demand for pesticides fell, and cost-cutting measures were being taken. Later investigations revealed that several safety systems were not working that night — the refrigeration system that kept the MIC cool was switched off, and the **scrubber** that neutralised gas and the **flare tower** that burned off leaking gas were also not effective. Smaller accidents had already happened at the factory before this.

## The Night of 2–3 December 1984

Late on the night of **2 December 1984**, water got into **tank E610**, causing a violent chemical reaction with the MIC and a sharp rise in pressure and temperature inside the tank. From about **12:40 a.m.** the gas began to escape. In less than an hour about 30 tonnes, and about **40 tonnes** in all, spread into the air.

On the cold night this gas, heavier than air, drifted slowly close to the ground into the settlements south of the factory. Sleeping people woke up coughing, with severe burning in their eyes, breathlessness and vomiting. Thousands ran into the streets. Hospitals were overwhelmed, while doctors did not even clearly know which gas they were treating. By morning the bodies of people and animals lay scattered on the streets.

## Deaths and Damage

The immediate official death toll was given as **2,259**. Later the **Madhya Pradesh government** confirmed **3,787** deaths linked to the gas leak. Estimates by many social organisations and researchers are much higher — some put the deaths in the first few days alone at more than 8,000, apart from deaths from illness in later years. In **2006** a government affidavit recorded **558,125** injuries.

Among the gas victims, chronic respiratory diseases, eye problems, cancer and reproductive difficulties were seen for a long time. Allegations also kept being raised that chemical waste lying in the factory was polluting the surrounding land and groundwater.

## Compensation and the Legal Battle

In **March 1985** Parliament passed the **Bhopal Gas Leak Disaster (Processing of Claims) Act**, under which the Government of India took over the right to make claims on behalf of the victims. In **February 1989**, in a settlement overseen by the Supreme Court, Union Carbide Corporation agreed to pay **470 million US dollars**. Victims' organisations called the amount far too small; in **October 1991** the Supreme Court upheld the settlement.

Union Carbide chairman **Warren Anderson** was arrested in Bhopal on **7 December 1984**, but after getting bail he left the country and never again appeared before an Indian court; he died in the United States in **2014**. In **2001** the American company **Dow Chemical** bought Union Carbide Corporation, which started a new debate over responsibility. In **June 2010** a Bhopal court convicted seven former UCIL officials of causing death by negligence and sentenced each to two years; critics called this sentence far too light compared with the scale of the tragedy. The central government's **curative petition** for additional compensation was dismissed by the Supreme Court on **14 March 2023**.

## Disposal of the Toxic Waste

Even after the factory closed, hundreds of tonnes of chemical waste lay there for decades. After a long legal process, on the direction of the Madhya Pradesh High Court, about **337 tonnes** of waste was taken in 12 sealed containers to a disposal plant at **Pithampur** (Dhar district) on **2 January 2025**. Amid protests by local people and trial runs, it was burned in phases, and the work was declared complete by the end of **June 2025**. According to officials, emissions stayed within the prescribed limits.

## Legacy

The Bhopal gas tragedy exposed the weaknesses of industrial safety and environmental laws in India. After it, the **Environment (Protection) Act** was passed in **1986** and rules for hazardous industries were tightened. The Supreme Court later also advanced the concept of 'absolute liability' for hazardous industries. Even today Bhopal's gas victims demand better treatment, fair compensation and clean water. Around the world, this tragedy is a reminder that ordinary people pay with their lives for laxity in industrial safety.
