## The Vacuum After Harsha

Around 647 CE **Harshavardhana** died without an heir. After this, for several decades **Kannauj**, which had become the most prestigious capital of North India, did not remain in the hands of any major power. Very little is known about this period.

In the first half of the 8th century, in this very empty space, **Yashovarman** rose. **Almost nothing is known for certain** about his family and ancestors. Alexander Cunningham suggested linking him with the Maukharis, but there is no solid evidence for this. He is regarded as the first king of the 'Varman dynasty' of Kannauj.

## Dates of His Reign

Yashovarman's dates are **disputed**. Vincent Smith placed his rule at about 728–745 CE, while R. S. Tripathi placed it at about **725–752 CE**. Some older scholars placed him even earlier. Broadly, he is regarded as a ruler from the second-third decade to the middle of the 8th century.

## Gaudavaho: Poetry Versus History

The biggest source about Yashovarman is the **Prakrit** epic **'Gaudavaho'** (the slaying of the Gauda king) by his court poet **Vakpatiraja**. It tells how Yashovarman undertook a conquest of the quarters — killed the king of Magadha and Gauda (Bengal), then conquered the kingdoms of Vanga, the south and the west.

Historians read this description **with caution**. In Tripathi's words, many of its exploits seem "more fancy than sober history". The poem has little fact and more figures of speech, nature description and praise of the king. Even so, it indicates that Yashovarman certainly carried out some major campaign to the east, up to Magadha and Bengal.

## Patron of Literature

Yashovarman's court is known for literature. Besides Vakpati, the great Sanskrit dramatist **Bhavabhuti** — author of 'Malatimadhava', 'Mahaviracharita' and 'Uttararamacharita' — is also linked with his court. In Kalhana's **Rajatarangini** too, both of these poets are mentioned in connection with Yashovarman.

Very few of his own inscriptions or monuments survive. An inscription found at **Nalanda** and some coins are linked with him, but this identification is **not certain**. It is said that he built a temple at Harishchandranagari (today's Ayodhya), but there is no firm evidence for this either.

## Clash with Lalitaditya

According to R. C. Majumdar, Yashovarman sent an envoy to the Tang court of China in **731 CE**, and at first he was friendly with **Lalitaditya Muktapida** of Kashmir. This friendship broke down around **740 CE**.

According to the **Rajatarangini**, Lalitaditya defeated Yashovarman and the power of Kannauj bowed before Kashmir. Most historians regard this defeat as **real**, but there is disagreement about what happened after it — Tripathi sees contradictions in Kalhana's account, and holds that Yashovarman probably remained on the throne as a nominal subordinate king.

In Jain accounts his son **Ama** is described as his successor, but this is not confirmed by other sources. Some scholars regarded the later Ayudha kings as his descendants, but there is no evidence for this either.

## Significance

Yashovarman is the link between Harsha and the later Pratihara-Pala-Rashtrakuta age. In his time Kannauj again became that 'capital of prestige' for the possession of which the **tripartite struggle** was later fought. At the same time 'Gaudavaho' is an important work of Prakrit literature, and it is a reminder of how much care is needed in reading court poetry as history.
