## Background: Born from the Bahmani Sultanate

The **Bahmani Sultanate** of the Deccan had grown weak by the end of the fifteenth century and its governors began to become independent. The governor of the Telangana region, **Sultan Quli**, was a Turkmen originally from the Hamadan region of Iran. From about 1501 he was ruling from Golconda, and in **1518** he laid the foundation of the practically independent **Qutb Shahi Sultanate**. It was one of the five sultanates of the Deccan. Sultan Quli lived until 1543; according to tradition he was murdered.

## Golconda Fort

Golconda Fort was originally a small fort of the **Kakatiya** rulers, which passed through the hands of the Bahmanis to the Qutb Shahis. The Qutb Shahis turned it into a huge fortress with several ramparts on a granite hill.

- At the top of the hill is the **Bala Hisar** pavilion.
- The acoustic system of the entrance, the **Fateh Darwaza**, is famous — a clap here can be heard at the top, so a warning of danger could travel quickly.
- About a kilometre north of the fort are the **Qutb Shahi tombs**, where the sultans of this dynasty are buried.

## Hyderabad and the Charminar

The fifth sultan, **Muhammad Quli Qutb Shah** (reigned 1580–1612), founded a new capital, **Hyderabad**, on the bank of the Musi River, outside the crowded Golconda. The city was planned by his prime minister **Mir Momin Astarabadi**.

At the centre of the new city the **Charminar** was built in **1591** — a structure with four tall minarets and four huge arches, with a mosque on its upper floor.

- Popular tradition says it was built in memory of the end of a **plague** epidemic that had spread in the region.
- Some historians link it to the completion of the first millennium of the Hijri calendar (1000 AH).
- A folk tale connects the old name of Hyderabad, "Bhagnagar", to the sultan's beloved **Bhagmati**, but the historical evidence for this is weak.

## Diamonds, Ports and Culture

A major basis of the Qutb Shahi Sultanate's prosperity was **diamonds**. Diamonds from mines such as **Kollur** near the Krishna River were sold at Golconda, so "Golconda diamonds" became famous worldwide. Famous diamonds like the **Koh-i-Noor** and the **Hope** are also believed to have come from this region. Foreign trade was carried on through the port of **Machilipatnam**.

Culturally, this court patronised both Persian and Telugu. **Ibrahim Quli** (reigned 1550–1580) gave shelter to Telugu poets, and later Telugu also became a language of administration. Muhammad Quli lifted the restriction on openly celebrating Hindu festivals and himself wrote poetry in Dakhni. In the battle of **Talikota** in 1565, Golconda was also among the Deccan sultanates that defeated Vijayanagara.

## Mughal Pressure and Decline

In 1636 Shah Jahan forced Golconda to accept Mughal overlordship. In the time of the last sultan, **Abul Hasan** "Tana Shah" (reigned 1672–1687), the Brahmin minister brothers **Madanna** and **Akkanna** were influential in the administration.

In **1687** **Aurangzeb** besieged Golconda Fort. After a siege of about **eight months** the fort fell — according to popular accounts, a gate was opened at night through the treachery of an officer. Abul Hasan was taken prisoner and sent to Daulatabad, and Golconda became a province of the Mughal Empire.

## Legacy

The Qutb Shahi Sultanate, which lasted about 170 years, blended Persian and local Telugu traditions to create a distinct culture in the Deccan. Today the Charminar is the symbol of Hyderabad, and Golconda Fort and the Qutb Shahi tombs are counted among the country's major historical sites.
