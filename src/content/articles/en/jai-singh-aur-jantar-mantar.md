## The Young Ruler of Amer

Jai Singh II was born on **3 November 1688**. After his father's death in 1699 he became ruler of the Kachwaha kingdom of Amer at about eleven years of age. The Kachwaha Rajputs had been close allies of the Mughal court since Akbar's time.

According to sources, during a campaign in the Deccan in 1702 (the siege of Khelna/Vishalgarh), **Aurangzeb** gave him the title **'Sawai'**, meaning 'one and a quarter' — that is, a quarter better than his contemporaries. In the unstable period after Aurangzeb, Jai Singh served under several Mughal emperors and was also governor of Malwa.

## Jaipur: A Planned City

Amer, the old capital surrounded by hills, was becoming too small for the growing population and trade. Jai Singh decided to found a new city on the plain. Construction of the city began around 1725 and the formal foundation was laid in **1727**; Jaipur's founding day is celebrated every year on **18 November** (some sources give another date).

The city was planned by the scholar **Vidyadhar Bhattacharya**, who came from Bengal. The city was laid out with wide straight streets, a grid-like pattern and separate blocks (chowkris), in which principles of traditional Indian architectural treatises are believed to have been used. By about 1733 Jaipur had taken the place of Amer as the capital. Today the walled city of Jaipur is also a UNESCO World Heritage Site.

## Interest in Astronomy

Jai Singh was a deep student of mathematics and astronomy. In his time there were errors in almanacs and tables of planetary calculation. He had Sanskrit, Arabic-Persian and European astronomical texts brought in and formed a team of scholars. The planetary table prepared under his patronage, the **'Zij-i-Muhammad Shahi'**, was named after the emperor **Muhammad Shah**.

His thinking was that, compared with small metal instruments, huge instruments of brick and stone could give more stable and accurate measurements.

## The Five Jantar Mantars

Jai Singh built five observatories in North India, completed between about 1724 and 1735:

- **Delhi** — the earliest, built around **1724** (the old plaque says 1710, which later research has held to be wrong)
- **Jaipur** — the largest, built around 1728–1734
- **Ujjain**, **Varanasi** and **Mathura** — the Mathura observatory no longer exists

These contain instruments such as the **Samrat Yantra** (huge sundial), **Jai Prakash Yantra**, **Ram Yantra** and **Mishra Yantra**, with which time, the position of the Sun, the altitude of stars and eclipses were calculated.

## Legacy

Jai Singh died on **21 September 1743**. His instruments were even then based on somewhat older technology — in Europe the telescope had by then become common — so their scientific influence remained limited. Even so, they are symbols of a unique blend of science, architecture and town planning in eighteenth-century India.

The Jantar Mantar at Jaipur, with about twenty major permanent instruments, was added to the UNESCO World Heritage List in **2010**. Even today some instruments work, and astrologers and students can be seen making calculations with them.
