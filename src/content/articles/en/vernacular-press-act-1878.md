## Background — A Growing Indian Press

By the middle of the nineteenth century, **newspapers in Indian languages** were growing rapidly in Bengal, Bombay, Madras and north India. These papers in Bengali, Hindi, Urdu, Marathi, Gujarati and Tamil reached ordinary literate people and commented openly on government policies.

In **1876** **Lord Lytton** arrived as Viceroy. During his time several decisions were taken that Indian newspapers sharply criticised — the **Great Famine of 1876–78**, in which millions died; in the middle of it the **Delhi Durbar of 1877**, whose grandeur was questioned; the removal of import duties on cotton; and preparations for the **Second Anglo-Afghan War**. The government felt that the Indian-language newspapers were spreading discontent among the people. In the eyes of British officials, English newspapers were mainly limited to European and educated readers, but papers in Indian languages could reach villages and small towns and shape the opinion of ordinary people.

## How the Law Was Made

Lytton had a bill drafted on the model of the press laws of Ireland. On **14 March 1878** the Viceroy's council passed it unanimously in a single sitting. There had been no public discussion of it beforehand, nor was Indian opinion consulted. Its full name was "An Act for the better control of Publications in Oriental Languages".

## Main Provisions

- The law applied only to newspapers in **"Oriental" (Indian) languages**; **English newspapers were outside it**.
- A **district magistrate** could require the printer and publisher of any Indian-language newspaper to give a written undertaking (bond) that they would not publish any material spreading disaffection against the government or enmity between different castes and religions.
- The magistrate could demand a **security deposit**. For breaking the rules there would first be a warning, then forfeiture of the security, and for repeated violations the **printing press and materials could be confiscated**.
- **No appeal** against the magistrate's decision could be made **in any court**.
- A newspaper wishing to avoid these conditions had to show its proofs to a government censor before publication.

## The Cleverness of Amrita Bazar Patrika, and the Protests

Bengal's newspapers were seen as the special target of this law. The most famous episode is that of the **Amrita Bazar Patrika**. Run by **Sisir Kumar Ghosh** and **Motilal Ghosh**, the paper was then printed in both Bengali and English. To escape the law it became an **entirely English** weekly **within a week**, because the law did not apply to English papers.

Action was taken against several newspapers; papers such as **Som Prakash**, Bharat Mihir, Dacca Prakash and Samachar were caught in its grip. **Dwarkanath Vidyabhushan**, the editor of Som Prakash, chose to close his paper for a time rather than give a bond.

There were protests across the country. The **Indian Association**, led by **Surendranath Banerjee**, was among its biggest critics. Protest meetings were held at the Town Hall in Calcutta, attended by people of every religion and caste. Critics argued that the law openly discriminated between the Indian and English press and crushed civil rights by closing the path to the courts. Liberal members of the British Parliament also raised questions about it. People called it the **"Gagging Act"**.

## Amendment, Repeal and Legacy

Because of the protests, the condition of showing proofs in advance was dropped in **October 1878**, but the security system remained. In 1880 **Gladstone's** Liberal government came to power in Britain and **Lord Ripon** became Viceroy. Ripon's government decided in **1881** to withdraw the law, and in **1882** it was formally repealed.

The Vernacular Press Act lasted only four years, but its effect was long-lasting. It showed Indians that the government feared their languages and their ideas, and the opposition to it helped bring the educated classes of different provinces onto one platform. The controversies of Lytton's time made the need for political organisation even clearer, the result of which was later seen in the formation of the **Indian National Congress** in **1885**. The question of press freedom kept arising again and again after this, with the press laws of 1908 and 1910.
