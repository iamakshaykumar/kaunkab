## What Was the Problem?

There was open racial discrimination in the judicial system of British India. According to a change made in the Criminal Procedure Code in **1872**, outside the Presidency cities such as Calcutta, Bombay and Madras — that is, in the **mofussil** (rural districts) — no Indian magistrate or sessions judge could hear a criminal case against a **European British subject**, however senior he might be in rank.

By the 1870s some Indians had entered the **Indian Civil Service (ICS)** and begun to reach high judicial posts. This created an odd situation in which a senior Indian judge had less authority than his junior English colleague. It is generally believed that the ICS officer **Behari Lal Gupta** drew the attention of the Bengal government to this anomaly in **1882**.

## Ripon and Ilbert's Bill

The Viceroy at that time was **Lord Ripon** (1880–1884), who was influenced by the ideas of Britain's Liberal prime minister **Gladstone** and is known for reforms such as local self-government. **Sir Courtenay Ilbert**, the law member of his council, prepared a bill so that senior Indian district magistrates and sessions judges could also try European accused. Ilbert sought leave to introduce it on **2 February 1883** and it was formally introduced on **9 February 1883**.

## The Fierce Opposition of the British

The European community living in India, especially the **planters** and merchants of **Bengal**, opposed the bill fiercely. Their argument was that a "native" judge could not pass judgment on an Englishman — this was open racial thinking.

- The **European and Anglo-Indian Defence Association** was formed in Calcutta.
- English newspapers controlled by European merchants ran a sharp campaign, and the **Chambers of Commerce** of Calcutta, Bombay and Madras joined the opposition.
- Meetings, memorials and delegations to London were organised. Among the opponents were many European women, who spread fear by saying that English women would not be safe before Indian judges.
- Some government officials and army officers were also quiet supporters of this opposition.
- The controversy reached Britain. People such as the former colonial administrator **Sir Henry Bartle Frere** warned that the bill would stir up "dangerous racial hatred" — that is, they described the removal of discrimination itself as a danger.

## The Reaction of Indians

Educated Indians supported the bill. Meetings were held, memorials were sent and articles were written in Indian newspapers; some Indian women also gave petitions in its favour. But according to sources such as Banglapedia, the Indians' campaign was less organised than the European one.

## The Compromise (January 1884)

Under heavy pressure Ripon had to yield. In a modified form the bill was passed on **25 January 1884** as the **Criminal Procedure Code Amendment Act 1884** and came into force on **1 May 1884**. According to it, Indian district magistrates and sessions judges could hear cases against European accused, but the accused had the right to demand a **jury** in which **at least half the members were European**. No such condition applied before an English judge — that is, discrimination continued in a new form. In this way the original aim of the bill was weakened to a large extent.

## Impact on National Consciousness

The Ilbert Bill controversy showed educated Indians clearly where the limits of the promise of equality under British rule lay, and also that government policy could be changed by organised agitation — as the British themselves demonstrated. In the same year, **1883**, at the initiative of the **Indian Association** of **Surendranath Banerjee** and others, the first **Indian National Conference** was held in Calcutta in December. Historians count this controversy among the causes that prepared the ground, two years later in **1885**, for the founding of the **Indian National Congress**.
