## A Unique Pillar

In **Mehrauli**, Delhi, inside the **Qutb complex**, in the courtyard of the Quwwat-ul-Islam mosque, stands a dark brown-black metal pillar. It looks ordinary, but metallurgists around the world regard it with wonder. Although it has stood under the open sky for about **1,600 years**, it has not rusted the way ordinary iron does within a few years.

The total length of this **iron pillar** is about **7.21 metres**, of which about one metre is underground. Its diameter is about **41 centimetres** and its weight is estimated at **more than six tons**. On top of the pillar is a carved capital shaped like a bell. It is believed that a statue of Garuda once stood at the very top, because the inscription calls it the 'standard' of Vishnu.

## The Inscription of 'Chandra'

On the pillar, in Sanskrit and in the Gupta-period Brahmi script, is engraved a eulogy inscription of three verses. In **1838** **James Prinsep** published its text and translation, although the king's name 'Chandra' was read correctly only in 1875, in the edition of Bhau Daji Lad. The inscription gives no date. It praises a mighty king named **Chandra**, of whom it says that:

- he defeated enemies in battle in the lands of **Vanga** (Bengal);
- he crossed the seven mouths of the **Indus River** and conquered the **Vahlikas**;
- he was a devotee of Vishnu and had this standard-pillar of Vishnu set up on a hill called **Vishnupada**.

Who was this 'Chandra'? On the basis of the script, style and the description in the inscription, most historians consider him the Gupta emperor **Chandragupta II Vikramaditya** (about 375-415 CE). Still, this identification is not completely certain; some scholars have suggested other kings named 'Chandra'. It is also a matter of debate whether the inscription was written during the king's lifetime or after his death.

## How Did It Reach Delhi?

The pillar was not originally made in Delhi. There are several views on where the 'Vishnupada' hill of the inscription was — **Udayagiri** (near Vidisha), Mathura, or some other place. The most arguments are given in favour of Udayagiri, because there are inscriptions of Chandragupta II there, and inscriptions there also call that hill 'Vishnupadagiri'.

Who brought it to Delhi is also disputed. **Alexander Cunningham**, on the basis of a small later inscription on the pillar, linked it with the Tomar king **Anangpal**, but not everyone accepts this interpretation of that inscription. Other scholars hold that in the 13th century Sultan **Iltutmish** had it erected here as a victory symbol. Whatever the truth, today it is an integral part of the Qutb complex.

## Why Did It Not Rust?

The ironsmiths who made it did not cast it; they made it by **hammering and joining** pieces of hot iron (forge welding). It is about **98% pure wrought iron**, with very little carbon.

**R. Balasubramaniam**, a metallurgist at **IIT Kanpur**, and his colleagues carried out detailed research on it in the early 2000s. According to their studies:

- The **phosphorus** content in the pillar's iron is on average about **0.25%**, whereas in modern blast-furnace iron it is less than 0.05%. This resulted from the fuel and the furnace method of that time.
- Because of this phosphorus, a very thin, dense **protective layer** slowly formed on the surface of the iron — reported to include an iron phosphate compound and an iron oxy-hydroxide called '**misawite**'. This layer stops moisture and oxygen from reaching the iron inside.
- In 1,600 years this layer has grown only about **one-twentieth of a millimetre** thick.

The repeated wetting and drying of the surface with the weather is also believed to help in forming this layer. It is worth noting that this extraordinary resistance is said mainly for the upper part of the pillar, exposed to the air.

## Legacy

The iron pillar shows how skilled the blacksmiths of ancient India were at making high-quality iron on a large scale and giving it huge shapes. Its inscription is also an important source for the political history of the Gupta period. This is why the pillar remains a subject of equal fascination for historians and scientists alike.
