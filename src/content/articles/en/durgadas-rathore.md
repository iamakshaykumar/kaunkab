## Jaswant Singh's Death and the Succession Crisis

Durgadas Rathore was born in **1638** in the home of **Askaran Rathore**, a petty jagirdar of Marwar. He served Maharaja **Jaswant Singh** of Marwar, who was a senior mansabdar of the Mughal Empire.

In **December 1678**, Jaswant Singh died at **Jamrud** on the Afghan frontier. At that time he had no living son. Aurangzeb handed Marwar over to his officers and jagirdars. The historian John Richards regards this as an administrative step more than a permanent annexation, but the Rathores saw it as the seizure of their state. Soon after, in 1679, two sons were born to Jaswant Singh's queens, one of whom died soon. The surviving infant, **Ajit Singh**, was regarded by the Rathore chiefs as the true heir.

## The Dramatic Escape from Delhi

The Rathore chiefs demanded that Ajit Singh be recognised as king. According to the sources, Aurangzeb set the condition that the child would be brought up in the Mughal court and grow up as a Muslim. In 1679, when Mughal officers came to take the child into their custody, Durgadas and his companions fought their way out in Delhi and brought Ajit Singh safely to Marwar. Many Rathore warriors were killed in this struggle. Folk tales also say that another infant was handed over to the Mughals in place of the real child; this should be regarded as tradition only.

## The Rathore War and Prince Akbar

Ajit Singh's mother sought help from **Rana Raj Singh** of Mewar, and thus a Rathore-Sisodia alliance stood against the Mughals. In open battle the Mughal army was too strong, so Durgadas adopted guerrilla warfare in the hills and the desert.

In 1681 Durgadas encouraged Aurangzeb's son **Prince Akbar** to claim the throne against his father. But through Aurangzeb's stratagems this rebellion soon collapsed. Durgadas then took Akbar to Chhatrapati **Sambhaji** in the Deccan, which increased Aurangzeb's worries further, and he himself moved towards the Deccan. Akbar later went to Iran, but his children remained under Durgadas's protection, and he returned them to Aurangzeb after negotiations in the late 1690s.

## The Return to Jodhpur

For about twenty to twenty-five years Mughal officers controlled Marwar, and Rathore fighters kept harassing them continuously. After Aurangzeb's death in **1707**, taking advantage of the Mughal war of succession, Ajit Singh and Durgadas recaptured **Jodhpur**. Around 1710 the emperor Bahadur Shah I recognised Ajit Singh as the king of Jodhpur.

## The Last Years

After the victory, relations between Ajit Singh and Durgadas worsened, and the king exiled his old protector from Marwar. Durgadas received a jagir from Rana Amar Singh II of Mewar and spent the last years of his life outside Marwar. He died on **22 November 1718** at **Ujjain**, on the bank of the Kshipra river.

## Legacy

In the folk memory of Rajasthan, Durgadas is a symbol of loyalty and sacrifice: a commander who, though not a king, kept a state alive for three decades and, even after victory, did not seek power for himself. Historians regard this struggle as part of that phase of Aurangzeb's rule when strained relations with the Rajput states weakened the military and political strength of the Mughal Empire.
