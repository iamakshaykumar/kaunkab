## Background: Resistance to Persecution

In the latter half of the 17th century, religious oppression of the Kashmiri Pandits and the general public increased because of the narrow policies of the Mughal emperor Aurangzeb. When the Kashmiri Pandits appealed for protection to the ninth Guru, **Guru Tegh Bahadur**, Guru Tegh Bahadur sacrificed his life at Chandni Chowk in Delhi (1675 CE) but did not give up his faith.

At that time his son **Guru Gobind Singh** was only nine years old. He realised that tyrants cannot be stopped by spiritual teachings alone; courage to take up the sword is necessary to defend dharma and justice. He wrote:
> *"Chun kar az hama hilate dar guzasht, halal ast burdan ba shamshir dast."*
> (When all peaceful paths of justice are closed, then it is entirely proper and righteous to take up the sword in hand.)

## 13 April 1699: The Day of Baisakhi at Anandpur Sahib

About 80,000 people of the sangat had gathered in the vast ground at Anandpur Sahib. Suddenly Guru Gobind Singh came out of the tent brandishing a naked sword and said in a thundering voice:
> *"Is there any true Sikh here who can give his head for dharma and truth?"*

In the silence, five devotees came forward one by one:
1. **Daya Ram** (a Khatri of Lahore),
2. **Dharam Das** (a Jat of Hastinapur/Delhi),
3. **Himmat Rai** (a fisherman/water-carrier of Jagannath Puri, Odisha),
4. **Mohkam Chand** (a tailor of Dwarka, Gujarat),
5. **Sahib Chand** (a barber of Bidar, Karnataka).

Each time the Guru took one person into the tent and came out with a blood-stained sword (the Guru had tested their loyalty by sacrificing a goat). In the end he brought out all five, dressed in new clothes and equipped with weapons, and declared them the **"Panj Pyare" (Five Beloved Ones)**.

## The Khande di Pahul (the Amrit Ceremony)

The Guru took the holy water of the Sutlej in an iron bowl. His wife Mata Jito put sweets (patashe) into it. The Guru stirred the water with a double-edged khanda (sword) while reciting Gurbani and prepared the **"amrit"**.

- This amrit was given to the five Beloved Ones to drink, and all old castes, lineages, occupations and superstitions were abolished.
- All men were given the title **"Singh" (lion)** and women the title **"Kaur" (princess)**.
- The **Five Ks** were made compulsory for every Khalsa:
  1. **Kesh:** the natural form given by God,
  2. **Kangha:** a symbol of cleanliness and order,
  3. **Kara:** an iron bangle of discipline and resolve,
  4. **Kachera:** character and self-restraint,
  5. **Kirpan:** a weapon for protecting the weak and for self-defence against injustice.

The Guru himself bowed his head before these Panj Pyare and begged for amrit, which proved that in the Khalsa there is no high or low, nor any distinction between guru and disciple.

## The Battle of Chamkaur and the Zafarnama

Frightened by the growing power of the Khalsa, the hill rajas and the Mughal army besieged Anandpur Sahib.
- **The fortress of Chamkaur (1704):** Only 40 Sikhs stoutly faced a Mughal army of thousands. The Guru's two elder sons — Baba Ajit Singh (17 years) and Baba Jujhar Singh (14 years) — were martyred fighting on the battlefield.
- Wazir Khan, the Nawab of Sirhind, had the younger sons — Baba Zorawar Singh (9 years) and Baba Fateh Singh (7 years) — bricked alive into a wall, but they did not give up their faith.
- From Dina Kangar, Guru Gobind Singh sent a historic letter in Persian to the Mughal emperor Aurangzeb, called the **"Zafarnama" (Letter of Victory)**. In it he exposed Aurangzeb's false promises and moral defeat.

## Historical Significance

The founding of the Khalsa Panth filled a society that had been crushed for centuries with such indomitable self-respect and valour that *"I will make sparrows fight hawks, I will turn jackals into lions, I will make one fight a hundred and twenty-five thousand, only then will I be called Gobind Singh."* In time this same Khalsa, under Maharaja Ranjit Singh, built a vast and secular Sikh empire.
