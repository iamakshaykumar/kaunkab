## From Khwarazm to Ghazni

Abu Rayhan Muhammad ibn Ahmad **al-Biruni** was born in 973 CE in the town of Kath in the **Khwarazm** region of Central Asia (today's Uzbekistan). In his youth he had already become a serious scholar of mathematics, astronomy, geography and medicine, and he knew several languages besides Arabic and Persian.

In 1017 CE **Mahmud of Ghazni** took over Khwarazm. Along with many scholars and poets of the place, Al-Biruni was also taken to Ghazni; some scholars believe he went almost as a captive. At Ghazni he remained attached to the court and, during Mahmud's campaigns, came to the north-western parts of India, especially the regions of **Punjab**.

## Learning Sanskrit and Writing the Book

To understand India, Al-Biruni did what few foreigners of that time did — he learned **Sanskrit** and sat with Brahmin scholars to read texts. He translated several Sanskrit works into Arabic, chief among them **Patanjali's Yoga Sutras**. According to NCERT, he also translated Euclid's geometry into Sanskrit for his Brahmin friends.

Around **1030 CE** he completed his famous work in Arabic, whose full title is *Tahqiq ma li-l-Hind* and which is commonly called the **Kitab ul-Hind** or Tarikh-ul-Hind. It is a huge work divided into about 80 chapters.

## What Is in the Kitab ul-Hind

The book writes on almost every aspect of Indian life:
- Religion and philosophy — Indian ideas about God, the soul, rebirth and liberation
- Astronomy, mathematics, chronology and the calendar
- Festivals, customs, marriage and food
- The varna system, law, geography, weights and measures, and sculpture

What was special about Al-Biruni's style was that he first set out Indian thought as it appears in the Sanskrit texts, and then compared it with Greek or Islamic ideas. He wrote that he would place the doctrines of the Hindus before the reader "as they are". To explain the varna system he compared it with the four social classes of ancient Persia, but he described the notion of impurity (untouchability) as contrary to the laws of nature.

## Obstacles to Understanding and Frank Criticism

Al-Biruni honestly admitted that understanding India was not easy. He listed several obstacles — the difficulty of the Sanskrit language, differences in religious beliefs, and the distrust of local people towards outsiders. He also wrote that the devastation caused by invasions had made Indians suspicious of foreigners; his book also records that Mahmud's campaigns destroyed the prosperity of the country.

His criticism went in both directions. He commented on the self-centred attitude of Indian scholars, their lack of interest in historical writing and the careless copying of texts by scribes, but he also openly praised Indian mathematics and astronomy.

## Limitations and Legacy

Historians point out that Al-Biruni's India is mainly India seen through the eyes of **Sanskrit texts and Brahmin scholars**. Many of his descriptions of society therefore show the ideals of the scriptures more than real life. He came mainly only as far as north-western India, so he did not have experience of the whole subcontinent.

Besides India, Al-Biruni wrote major works on astronomy such as the *Qanun-i-Mas'udi* (dedicated to Mahmud's son Mas'ud). He died in Ghazni sometime between about 1048 and 1050 CE. Today he is counted among the most serious foreign scholars to have studied India — a writer who, even in an age of war, tried to understand another culture through knowledge.
