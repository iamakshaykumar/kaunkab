## An Ancient City on the Banks of the Ravi

**Harappa** is today in Pakistan's Punjab province, about 24 kilometres west of **Sahiwal**. It takes its name from a nearby modern village. In ancient times the **Ravi river** flowed past it; today its course has shifted about 8 kilometres to the north.

This is the site whose excavation brought to light an entire unknown civilisation. In archaeology a culture is often named after the site where it was first identified, which is why the Indus Valley Civilisation is also called the **"Harappan Civilisation"**.

## The Long Story of Discovery

In 1826 the European traveller **Charles Masson** mentioned the ruins here. Later **Alexander Cunningham** visited in 1853 and 1856, and in 1872–73 he did a small excavation and published a seal bearing a bull and six unknown signs. He could not recognise its real age.

Meanwhile, in the late 1850s, the contractors of the **Lahore–Multan railway line** took huge quantities of bricks from the mounds. This did great damage to the upper layers of the city.

At the initiative of **John Marshall**, Director General of the Archaeological Survey of India, **Daya Ram Sahni** began the first major excavation here in **January 1921** (some sources call it the winter season of 1920–21). After him **Madho Sarup Vats** worked here from 1926–27 to 1933–34, and in 1946 **Mortimer Wheeler** dug the walls around Mound AB. From 1986 the American "Harappa Project" excavated here again with new techniques.

## A Settlement of More Than a Thousand Years

Excavators have identified several phases at Harappa:
- **Ravi phase** — c. 3300–2800 BCE (some newer studies push it earlier)
- **Kot Diji (Early Harappan) phase** — c. 2800–2600 BCE
- **Mature Harappan phase** — c. 2600–1900 BCE
- **Transitional and Late Harappan phase** — c. 1900–1300 BCE

At its peak the city spread over about **150 hectares**. Estimates of its population vary widely — some scholars give about 23,500, while some newer studies suggest far more.

## Mounds, the "Granary" and Working Platforms

Harappa was built on several mounds. The high **Mound AB** was surrounded by thick walls of mud brick, which Wheeler called a "citadel". On **Mound F** Vats found a large building with parallel walls, which Marshall took to be a grain store, and it became famous as the **"Granary"**. Space for air was left under its rooms.

But neither grain nor storage vessels were found here, so many archaeologists today call it simply the **"Great Hall"** and consider its real use uncertain. To its south were found **circular brick platforms**, on which grain was probably pounded. A large number of seals were also found here — about 350 seals and inscribed objects in the 1926–27 season alone.

## Cemeteries R-37 and H

Harappa is among the few Indus cities whose cemeteries have been well excavated. **Cemetery R-37** was discovered in 1937–38 and belongs to the **Mature Harappan period**. Here most bodies were buried lying straight on their backs with the head to the north, and pottery was placed with them. Some graves also had ornaments, and one grave showed traces of a wooden coffin.

The nearby **Cemetery H** is later. Wheeler's trench showed that it came after R-37. Its painted urns and different manner of burial are linked to a separate culture of the Late Harappan period.

## Significance

Harappa showed for the first time that the history of the Indian subcontinent goes back, long before the Vedic period, to an advanced civilisation with cities and a script. Despite the damage done by the railway contractors, the site remains one of the most important keys to understanding the long development of the Indus civilisation — from village to city and then decline.
