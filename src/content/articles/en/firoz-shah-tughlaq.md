## An Unexpected Sultan

**Firoz Shah Tughlaq** was the nephew of **Ghiyasuddin Tughlaq**, the founder of the Tughlaq dynasty. His father was the commander **Malik Rajab** and his mother, **Bibi Naila**, was from the Bhatti family of the Dipalpur region.

In **March 1351** his cousin **Muhammad bin Tughluq** died during a campaign near Thatta in Sindh. The army was in crisis, and the nobles and ulama chose Firoz as sultan on **23 March 1351**, right there on the bank of the Indus. After the harsh and experimental policies of Muhammad bin Tughluq, Firoz adopted a mild, conciliatory rule; keeping the nobles and ulama satisfied was central to his policy.

## Canals and New Cities

Firoz Shah is remembered most for his construction works:
- **Canals:** he had several large canals dug; he restored an old canal drawn from the Yamuna, which later became the basis of the **Western Yamuna Canal**. These increased irrigation in the Haryana-Punjab region. On irrigated land he levied 'haqq-i-sharb' (an irrigation tax).
- **Cities:** **Firozabad** on the bank of the Yamuna in Delhi (today's Firoz Shah Kotla area), and cities such as **Hisar (Hisar-i-Firoza)**, **Fatehabad** and **Jaunpur** are linked with him.
- **Repairs:** he had many old buildings repaired, including the Qutb Minar. At Hauz Khas he built a madrasa, near which is his tomb.

## The Pillars of Ashoka in Delhi

Firoz Shah had two pillars of Emperor Ashoka brought to Delhi, one from **Topra** (in today's Haryana, near Yamunanagar district) and the other from **Meerut**. The Topra pillar was erected on the roof of a stepped building at **Firoz Shah Kotla**, where it still stands. The other pillar was set up in the northern Ridge area of Delhi. The contemporary writer Afif gave a detailed description of how these heavy pillars were brought on wheeled carts and boats.

No one at that time could read the **Brahmi** inscriptions carved on these pillars. They were understood only after the script was deciphered by James Prinsep in 1837.

## Welfare Departments and Administration

- **Diwan-i-Khairat:** a department for the marriages of daughters of poor families and for helping the needy.
- **Diwan-i-Bandagan:** a department for the care and training of a very large number of slaves (bandagan). The sources give different figures for their number.
- **Hereditary posts:** the posts and jagirs of soldiers and officers began to pass to their sons; soldiers could send someone else in their place. This kept the nobles satisfied, but the army and administration became lax.
- He had Sanskrit works translated into Persian and had libraries and madrasas built.

## Campaigns and Religious Policy

He marched against Bengal twice (1353 and 1358–59) but could not reincorporate it into the Sultanate. There were campaigns against Odisha (1360), Nagarkot (Kangra) and Thatta (around 1362–63), in the last of which the army suffered great hardship.

In his autobiographical work **'Futuhat-i-Firoz Shahi'** Firoz himself describes himself as a ruler who followed the shariat. In his time there are mentions of the destruction of some temples, the persecution of some Muslim sects and the imposition of **jizya on Brahmins**; for this reason historians consider his 'liberality' limited and concentrated mainly on certain groups.

## The Last Years and an Assessment

In old age Firoz handed over the rule to his son in 1387, and he died on **20 September 1388**; he was buried at **Hauz Khas**. After him, disputes over succession weakened the Sultanate rapidly, and in 1398 **Timur** plundered Delhi.

Firoz Shah's rule is remembered on the one hand for public works and public welfare, and on the other for hereditary posts, a growing slave system and religious harshness, which weakened the foundations of the Sultanate.
