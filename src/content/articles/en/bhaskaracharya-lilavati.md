## The Teacher of Ujjain

**Bhaskaracharya** is also called **Bhaskara II** in history, to distinguish him from the seventh-century astronomer **Bhaskara I**. He wrote that he was born in **Shaka year 1036**, i.e. **1114 CE**, at a place called **Vijjadavida** near the Sahyadri mountains. Scholars disagree about where this place is today; most place it in present-day **Maharashtra**.

His father **Maheshvara** was himself an astrologer and scholar, and Bhaskara learned mathematics and astronomy from him. Later he became head of the famous astronomical observatory at **Ujjain** — the same tradition in which teachers such as **Varahamihira** and **Brahmagupta** had worked before.

## The Siddhanta Shiromani

In **1150 CE**, at the age of **36**, Bhaskara completed his greatest work, the **"Siddhanta Shiromani"** ("crown of treatises"). It is written in Sanskrit verse and has four parts:

- **Lilavati** — arithmetic, mensuration (measurement) and combinations;
- **Bijaganita** — algebra and equations;
- **Grahaganita** — calculation of the motion and positions of the planets;
- **Goladhyaya** — spherical astronomy, a discussion of the earth and the celestial sphere.

Many scholars treat the Lilavati and Bijaganita as separate works and call only the last two the Siddhanta Shiromani. Later, in **1183**, he also wrote a practical handbook of astronomical calculation, the **"Karanakutuhala"**. He is believed to have died around **1185**.

## Lilavati — Mathematics in Verse

The **Lilavati** has thirteen chapters covering topics such as definitions, interest calculation, arithmetic and geometric progressions, plane and solid geometry, measuring height from shadows, **kuttaka** (indeterminate equations) and combinations. Its special feature is that problems are posed as beautiful verses addressed to a girl. One famous problem: the square root of half a swarm of bees went to a jasmine bush, eight-ninths of the whole swarm stayed behind, and one bee hums around its companion trapped inside a lotus — how many bees were in the swarm? (Answer: **72**)

A folk tale is attached to the book's name. It is said that Bhaskara, studying the horoscope of his daughter **Lilavati**, learned that her marriage would succeed only at one particular auspicious moment. To measure the time he set up a water clock, but a pearl from Lilavati's clothing fell into it, the clock stopped and the auspicious moment passed. To console his grieving daughter, Bhaskara wrote this book of mathematics in her name. The story is of later origin and is not historically confirmed.

## Achievements in Algebra and Astronomy

In the **Bijaganita** Bhaskara stated several things clearly — for instance, that every positive number has **two square roots** (positive and negative). He called the quantity obtained by dividing by zero **"khahara"** and explained that adding to or subtracting from it does not change it — an idea similar to today's "infinity".

His most famous work is the **chakravala method**. It is a cyclic way of solving equations of the form **Nx² + 1 = y²** (later called "Pell's equation" in Europe). Building on the work of Brahmagupta and Jayadeva, Bhaskara gave it a systematic form and solved a difficult example such as **61x² + 1 = y²**. In Europe a complete solution of such equations came only in the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries.

In the **Grahaganita** he considered the **"tatkalika gati"** (instantaneous motion) of the planets — their motion at a single instant. One of his results on differences of the sine, in today's language, shows that the "derivative" of the sine is the cosine. Historians point out that Bhaskara did not build a full theory of calculus, but his ideas are early signs in that direction. He also worked on spherical trigonometry and computed the values of the sines of 18° and 36°.

## Legacy

Bhaskara's works were taught across India for centuries, and many commentaries were written on them. His grandson **Changadeva** founded a school (matha) in **1207** at **Patan** (near Chalisgaon) in Maharashtra for the study of Bhaskara's works; its inscription has been found.

In **1587** Akbar's court poet **Faizi** translated the Lilavati into **Persian**, and in **1817** the English scholar **Henry Thomas Colebrooke** published an English translation of the works of Brahmagupta and Bhaskara, bringing them to Europe's attention. In 1981 ISRO named its satellite **Bhaskara-II** in his honour.

Bhaskaracharya's work shows that India's mathematical tradition was alive and creative in the medieval period too. He is a strong link between Aryabhata, Brahmagupta and, later, Madhava of Kerala, and the Lilavati is still seen as a symbol of learning mathematics with joy.
