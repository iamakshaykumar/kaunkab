## From Kalyani to Devagiri

Historians also call the Yadava dynasty the **Seuna** dynasty. At first these kings were feudatories under the Western Chalukyas of Kalyani. When Chalukya power weakened at the end of the twelfth century, **Bhillama V** took over Kalyani in about **1187** and declared himself an independent king. He made **Devagiri** his new capital.

The hill of Devagiri is a natural fortress in itself. Its lower rock has been cut sheer all around so that enemies could not climb it. This place later came to be called **Daulatabad**, which is today near Chhatrapati Sambhajinagar (Aurangabad) in Maharashtra.

## Singhana II and the Expansion of the Kingdom

The most powerful of the Yadava kings is considered to be **Singhana II**, who ruled from about 1210 to 1246. In his time the kingdom extended from the Narmada in the north to the Tungabhadra in the south. During this period the Yadavas fought constant wars with the Hoysalas, the Kakatiyas and the kings of Gujarat and Malwa.

Singhana was also a patron of learning. In his time **Sharngadeva** wrote the authoritative work on Indian music, the **Sangita Ratnakara**, which is still read with respect in both the Hindustani and Carnatic musical traditions.

## Hemadri and the Rise of Marathi

In the latter half of the thirteenth century, **Hemadri** (Hemadpant) was the chief minister and scholar at the court of kings **Mahadeva** and **Ramachandra**. He compiled the huge dharmashastra collection **Chaturvarga Chintamani**. Tradition also links him to the black-stone **Hemadpanti** temple style, although many temples of this style are older than he was.

The Yadavas were the first major dynasty to give **Marathi** a place in governance, although Sanskrit and Kannada also remained important. During Ramachandra's reign, in about 1290, the saint **Dnyaneshwar** composed the **Dnyaneshwari**, a Marathi commentary on the Bhagavad Gita. Historians note that there is no evidence that the court directly patronised this literature, but the kingdom certainly formed the background for the early growth of Marathi literature.

## The Raid of 1296

In **1296**, **Alauddin Khilji**, the governor of Kara, advanced south with about 8,000 soldiers without informing Sultan Jalaluddin and suddenly reached Devagiri. At that time the crown prince Singhana (Shankaradeva) was away with a large part of the army. King **Ramachandra** had to take shelter in the fort and finally had to make a treaty, giving huge wealth, elephants and horses and promising an annual tribute.

With the wealth from this plunder, Alauddin removed his uncle Sultan Jalaluddin in the same year and gained the throne of Delhi. Thus the raid on Devagiri was also a turning point in the history of the Delhi Sultanate.

## The End and the Legacy

When Ramachandra did not send the promised tribute regularly, the army of **Malik Kafur** came to Devagiri again in 1307–08. Ramachandra was taken to Delhi, but Alauddin sent him back as a subordinate ruler. After him Shankaradeva (Singhana III) broke the subordination and was killed in 1313 fighting Kafur's army. The revolt of the last Yadava ruler, **Harapaladeva**, was crushed in about **1317**, and the kingdom was merged into the Sultanate.

Later **Muhammad bin Tughluq** renamed Devagiri as Daulatabad and tried for a time to bring his capital here. Today the fort of Devagiri-Daulatabad, the Sangita Ratnakara, the Chaturvarga Chintamani and the Dnyaneshwari all recall that Yadava period, when Marathi culture began to take shape in the Deccan.
