## Governors of Vijayanagara

In the sixteenth century a large part of South India was under the **Vijayanagara empire**. The empire handed over its distant provinces to military administrators called **Nayaks**. In **1529** the emperor **Krishnadevaraya** made **Viswanatha Nayak**, son of his commander Nagama Nayak, the governor of Madurai. The Madurai Nayak dynasty is considered to begin from here.

At first Viswanatha was not an independent king but a representative of the emperor. But after the Battle of Talikota in 1565, when Vijayanagara weakened, the Nayaks of Madurai, Thanjavur and Gingee gradually became independent in practice.

## The Palayam System

Viswanatha and his dalavay (prime minister) **Ariyanatha Mudaliar** divided the kingdom into **72 palayams**. Each palayam was under a **palayakkarar** (in English "poligar"), who collected taxes from his area, gave a part to the king and sent soldiers when needed. According to tradition, the 72 bastions of the Madurai fort were linked with these palayams. This system lasted for two centuries, and many southern palayakkarars who later fought against the British came out of this tradition.

Around 1604 the Nayaks recognised the **Sethupati** chiefs of Ramanathapuram, who later became a powerful local dynasty.

## The Golden Age of Tirumala Nayak

The most famous ruler of this dynasty was **Tirumala Nayak**, who ruled from **1623 to 1659**. From 1616 the capital was Tiruchirappalli; Tirumala returned it to Madurai in about 1634–35. He fought several wars with neighbouring powers such as Mysore and Bijapur.

Tirumala Nayak is remembered most for his building works. His palace — the **Tirumala Nayak Palace** — built around **1636**, is famous for its tall round pillars, huge courtyard and arches. The part that survives today is considered to be only about a quarter of the original palace.

## The Meenakshi Temple and Culture

The Nayak rulers greatly expanded Madurai's **Meenakshi-Sundareshwarar temple**. New enclosures (prakaras), mandapas and tall **gopurams** were built, some about 50 metres high. The markets and festivals that grew around the temples made Madurai a commercial as well as a religious centre.

The Nayaks were originally Telugu-speaking, so in their court both Telugu and Tamil were respected. Music, dance and temple art enjoyed their patronage. Madurai then attracted people from far away — in 1606 the Italian Jesuit priest **Roberto de Nobili** came here, who learned Tamil and Sanskrit and adopted local dress. Many mandapas, tanks and gopurams built in the Nayak period are still the identity of the cities of southern Tamil Nadu today.

## The Queens and the End

From **1689 to 1704**, **Rani Mangammal** ruled as the guardian of her infant grandson. She saved the kingdom from war by paying tribute to the Mughal army (Zulfikar Khan) that had reached the south, and is remembered for her works of public welfare.

After the death of King Vijayaranga Chokkanatha in 1732, his widow **Rani Meenakshi** became regent, but a succession dispute broke out. Taking advantage of this, **Chanda Sahib**, on behalf of the Nawab of Arcot, captured Tiruchirappalli in **1736**. With this the independent rule of the Madurai Nayaks came to an end. According to traditional accounts Rani Meenakshi died of poison in 1739 while in confinement.
