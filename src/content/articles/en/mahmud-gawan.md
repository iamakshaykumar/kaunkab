## The Merchant from Gilan

**Mahmud Gawan** was born in **1411** in the village of **Gawan** in the **Gilan** region of northern Iran. His family had held high posts in the service of the local rulers, but political intrigue forced him to leave home. He took up trade and travelled to several centres of learning in West Asia.

In **1453** he arrived as a trader in horses and goods at the west-coast port of **Dabhol**, and from there came to **Bidar**, capital of the Bahmani Sultanate. Impressed by his learning and judgement, the Bahmani court took him into service. He received the title **"Malik-ut-Tujjar"** (chief of the merchants).

## Vizier and Guardian of the Sultanate

In the time of Sultan **Humayun Shah** (1458–1461) Mahmud Gawan rose to high office. After Humayun's death a regency council was formed for the boy sultan **Nizam Shah**, in which the queen mother **Makhdumah-i-Jahan** and Mahmud Gawan were the leading figures. After this the boy **Muhammad Shah III** became sultan. Around **1466** the council broke up and Mahmud Gawan, as **vizier** of the sultanate, became the real power behind the government. This was his most influential period, lasting until **1481**.

Under his leadership the Bahmani army campaigned against Vijayanagara, Malwa and the Gajapatis of Odisha. After a long struggle in the Konkan region on the west coast, the port of **Goa** was taken from Vijayanagara in **1472**, and **Belgaum** was also conquered. At this time the Bahmani Sultanate reached its **greatest extent**.

## Administrative Reforms

Mahmud Gawan realised that the governors (tarafdars) of very large provinces had become so powerful that they could challenge the sultan. So around **1473** he divided the four large provinces (**tarafs**) into **eight**. In each province some land was set aside directly for the sultan's treasury, and the commanders of some provincial forts began to be appointed directly by the centre. Land measurement and the revenue system were also reformed.

These reforms strengthened the centre, but many powerful nobles became resentful of him — and this resentment later led to his downfall.

## The Madrasa of Bidar (1472)

Mahmud Gawan was himself a scholar, poet and a major writer of Persian prose. For the sake of education he built a large **madrasa** at Bidar at his own expense, completed in Hijri 876, i.e. **1471–72 CE**. Its design was inspired by the madrasas of Khurasan and Samarkand:

- a **three-storey** building of about **62 × 55 metres**, with an open courtyard in the middle and rooms all around;
- on the front, **two minarets** about **30 metres** high, covered with coloured glazed tiles (only one survives today);
- lecture halls, a mosque, a library, teachers' quarters and student hostels;
- a library of about **3,000 manuscripts**.

Islamic studies were taught here along with mathematics, astronomy and other sciences, and students from India and abroad received free food and lodging. In a sense the madrasa was a residential university.

After the Mughals captured Bidar in **1656**, the building was used as a barracks for soldiers. An explosion in a gunpowder store destroyed about a quarter of it and one minaret, and lightning in **1695–96** caused further damage. Today it is a monument of national importance protected by the **Archaeological Survey of India**, and in 2014 it was placed on UNESCO's tentative list under "Monuments and Forts of the Deccan Sultanate".

## The Forged Letter and a Tragic End

At the Bahmani court there was deep factionalism between the **"Deccanis"** (local and long-settled nobles) and the **"Afaqis"** (newcomers from Iran and Central Asia). Mahmud Gawan was an Afaqi, and nobles angered by his reforms plotted against him. According to the sources, a letter was written on a blank sheet bearing his seal, inviting the ruler of Odisha to attack the sultanate (some accounts link it to Vijayanagara instead). Mahmud Gawan acknowledged the seal as his but denied writing the letter.

Even so, on **5 April 1481** Sultan **Muhammad Shah III** had him executed. He was then about 70 years old. According to the historian Ferishta, the sultan later deeply regretted it, and he himself died the very next year, in 1482. **Malik Hasan Bahri**, a leading figure in the plot, rose to high office after him. Mahmud Gawan was buried at Bidar.

## Legacy

Most historians believe Mahmud Gawan's death marked the beginning of the Bahmani Sultanate's decline. After him the provincial governors became increasingly independent, and by about **1490** Bijapur, Ahmadnagar and Berar had become separate sultanates. Several of his supporters, such as **Yusuf Adil Khan**, went on to found new states themselves.

Mahmud Gawan is remembered as a capable administrator, a lover of learning and an honest minister. His madrasa at Bidar, even in ruins, is a rare surviving sign of higher education in medieval India.
