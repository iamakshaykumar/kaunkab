## Background: Rising Political Tension

In the 1971 general election, with the slogan 'Garibi Hatao' (Remove Poverty), Indira Gandhi won a huge majority, and her popularity peaked in the same year with India's victory in the Bangladesh war. But by 1973–74, discontent grew because of inflation, food shortages and unemployment. In Gujarat, after the students' **Navnirman Movement**, the state government had to resign. In Bihar the student movement came under the leadership of **Jayaprakash Narayan** (JP), who called for 'Total Revolution'. In May 1974 a nationwide railway strike led by George Fernandes was held, which the government suppressed sternly. There was also friction between the judiciary and the government — in 1973 the appointment of A. N. Ray as Chief Justice, superseding three senior judges, had been sharply criticised.

## The Allahabad High Court Verdict

**Raj Narain**, who had lost to Indira Gandhi from Rae Bareli in 1971, had filed a petition alleging misuse of government machinery in the election. On **12 June 1975**, Justice **Jagmohanlal Sinha** of the Allahabad High Court dismissed serious charges such as bribery, but found her guilty on charges such as taking the help of government officials, set aside her election and barred her from contesting elections for six years. On **24 June**, **V. R. Krishna Iyer**, a vacation judge of the Supreme Court, granted only a conditional stay on the verdict — she could remain Prime Minister but could not vote in Parliament. On 25 June a large opposition rally was held at the Ramlila Maidan in Delhi, where demands for her resignation were raised.

## 25 June 1975: Declaration of the Emergency

On the night of 25 June 1975, President **Fakhruddin Ali Ahmed** signed the proclamation of Emergency under Article 352 on grounds of 'internal disturbance'; the Cabinet's approval was taken the next morning. The government's argument was that the opposition's agitation — in which JP had appealed to the police and the army not to obey 'illegal orders' — could spread anarchy in the country, and that extraordinary steps were necessary to restore economic discipline and order.

## The 21 Months of the Emergency

- **Arrests:** Thousands of opposition leaders and workers, including JP, Morarji Desai, Atal Bihari Vajpayee and Lal Krishna Advani, were jailed under preventive detention laws such as MISA; by some estimates the total number was more than one hundred thousand.
- **Press censorship:** Newspapers were put under pre-censorship. Several newspapers printed blank editorials in protest.
- **Suspension of rights:** In the **ADM Jabalpur** case of April 1976, the Supreme Court held by a 4–1 majority that even against illegal detention one could not approach the court during the Emergency; Justice **H. R. Khanna** alone recorded a dissent.
- **Constitutional amendments:** The **42nd Amendment** of 1976 made sweeping changes to the Constitution and extended the term of the Lok Sabha.
- **Government programmes:** The government ran the **20-Point Programme** to raise production and reduce poverty. Supporters cited discipline, trains running on time and a fall in prices.
- **Excesses:** The forced sterilisation campaign linked to Sanjay Gandhi and slum-clearance drives such as the one at Delhi's Turkman Gate drew wide criticism.

## The 1977 Election and the Shah Commission

On 18 January 1977, Indira Gandhi suddenly announced elections and released many leaders. The opposition parties came together as the **Janata Party**. In the election of **16–20 March 1977**, the Janata Party and its allies won 298 seats while the Congress was reduced to 154. On **21 March 1977** the Emergency was formally lifted, and **Morarji Desai** became the first non-Congress Prime Minister of independent India.

On 28 May 1977 the new government set up the **Shah Commission** under former Chief Justice **J. C. Shah**. Its reports gave details of the circumstances of the declaration of the Emergency, the curbs on the press, and the excesses of Turkman Gate and the sterilisation drive. Indira Gandhi questioned the legality of the Commission and refused to make statements on oath; later the Commission's procedure also drew some legal criticism.

## Significance

The Emergency is counted among the most controversial periods of independent India. Supporters called it a necessary step in a time of instability, while critics regarded it as a deep blow to democratic institutions and civil rights. The **44th Amendment** of 1978 tightened the conditions for imposing an Emergency and ensured that the rights to life and liberty (Articles 20 and 21) could not be suspended even during an Emergency. The 1977 election also showed that voters can change those in power peacefully.
