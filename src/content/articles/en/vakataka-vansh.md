## Rise: Vindhyashakti and Pravarasena I

After the fall of the Satavahanas, several new powers arose in the Deccan in the 3rd century. Among them were the **Vakatakas**, whose centre was **Vidarbha** (in present-day Maharashtra). **Vindhyashakti** is regarded as the founder of the dynasty, and he began his rule around 250 CE. Very little is known about him; he is mentioned mainly in later inscriptions and in the inscription of Ajanta Cave 16.

His son **Pravarasena I** (c. 270–330 CE) was the most powerful early king of the dynasty. He took the titles **Samrat** and **Dharmamaharaja** and performed Vedic sacrifices, including, it is said, four Ashvamedhas. It is believed that after him the kingdom split into several branches, of which we have information about two — the main branch of **Nandivardhana-Pravarapura** and the branch of **Vatsagulma** (present-day Washim).

## Relations with the Guptas: Prabhavatigupta

At the end of the 4th century, **Rudrasena II**, king of the main branch, married **Prabhavatigupta**, daughter of the Gupta emperor **Chandragupta II**. Historians regard this as a political alliance — for the Guptas, a friendly kingdom in the Deccan was useful in their campaign against the Shakas (Western Kshatrapas) in the west.

Rudrasena II's reign was short and he died early. Prabhavatigupta then ruled the kingdom for about twenty years as **regent** for her young sons — roughly from 385/390 to 405/410 CE (the sources differ slightly). Copper-plate grants issued by her have been found. It is worth noting that in these she traces her lineage to her father's Gupta dynasty. Many historians believe that Gupta influence on the Vakataka kingdom grew considerably in this period.

## Pravarasena II and Literature

**Pravarasena II**, the son of Prabhavatigupta, ruled for a long time and shifted the capital from Nandivardhana to a new city, **Pravarapura**. The famous Maharashtri Prakrit poem **Setubandha** (also called Ravanavaho), which tells the story of Rama building a bridge across the sea, is associated with his name. One tradition says that Kalidasa helped polish this poem, but this is not proven.

In this same period, stone temples also began to be built in Vidarbha. On the hill of **Ramtek**, near Nagpur, remains of early Vakataka-period temples can be seen.

## Harishena and Ajanta

**Harishena** of the Vatsagulma branch (c. 475–500 CE; some scholars give earlier dates as well) was the last great ruler of the Vakatakas. The inscription in Ajanta Cave 16 describes the area of his influence as stretching from Malwa to Kosala and Andhra, although such praise-poems also contain exaggeration.

Today Harishena's name is remembered most because of **Ajanta**. The Buddhist caves at Ajanta had been made from the 2nd century BCE, but after a long gap a second major phase of excavation took place in his time.
- **Cave 16** — it was built by Harishena's minister **Varahadeva**, as recorded in the inscription there.
- **Cave 17** — it was donated by a local feudatory king.
- **Cave 19** — the chaitya hall with beautiful carving also belongs to this period.

The murals in these caves — the bodhisattva Padmapani, Jataka stories, scenes of court and city life — are a priceless heritage of Indian painting.

## Decline

It is not clear what happened to the Vakataka dynasty after Harishena. Even the names of his successors are not known with certainty. The dynasty came to an end around 500 CE. It is conjectured that the **Kalachuris of Mahishmati** or other neighbouring powers took over their territory. Dandin's later work 'Dashakumaracharita' contains the story of the fall of a Vidarbha royal line, which some scholars link to the Vakatakas, but this is uncertain. The work at Ajanta was also left unfinished at about this time.

## Significance

The Vakataka period is often called the "Gupta-Vakataka age", because the cultures of the north and the Deccan were deeply connected with each other at this time. The Vakatakas carried the Sanskrit-Prakrit culture of North India to the Deccan, carried forward the tradition of land grants to Brahmins, and left a heritage like Ajanta, which is today a UNESCO World Heritage Site. Prabhavatigupta's rule also shows that queens in ancient India sometimes held real power.
