## Devotion Rising from the Tamil Land

The oldest stream of the **bhakti movement** in India is seen in the Tamil country of the south. Between about the **6th and 9th centuries**, two saintly traditions arose here — the **Alvars**, devotees of Vishnu, and the **Nayanars**, devotees of Shiva. These saints wandered from village to village and temple to temple, singing songs in **Tamil** in praise of their beloved deity.

Unlike Sanskrit ritualism, these songs had personal love, surrender and yearning towards God. "Alvar" means "one immersed in devotion to God", and "Nayanar" means "master" or "leader". Their exact dates are not certain; scholars place different saints in different centuries.

## The Twelve Alvars

Tradition recognises **twelve Alvars**. Among them **Nammalvar** is regarded as the most prominent; in his work the **Tiruvaymozhi**, God is seen as the truth dwelling within every being. Alvars such as **Periyalvar** and Kulasekhara are also famous.

The only woman among the Alvars was **Andal** (Kodai), whom scholars place at about the eighth century. According to tradition, Periyalvar found her under a tulasi plant at **Srivilliputtur** and raised her himself. Andal refused worldly marriage and regarded only Vishnu as her bridegroom. Her **Tiruppavai** (30 verses) and **Nachchiyar Tirumozhi** are gems of Tamil devotional poetry. The story is that she was divinely wedded to Ranganatha of Srirangam.

## The Sixty-Three Nayanars

The Shaiva tradition recognises **63 Nayanars**. Foremost among them are **Appar** (Tirunavukkarasar), the child saint **Sambandar** and **Sundarar**. Sambandar and Appar are believed to have lived in the seventh century. Tradition says that Appar was earlier a Jain and, after becoming a Shaiva, also turned the Pallava king **Mahendravarman I** towards Shaivism.

The Nayanars included people of different social backgrounds — Brahmins, Vellalar farmers, Vanniyars and even those who were then regarded as "untouchables". Three women, including **Karaikkal Ammaiyar**, are also counted as Nayanars. **Manikkavacakar** of the ninth century and his **Tiruvacakam** are also an immortal work of Shaiva devotion, though he is not counted in the list of 63.

## The Compilations: Divya Prabandham and Tevaram

The songs of these saints continued in oral tradition for centuries. Around the ninth-tenth century the Vaishnava teacher **Nathamuni** collected about **four thousand verses** of the Alvars, called the **Nalayira Divya Prabandham**. Vaishnavas give it the status of the "Tamil Veda". The temples praised in these verses are called the **108 Divya Desams**.

On the Shaiva side, in the time of the Chola king **Rajaraja I** (tenth century), **Nambiyandar Nambi** compiled the hymns of Appar, Sambandar and Sundarar as the **Tevaram**, which is part of the **Tirumurai**. In the twelfth century **Sekkizhar** wrote the life stories of the 63 Nayanars in the **Periya Puranam**.

## Legacy

The ideas of the Alvars later deeply influenced the philosophy of the teacher **Ramanuja**. In the Chola period the singing of their verses became regular in temples, and bronze images of the Nayanar saints were made and installed in temples.

The Alvar-Nayanar tradition showed that the language of the people, music and love are enough to reach God. This very idea reached the saints of Karnataka, Maharashtra and North India in later centuries. Even today the hymns of the **Tevaram** and the **Divya Prabandham** resound in the temples of Tamil Nadu.
