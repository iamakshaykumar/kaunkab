## From Dhudike to Lahore

**Lala Lajpat Rai** was born on **28 January 1865** in the village of **Dhudike** in Punjab. From **1880** he studied law at Government College, Lahore. Here he came into contact with the **Arya Samaj** and, along with **Mahatma Hansraj**, took part in founding the **Dayanand Anglo-Vedic (DAV) School** in Lahore.

From **1886** he practised law at **Hisar** and helped set up branches of the Congress and the Arya Samaj there. In **1892** he moved to Lahore to practise in the Lahore High Court. He also worked in journalism and wrote in several newspapers including *The Tribune*. He believed that Hindu society would have to face questions such as caste, the condition of women and untouchability.

## Lal-Bal-Pal and the Mandalay Deportation (1907)

In the early twentieth century the "extremist faction" emerged within the Congress, with three main faces — **Lal** (Lajpat Rai) of Punjab, **Bal** (Tilak) of Maharashtra and **Pal** (Bipin Chandra Pal) of Bengal. These leaders were dissatisfied with a politics of mere petitions and stressed swadeshi, boycott and mass movement.

In **1907** a farmers' agitation flared up in Punjab against the Canal Colonies law and the rise in water rates, with which **Sardar Ajit Singh**'s "Pagri Sambhal Jatta" movement was linked. In **May 1907** the British government sent Lajpat Rai, and later Ajit Singh, **without trial** to **Mandalay** in Burma under the **State Prisoners Regulation of 1818**. In the British Parliament the Secretary of State for India, **John Morley**, called it necessary for "reasons of state". Under public pressure both were released on **11 November 1907**. Lajpat Rai wrote *The Story of My Deportation* (1908) on this experience.

## Stay in America

In **1914** he gave up his legal practice and stayed abroad during the First World War. In **1917** he reached America and founded the **Indian Home Rule League of America** in **New York**. He remained there until **1920** and tried to build American public opinion in favour of India's freedom. Books such as *Young India* (1916) and *England's Debt to India* (1917) came out in this period.

## Congress President and Social Service

On returning to India he was elected in **1920** president of the Congress **special session at Calcutta** — the very session that opened the way for the **Non-Cooperation Movement**. From **1921** to **1923** he was in jail. In **1921** he formed the **Servants of the People Society** at Lahore, which trained workers devoted to public service. After release from jail he was elected a member of the Legislative Assembly.

He was also interested in the economy and in building institutions — in **1894** he was associated with the early management of the **Punjab National Bank** and the **Lakshmi Insurance Company**. (It is often said that he "founded" the Punjab National Bank; it is more accurate to say that he was among its early directors.)

## The Simon Commission and the Last Days (1928)

The **Simon Commission** that came in **1928** consisted entirely of British members, so it was boycotted across the country. On **30 October 1928** at Lahore, Lajpat Rai led a peaceful procession in protest against it. On the orders of the Superintendent of Police **James A. Scott**, a lathi charge took place and Lajpat Rai was injured. He had said — "I declare that the blows struck at me today will be the last nails in the coffin of British rule in India."

He died on **17 November 1928**. Doctors believed that the injuries from the batons hastened his death, but the British government in Parliament denied responsibility. To avenge this, **Bhagat Singh** and his comrades planned to kill Scott on **17 December 1928**, but by a mistake in identification another police officer, **John P. Saunders**, was killed.

Remembered today as "Punjab Kesari", Lajpat Rai has many institutions, roads and colleges named after him across the country.
