## Chotanagpur and the "Kols"

The **Chotanagpur** plateau of present-day Jharkhand is a region full of forests and hills, where tribal communities such as the **Munda, Ho, Oraon and Bhumij** have lived for centuries. They had their own village system: the village headman was called the **Munda**, and the head of a group of several villages (pirh) was called the **Manki**. The community regarded the land as the shared inheritance of its ancestors. Nineteenth-century British officials and outsiders lumped these communities together as "Kols".

## Causes of Discontent

At the end of the eighteenth century this region came under the influence of the East India Company. After this, many changes came quickly:

- The rajas and zamindars of Chotanagpur, to raise revenue, leased villages out to outside **contractors** (thikadars), who collected rent arbitrarily.
- Outside **moneylenders** and traders — whom the tribals called "dikus" — lent money at high interest, seized land and pushed people into bonded labour.
- The Company's courts and police were distant, expensive and often sided with outsiders.
- New taxes were imposed on salt, liquor (handia) and market goods.

According to sources, the immediate cause was that some villages of the Mankis in **Sonepur pargana** were taken away and given to Sikh and Muslim contractors. Some accounts also mention incidents of insults to tribal women by the contractors.

## The Beginning of the Rebellion (December 1831)

In late 1831 chiefs such as **Sindrai Manki**, **Bindrai Manki** and **Surga Manki** called an assembly of tribal chiefs in the Tamar region and decided on armed resistance. From **December 1831** the rebels began attacks on the villages and fields of the contractors. The fire spread rapidly and the areas of **Ranchi, Singhbhum, Palamau, Hazaribagh and Manbhum** were caught in it.

The rebels' targets were first the contractors, then moneylenders, traders and the Company's posts; court records and revenue offices were burnt. During this many outside settlers were also killed. The rebels' main weapons were bows and arrows, axes and spears.

## Suppression (1832)

The Company sent troops from several directions, led by several officers including Captain **Thomas Wilkinson**. On **14 February 1832**, at **Silagai** in the Ranchi region, the main leader **Budhu Bhagat** was surrounded and killed along with his sons and about a hundred and fifty companions. The army burnt villages and destroyed grain so that the rebels would be forced to surrender. By the spring of **1832** most of the leaders had been captured or killed and the armed struggle subsided, although sporadic resistance continued in some areas later.

## Outcome: A Separate Administration

The rebellion showed the British that the ordinary laws and courts of Bengal were not working in this region. Under **Regulation XIII of 1833**, Chotanagpur was separated from the ordinary Bengal rules and the **South-West Frontier Agency** was created (sources give the year of its formation as 1833 or 1834). **Wilkinson** became its first Agent. The Manki-Munda system was given back some administrative role, and rules were made to settle disputes in a simpler way.

## Significance

The Kol Rebellion was an early link in a chain of major tribal movements in the Jharkhand region. The questions of land, forest and outside exploitation raised in it re-emerged later in the **Santhal Hul (1855–56)** and **Birsa Munda's Ulgulan (1899–1900)**. The result of this long struggle was later seen in laws such as the **Chotanagpur Tenancy Act (1908)**, which prohibited the transfer of tribal land to outsiders.
