## Birth and Family

According to Jain tradition, **Vardhamana** was born in **599 BCE** at **Kundagrama** (near Vaishali, in present-day Bihar). His father **Siddhartha** was a Kshatriya chief of the Jnatri (Naya) clan, and his mother **Trishala** was the sister of **Chetaka**, the Licchavi chief of Vaishali. The Jain community celebrates his birthday as **Mahavir Jayanti** on the thirteenth day of the bright half of Chaitra.

There is serious disagreement about Mahavira's dates. The Shvetambara tradition places his nirvana in 527 BCE, while the Digambara reckoning is somewhat different. Since Mahavira was a contemporary of the Buddha, and many modern scholars place the Buddha's dates later than before, they also place Mahavira's time about a century later — one popular estimate puts his death at about 468 BCE.

In the Shvetambara text *Kalpasutra* there is a story that Mahavira's embryo was first in the womb of the Brahmin woman **Devananda**, and that the god Harinegameshi, on Indra's order, moved it into Trishala's womb. This is a religious narrative, not historical fact.

## Household Life and Renunciation

The two Jain sects say different things about Mahavira's marriage. According to the **Shvetambara** tradition he married **Yashoda** and had a daughter, **Priyadarshana**. The **Digambara** tradition holds that he never married.

At about **30 years** of age, after the death of his parents, Vardhamana gave up home, family and royal comforts and became an ascetic.

## Twelve and a Half Years of Austerity

After this, for about **twelve and a half years**, he practised severe austerities — long fasts, silence, and enduring hardships with equanimity. Jain texts say that during this time he suffered insult and physical pain many times. According to the Digambara tradition he even gave up clothes.

At about 42 years of age, under a **sal tree** on the bank of the **Rijupalika** (Rijubalika) river near **Jrimbhikagrama**, he attained **Kevala** (Kevala Jnana, that is, perfect knowledge). Scholars disagree about the exact identification of these places today. After Kevala he was called **Jina** (the conqueror — one who has conquered the senses and attachment and aversion) and **Mahavira**. The word "Jain" comes from this "Jina".

## Teachings

Jain tradition regards Mahavira not as the founder of the religion but as the twenty-fourth **Tirthankara**. Before him came the twenty-third Tirthankara, **Parshvanatha**, whose "Chaturyama" (four vows) is mentioned in Buddhist texts too. According to tradition Mahavira added a fifth vow to these, **brahmacharya**. In this way the **five Mahavratas** of Jain monks came to be:

- **Ahimsa** — not harming any living being
- **Satya** — speaking the truth
- **Asteya** — not stealing
- **Brahmacharya** — self-restraint
- **Aparigraha** — giving up hoarding and attachment

One important doctrine of Mahavira is **Anekantavada** — truth has many sides, and no single viewpoint can see the whole truth. He formed a **fourfold sangha** with four parts: monks, nuns, laymen (shravaka) and laywomen (shravika). His chief disciple was **Indrabhuti Gautama**, whom tradition credits with compiling his teachings in the **Agama** texts. Mahavira preached in **Prakrit** (Ardhamagadhi), the language of the common people.

## Nirvana at Pavapuri

After about 30 years of wandering and preaching in Magadha, Videha and the surrounding regions, at the age of **72** he attained **nirvana** at **Pavapuri** (in Nalanda district of present-day Bihar). The Jain community observes **Deepavali** as the day of Mahavira's nirvana. The **Jal Mandir** at Pavapuri, built in the middle of a pond, is linked with the site of his cremation and is today a major pilgrimage place.

Mahavira's teaching of non-violence, vegetarianism, self-restraint and understanding the viewpoints of others left a deep mark on Indian society, trade and thought — in the modern age, Jain thought is also believed to have influenced Mahatma Gandhi's idea of non-violence.
