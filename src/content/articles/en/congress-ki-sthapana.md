## Background: The Need for an All-India Platform

After the revolt of 1857, the direct rule of the British Crown began in India. In the next few decades a new English-educated Indian class emerged — lawyers, teachers, journalists and doctors — who began to question the government's policies through newspapers and meetings.

In the 1870s and 1880s many regional bodies were formed — such as the Poona Sarvajanik Sabha, the Indian Association of Calcutta, the Madras Mahajana Sabha and the Bombay Presidency Association. Meanwhile the policies of Lord Lytton (such as the Vernacular Press Act) and the **Ilbert Bill** controversy of 1883 made it clear that Indians needed an **all-India organisation** to put forward their views.

## A. O. Hume's Initiative

**Allan Octavian Hume** was a retired British official. In 1883 he wrote an open letter to the graduates of Calcutta University and appealed to them to organise to serve the country. He contacted leading Indian leaders across the country.

By May 1885 Hume had planned an organisation called the 'Indian National Union' and also obtained the consent of the Viceroy **Lord Dufferin** for it. Its first meeting was to be held at Poona in December 1885, but because of an outbreak of cholera the venue was changed to Bombay.

## 28 December 1885: The First Session

From 28 to 31 December 1885 the first session was held at the **Gokuldas Tejpal Sanskrit College** in Bombay. **72 delegates** came from different provinces of the country, most of them lawyers, journalists and teachers. The famous Calcutta barrister **Womesh Chunder Bonnerjee** (W. C. Bonnerjee) was elected president and Hume became general secretary. The delegates included names such as Dadabhai Naoroji, Pherozeshah Mehta, K. T. Telang and Dinshaw Wacha.

Some major demands were put forward at the first session:
- A Royal Commission should be set up to inquire into Indian administration.
- The legislative councils should be expanded and have elected Indian members.
- The Indian Civil Service (ICS) examination should be held in India as well as in England.
- Military expenditure should be reduced.

## The Moderate Phase and the 'Safety Valve' Debate

In the early years the Congress leaders were called **Moderates**. They demanded reform through constitutional methods — petitions, resolutions, speeches and writings. Through his theory of the 'Drain of Wealth', Dadabhai Naoroji showed that British rule was carrying India's wealth out of the country.

There is also a debate among historians about the founding of the Congress. Some held that Hume created it as a **'safety valve'** to let Indian discontent out peacefully. But historians such as Bipan Chandra point out that Indian leaders themselves wanted to build a national organisation, and took Hume's help so as not to face the government's hostility at the very start.

## Significance

Despite its small beginning, the Congress, by holding sessions in different cities each year, kept bringing people from different parts of the country onto one platform. Later, under the leadership of leaders such as Bal Gangadhar Tilak, Gopal Krishna Gokhale, Mahatma Gandhi, Jawaharlal Nehru and Subhas Chandra Bose, this same organisation became the main vehicle of the freedom movement — and it played a central role until India's independence in 1947.
