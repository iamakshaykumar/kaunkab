## From Symbol to Statue

In early Buddhist art the Buddha was not shown in human form. At the stupas of Sanchi and Bharhut his presence was shown by **symbols**: the Bodhi tree, the Dharmachakra, footprints, an empty seat or an umbrella. Around the first century CE this tradition changed. In Mahayana Buddhism the emphasis on devotion and worship increased, and images of the Buddha and bodhisattvas began to be made.

This change took place mainly in the period of the **Kushan Empire**, which extended from Central Asia to the Gangetic plain. At two ends of this same empire two great art styles flourished: **Gandhara** and **Mathura**.

## The Gandhara Style

The Gandhara region spread over today's north-western Pakistan (the Peshawar valley, Taxila, Swat) and eastern Afghanistan. After Alexander, Greek, then Shaka, Parthian and Kushan rulers held it, and it lay on the Silk Road. So the art here shows clear **Greco-Roman** influence.
- **Stone:** mainly grey-blue **schist**; in later periods **stucco** was also used a great deal.
- **Form of the Buddha:** wavy curly hair with a topknot (ushnisha) above, a calm face like that of the Greek god Apollo, sometimes a moustache.
- **Clothing:** a thick robe full of folds that usually covers **both shoulders**, like the Greek *himation*.
- **Decoration:** Greek-style pillars, vine scrolls, and the Buddha's protector Vajrapani is sometimes shown in a form like the Greek hero Heracles.

The peak of Gandhara art is considered to be from the first to the fifth century CE.

## The Mathura Style

Mathura was an ancient trading and religious city on the bank of the Yamuna. Even before the Kushans, from about the second century BCE, huge stone statues of **yakshas and yakshis** were made here. The Buddha images of Mathura grew out of this native tradition.
- **Stone:** the **spotted red sandstone** of the Sikri quarries.
- **Form of the Buddha:** a round, full, smiling face; in early images a shell-like lock on the head (the "kapardin" type).
- **Clothing:** a thin garment clinging to the body, which usually covers only the **left shoulder**; the right shoulder is left bare.
- **Mood:** figures full of strength and energy, looking straight ahead.

A special feature of Mathura was that not only Buddhist but also **Jain** Tirthankara and **Hindu** deity images were made here. Mathura sculptures were sent far and wide; the famous "Bodhisattva image of Bhikshu Bala" found at Sarnath is also of Mathura red stone and carries an inscription of the Kushan period.

## The First Buddha Image: Gandhara or Mathura?

This question has been a subject of debate for more than a hundred years. The French scholar **Alfred Foucher** held that the idea of the human image of the Buddha was born in Gandhara through Greek influence. In reply, **Ananda Coomaraswamy** argued in 1927 that the Buddha image developed independently in Mathura out of the long tradition of yaksha sculpture.

Today most scholars believe that Buddha images were made in both centres at about the same time, borrowing some influence from each other. Fixing dates is difficult, because even the date of **Kanishka**'s accession is disputed; in the older view it was linked to 78 CE, while many scholars now place it at about 127 CE.

## Significance

Together Gandhara and Mathura changed the direction of Indian sculpture. Later, in the Gupta period, the workshops of **Sarnath** and Mathura combined these two traditions to create that calm, spiritual form of the Buddha which is still regarded as the ideal. Gandhara art continued to influence Buddhist art through Central Asia as far as China, while Mathura played a major role in laying the foundation of Hindu and Jain iconography in India.
