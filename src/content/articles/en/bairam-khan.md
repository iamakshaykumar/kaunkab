## Who Was Bairam Khan

Bairam Khan is believed to have been born around 1501 in the Badakhshan region of Central Asia. He belonged to the Turkmen **Baharlu** tribe and was a follower of the Shia faith. His father and grandfather had served Babur, and Bairam too entered Mughal service in his teens.

In Humayun's time he was counted among his most trusted companions. After the defeat at **Kannauj** in 1540 he fell into the hands of Sher Shah's men, but escaped and in 1543 rejoined the exiled Humayun in Sindh. When Humayun returned from Iran and conquered Kandahar, its governance was entrusted to Bairam. In the reconquest of Hindustan in 1555 too he was a chief commander.

## Regent and Khan-i-Khanan

In January 1556 Humayun died in Delhi after falling from the steps of his library. At that time the thirteen-year-old prince Akbar was in Punjab with Bairam Khan. On **14 February 1556**, on a simple platform at **Kalanaur** in Punjab, Akbar was enthroned. Bairam Khan became the **wakil** (the highest officer of the state and guardian of the boy emperor) and received the title **Khan-i-Khanan**.

It was an extremely uncertain time. Mughal power was only a few months old, and several claimants from the Afghan Sur rulers were still in the field.

## The Second Battle of Panipat (1556)

**Hemu**, the general of the Sur ruler Adil Shah, seized Delhi in October 1556. Bairam Khan had the Mughal governor **Tardi Beg**, who had come back after being defeated at Tughluqabad, put to death — historians regard this both as a harsh step to maintain discipline and as a ploy to remove a rival.

On **5 November 1556** the two armies clashed on the field of Panipat. Hemu's army was gaining the upper hand, but an arrow struck his eye and he lost consciousness. As soon as the leader fell, his army scattered. Who beheaded the captive Hemu — Bairam Khan or Akbar — is a matter on which the sources differ. This victory firmly laid the foundation of Mughal rule in North India. In 1557, after the siege of Mankot, Sikandar Shah Sur also surrendered.

## Rule During the Regency

In the next four years, under Bairam Khan's leadership, regions such as Gwalior, Ajmer and Jaunpur came under Mughal control. The real reins of government were in his hands. But gradually his opponents in the court increased. He was accused of favouring his own favourites — especially Shia officers — and of taking big decisions alone. Akbar's wet nurse **Maham Anga**, her son **Adham Khan** and a large faction of the court stood against him. Akbar, now nearing eighteen, himself wanted to take charge of the government.

## Fall and Death

In the spring of **1560** Akbar sent a message to Bairam Khan that he should either take an honourable post at court or go on the Hajj pilgrimage to Mecca. Bairam chose Hajj, but he took the conduct of Pir Muhammad, sent to follow him, as an insult and rebelled. He was defeated by the royal army near Jalandhar. On surrendering before Akbar he received honourable forgiveness and again set out towards Gujarat for the Hajj.

On **31 January 1561**, near the Sahasralinga tank at Patan (Gujarat), Afghans led by **Mubarak Khan Lohani** murdered him. It is said that Mubarak Khan's father had earlier been killed by the Mughals in a battle, so this was an act of revenge.

## Legacy

Without Bairam Khan Akbar's rule might have collapsed in its very first year. He kept Mughal power standing in a time of crisis and then handed Akbar a stable kingdom. His fall also shows that Akbar had resolved, even in his teens, to take power into his own hands. Akbar raised Bairam's younger son **Abdul Rahim** at court, who later became Khan-i-Khanan himself.
