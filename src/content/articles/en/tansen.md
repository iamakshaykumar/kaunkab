## The Boy from Gwalior

Tansen was born in the **Gwalior** region. His date of birth is not certain; different sources place it between **1493 and 1506**, so "about 1500" is usually written. According to tradition his childhood name was **Ramtanu** and he came from a Brahmin family.

At that time Gwalior was a major centre of music. The Tomar king **Man Singh Tomar** (died 1516) had promoted **dhrupad** singing, and Tansen got his early recognition in this atmosphere. There are many traditions about his teachers. The most famous name is that of the saint-musician **Swami Haridas** of Vrindavan. The Sufi saint of Gwalior **Muhammad Ghaus** is also regarded as his spiritual guide. Solid contemporary evidence for these relationships is scarce, so they should be understood only as tradition.

## From Rewa to Akbar's Court

Tansen spent a large part of his life at the court of **Raja Ramchandra** of **Rewa (Bandhavgarh)**, a great patron of music. Some sources say that before that he also lived under the protection of Daulat Khan, a son of Sher Shah Suri.

His fame reached **Akbar**, and in **1562**, at about the age of sixty, he came to the Mughal court. Abu'l-Fazl's writings show that Akbar held him in great respect. Akbar gave him the title **'Miyan'**, meaning a learned or respected person. Popular tradition counts him among Akbar's **Navratnas** — along with Birbal, Todar Mal, Abu'l-Fazl and others. Note that the list of the "Navratnas" became fixed in later tradition.

## Contribution to Music

Tansen was mainly a singer and composer of **dhrupad** — a serious, slow and word-centred style, in which songs of gods, kings and nature were sung. Many ragas are linked to him, which is why "Miyan" is added to their names:

- **Miyan ki Todi**
- **Miyan ki Malhar**
- **Darbari** (Darbari Kanada)

He is also credited with popularising and improving the **rabab** instrument. Works such as *Sangeet Sar* and *Shri Ganesh Stotra* are attributed to him. Many musical traditions later became linked with the lineage of his sons and daughter; many gharanas of Hindustani music connect themselves to Tansen's tradition.

## Legends and Disagreements

There are more stories about Tansen than firm history. It is said that singing **Raga Deepak** made lamps light up and **Megh Malhar** brought rain. In one story Akbar and Tansen go in disguise to Vrindavan to hear Swami Haridas sing. Historians regard these as inspiring folktales, not proven events.

There is also disagreement about his religion. One tradition says he adopted Islam; but many of his compositions praise Hindu deities, so scholars are cautious about this claim. His wife's name is given as **Hussaini**. He had four sons and a daughter, **Saraswati**. Saraswati was married to the veena player **Misri Singh** (Naubat Khan), and the descendants of Tansen's sons and of this son-in-law carried on the tradition of court music for many generations.

The picture in this article is also worth noting. It is a Mughal miniature made in Akbar's time, about **1585–90**, and is today in the **National Museum, New Delhi**. It is regarded as a genuine contemporary portrait of Tansen — that is, made during his lifetime.

## Death and Tomb

According to the Akbarnama, Tansen died on **26 April 1589**. Some other sources mention 1586 and Delhi, but 1589 is more widely accepted. He was buried in **Gwalior** in the complex of the grand mausoleum of the Sufi saint **Muhammad Ghaus**, where his comparatively simple tomb stands. The mausoleum of Muhammad Ghaus has been a protected monument of national importance since 1962.

Even today musicians come to this tomb for blessings. Every December the **Tansen Samaroh** is held here, and Hindustani classical musicians are given the **Tansen Samman** in his name. Even after about four and a half centuries, the word "Tansen" remains in India a synonym for a great singer.
