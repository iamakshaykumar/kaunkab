## The Karkota Dynasty and Lalitaditya

In the seventh century the **Karkota dynasty** began to rule Kashmir; it claimed descent from the Naga king Karkotaka. **Lalitaditya Muktapida** was the youngest son of King Durlabhaka (Pratapaditya II). Before him his two elder brothers — Chandrapida and Tarapida — had already sat on the throne.

Most of our information about him comes from Kalhana's **Rajatarangini**, written about four hundred years later. According to Kalhana, Lalitaditya ruled for about 36 years, but the dates that come out of his reckoning (about 700–736 CE) are considered somewhat too early; on the basis of Chinese records, modern historians place his reign at about **724/725 to 760 CE**.

## Campaigns of Conquest: History and Exaggeration

Kalhana describes Lalitaditya as the conqueror of almost all of India and Central Asia. Historians test these claims one by one:
- **The victory over Yashovarman of Kannauj** is considered real by most scholars. At that time Kannauj was the centre of prestige in northern India. There is disagreement over the date of the war and how far Yashovarman remained subordinate afterwards.
- **Tibet and Tang China** — Chinese sources say that Kashmir cooperated with the Tang empire against Tibet and that the Tang court gave the king of Kashmir the title of "king". So there is some independent evidence for Lalitaditya's international role.
- **The conquests of Bengal, Odisha, the Deccan, Gujarat and as far as Central Asia** are regarded by many historians as exaggerated or merely raids. M. A. Stein called the conquests after Yashovarman "clearly legend".

Kalhana's stories also contain miracles — such as driving a spear into the ground to bring out a stream of water, or the minister **Chankuna** separating the water of a river with a jewel. These should be regarded only as legend.

## The Sun Temple of Martand

Lalitaditya's most lasting gift is the **Martand Sun Temple**, on a high plateau near Mattan in today's Anantnag district. "Martand" is itself a name of the Sun. Kalhana describes this temple and the town around it as Lalitaditya's creation.

The plan of the temple is unusual — the main shrine in the middle and around it a huge pillared courtyard in which **84 small shrines** were built. Its style shows elements of Gandhara and Gupta art, reflecting Kashmir's links with Central Asia and northern India.

The later historian Jonaraja blames Sultan **Sikandar Shah Miri** (reigned 1389–1413) for its destruction; some modern scholars advise reading these accounts with caution. Earthquakes later also damaged the ruins. Today it is a monument of national importance protected by the Archaeological Survey of India.

## Parihasapura and Other Buildings

Lalitaditya founded a new city called **Parihasapura** and made it his main residence. According to Kalhana, temples and monasteries of Vishnu, Shiva and the Buddha were built there. By Kalhana's time the city had fallen into ruin, but even today remains of large platforms and a stupa are found there. The king also had irrigation and water-distribution works carried out, and his minister **Mitrasharman** also built a Shiva temple.

## Death and Legacy

Lalitaditya's death is shrouded in mystery. Kalhana himself gives different stories — according to one, he died in an untimely snowfall in some country of the north; according to another, he gave up his life in a fire himself. Nothing is certain. After him his son Kuvalayapida sat on the throne.

Lalitaditya is considered the most famous king in Kashmir's history. His time was the peak of Kashmir's political power and art. At the same time his story also teaches us that instead of trusting a single text completely, we should test history by combining different sources — inscriptions, foreign accounts and archaeology.
