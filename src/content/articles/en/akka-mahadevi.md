## Mahadevi of Udutadi

**Akka Mahadevi** was born around **1130 CE** in the village of **Udutadi** in the **Shivamogga** district of Karnataka. According to some scholars, her parents **Nirmalshetti** and **Sumati** were devotees of Shiva. Tradition says she received Shiva initiation in childhood and chose **Mallikarjuna**, the deity of Srisailam, as her beloved god. In her vachanas she calls Shiva **'Chennamallikarjuna'** — meaning 'the beautiful lord, white as jasmine'. This name comes at the end of every one of her vachanas as a 'mudrika' (signature).

Information about her life comes mainly from her own vachanas and from later works, such as the biography written by the 13th-century poet **Harihara**. So history and tradition are mixed in many of the events.

## King Kaushika and Leaving Home

According to tradition, the king of the region, **Kaushika** — whom many sources describe as a Jain — saw Mahadevi's beauty and wanted to marry her. The stories differ on this: in Harihara's account the marriage was only nominal, while in other stories Mahadevi refused the marriage altogether. All the stories agree that Mahadevi considered herself the 'bride' of Chennamallikarjuna alone, and left the comforts of the palace.

It is said that she gave up even her clothes and covered her body only with her long hair. The sense of one of her famous vachanas is: when the whole world is the form of the lord, from whom should one feel shame, and why cover oneself? This is why her statues and pictures usually show her as a figure covered by long hair, as in this article's picture.

## The Test at the Anubhava Mantapa

After leaving home, Akka Mahadevi reached **Kalyana** (today's **Basavakalyan**) in northern Karnataka, where the movement of the Veerashaiva 'sharanas' was under way under the leadership of **Basavanna**. In its **Anubhava Mantapa**, saint-poets discussed spiritual experiences, rising above caste, gender and ritual.

According to tradition, the chief saint of the Mantapa, **Allama Prabhu**, first asked her hard questions — about her unclothed form, her 'husband' and her renunciation. Satisfied with Mahadevi's answers, the assembly accepted her, and sharanas such as **Basavanna**, **Allama Prabhu** and **Siddharama** honoured her by calling her **'Akka'** — that is, 'elder sister'. Allama Prabhu is regarded as her spiritual guide. This dialogue was later recorded in Veerashaiva texts such as the 'Shunya Sampadane'.

## Vachanas — Plain Words, Deep Experience

Instead of Sanskrit, the Veerashaiva saints composed short prose-poems in **Kannada**, the language of ordinary people, called **vachanas**. About **350 to 430** of Akka Mahadevi's vachanas survive (the number differs between sources). Besides these, two short works — **Mantrogopya** and **Yogangatrividhi** — are also attributed to her.

Her vachanas are full of love, longing and complete surrender. She sees herself as the lover and Shiva as the beloved, and describes worldly bonds as being like a silkworm that wraps itself in its own thread. Her language is simple, but her images are very sharp — jasmine, mountains, forests, birds and bees appear again and again in her poetry. Because she openly challenged the limits set by society as a woman, modern scholars count her among the earliest and most powerful **women's voices** in Indian literature.

## Srisailam and Legacy

After staying in Kalyana for some time, Akka Mahadevi went towards **Srisailam** (in today's Andhra Pradesh) in search of her beloved god. While practising austerities in the dense **Kadali forest** there, according to tradition, she merged with Chennamallikarjuna while still young — around **1160 CE**. Caves near Srisailam are still called the 'Akka Mahadevi caves'.

Akka Mahadevi holds a very high place in Kannada literature and the history of Karnataka. There is a statue and memorial to her at Udutadi, many roads and institutions are named after her, and the state women's university at Vijayapura is now called the **'Karnataka State Akkamahadevi Women's University'**. In 2010 a 13th-century relief was found near Hospet that is believed to depict her. The poet **A. K. Ramanujan** translated her vachanas into English in his book **'Speaking of Siva'**, bringing her to readers around the world. Eight hundred years later, her vachanas are still sung in Karnataka and remain an example of devotion, freedom and courage.
