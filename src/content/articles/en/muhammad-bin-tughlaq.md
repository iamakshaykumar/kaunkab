## Background: A Learned Prince

Muhammad bin Tughluq's original name was **Jauna Khan** and he was born around 1290. He was a son of **Ghiyasuddin Tughluq**, the founder of the Tughluq dynasty, and in his father's time had already led southern campaigns such as that against Warangal.

In 1325 Ghiyasuddin died when a wooden pavilion collapsed. Some writers such as Ibn Battuta suspected a plot by the prince in this, but the charge is not proven and historians differ on it. After this, in 1325, Jauna Khan became Sultan under the name Muhammad bin Tughluq.

Contemporary writers describe him as knowledgeable in philosophy, mathematics, astronomy, Persian poetry and calligraphy. His plans were new and far-sighted, but his way of implementing them was often harsh and hasty.

## Shifting the Capital to Daulatabad

Around **1327** the Sultan ordered that Devagiri in the Deccan be renamed **Daulatabad** and made a second capital. There were reasons behind it — better control over the newly conquered southern regions, a capital situated in the middle of the empire, and distance from Mongol attacks from the north-west.

According to Barani and Ibn Battuta, the people of Delhi were forced to move more than a thousand kilometres, and many died on the way. Modern historians believe that the description of "emptying the whole of Delhi" is exaggerated and that mainly nobles, ulama, officials and their families were sent. A few years later, around 1335, because of concerns in the north and revolts in the south, the capital was returned to Delhi.

## The Token Currency Experiment

Around **1329-30** the Sultan issued copper and brass coins that were to be treated as equal in value to silver coins. At that time there was a shortage of silver in the world, and examples of paper money in China also existed. The idea was modern, but there was no arrangement to prevent the coins from being copied.

In Barani's words, people started minting coins in their own homes. A flood of fake coins came, trade came to a standstill and the value of the coins fell. Finally the Sultan took back the coins and gave real gold and silver coins in exchange, which caused heavy loss to the treasury.

## Other Plans and Revolts

- **Tax increase in the Doab:** The land revenue in the Ganga-Yamuna Doab was raised, which by chance happened at a time of famine. Discontent and revolt spread among the peasants. Later the Sultan created a separate department for agricultural reform and also gave loans to farmers.
- **Khurasan plan:** A large army was recruited and paid for a campaign against Central Asia, but the campaign did not take place and the army was disbanded.
- **Qarachil expedition (about 1333):** The army sent to the hill region of Kumaon-Garhwal was almost destroyed because of difficult geography and resistance.

From the 1330s revolts in the empire kept growing — Madurai (Ma'bar) became independent, Bengal broke away, around 1336 **Vijayanagara** in the south and in 1347 the **Bahmani Sultanate** in the Deccan were founded.

## The End and an Assessment

To suppress the revolts the Sultan was constantly on campaign. While chasing a rebel in Sindh he died near Thatta on **20 March 1351**.

In popular speech he is called "eccentric" or the "mad emperor", but most historians consider this an incomplete assessment. Many of his plans had sound reasoning behind them; the cause of failure was mostly poor preparation, harsh punishment and failure to win the people's trust. Under his rule the Sultanate became geographically the largest, but in the same period its disintegration also began.
