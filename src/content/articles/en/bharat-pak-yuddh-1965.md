## Background

After the war of 1947–48, the Kashmir question remained unresolved. After India's defeat by China in 1962 and Nehru's death in 1964, Pakistan's military ruler **Ayub Khan** came to assess that India was in a weak position. In April 1965, clashes took place between the armies of the two countries in the **Rann of Kutch** in Gujarat, and were halted through British mediation. India's Prime Minister was **Lal Bahadur Shastri**, who had taken office in June 1964.

## Operation Gibraltar

From **5 August 1965**, under **'Operation Gibraltar'**, Pakistan sent thousands of armed infiltrators across the ceasefire line into Indian-administered Kashmir. The plan was to incite a rebellion among the local population, but this aim was not achieved, and many infiltrators were reported by the locals themselves. In response, the Indian Army crossed the ceasefire line on 15 August and by 28 August had captured the strategic **Haji Pir Pass**. On 1 September, under **'Operation Grand Slam'**, Pakistan launched a large armoured attack from the Chhamb sector towards **Akhnoor**, but could not reach Akhnoor.

## War on the International Border

To ease the pressure on Kashmir, on **6 September 1965** the Indian Army crossed the international border in Punjab and began advancing towards **Lahore**. Heavy fighting took place at Burki and Dograi around the Ichhogil Canal. Pakistan still observes 6 September as 'Defence Day'.

In the Battle of **Asal Uttar** (8–10 September) near **Khem Karan**, the Indian Army stopped a major Pakistani armoured offensive, and many of its tanks were destroyed or abandoned; in this same battle Havildar **Abdul Hamid** was posthumously awarded the Param Vir Chakra. In the Sialkot sector, large tank battles were fought near **Phillora** and **Chawinda** (14–19 September), where the Indian advance was halted. In the air war neither side gained clear superiority, and both made differing claims of shooting down each other's aircraft. Naval action was limited; on 7 September the Pakistani Navy shelled **Dwarka**.

## Ceasefire and Losses

On 20 September the UN Security Council passed **Resolution 211** calling for a ceasefire. India accepted it immediately and Pakistan on 23 September; the fighting stopped from that day. According to neutral estimates, about 3,000 Indian and 3,800 Pakistani soldiers were killed, although the two sides' own figures and their claims of aircraft and tank losses differ greatly from one another. At the time of the ceasefire, India held about 1,840 sq km of Pakistani territory and Pakistan held about 540 sq km of Indian territory.

## The Tashkent Declaration

Under the mediation of Soviet Premier **Alexei Kosygin**, Shastri and Ayub Khan met in Tashkent, and on **10 January 1966** the **Tashkent Declaration** was signed. The two countries agreed to withdraw their forces to the positions held before August 1965 by 25 February 1966, and to settle disputes by peaceful means. In India some critics questioned the return of areas such as Haji Pir and the absence of a 'no-war' agreement; the declaration was also opposed in Pakistan. The next day, on **11 January 1966**, Shastri died of a heart attack in Tashkent itself.

## Assessment and Significance

Militarily, the war ended in a stalemate. Most neutral historians believe that India held some advantage on the ground at the time of the ceasefire and that Pakistan failed in its main objective of inciting a rebellion in Kashmir; in Pakistan the war is remembered as a successful defence. In India, the war restored the army's morale after 1962, and Shastri's slogan **'Jai Jawan, Jai Kisan'** became the hallmark of this period.
