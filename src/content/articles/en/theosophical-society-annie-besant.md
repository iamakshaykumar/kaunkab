## From New York to Adyar

On **17 November 1875**, in **New York** City, the Russian-born **Helena Petrovna Blavatsky**, the American army officer and lawyer **Colonel Henry Steel Olcott**, **William Quan Judge** and a few others founded the **Theosophical Society**. 'Theosophy' means 'divine wisdom'. The society had three main objects: to form a nucleus of **universal brotherhood** without distinction of race, creed, sex or colour; to encourage the comparative study of religions, philosophies and science; and to investigate the hidden powers of nature and of human beings.

Blavatsky and Olcott regarded the religions of the East — especially the Hindu and Buddhist traditions — as an ancient source of spiritual knowledge. Drawn by this, the two reached **Bombay** in **1879** and set up the society's first centre in India there. For a while they were also in contact with **Swami Dayanand Saraswati**'s Arya Samaj, but differences of outlook soon ended the association. In **1882** the society bought land on the banks of the **Adyar river** on the outskirts of Madras, and in **December 1882** moved its international headquarters there. Since then the organisation has been known as the **'Theosophical Society Adyar'**.

## The Adyar Centre and Indian Culture

The Adyar campus soon became a place of pilgrimage for theosophists from around the world. Conventions began to be held there every year, and in **1886** Olcott started the **Adyar Library**, which gathered thousands of manuscripts and texts in Sanskrit, Pali, Tamil and other languages. Olcott also worked for the revival of Buddhism in Sri Lanka and for Buddhist schools there.

In the nineteenth century, Christian missionaries and many British officials described Indian religions as backward. At such a time, when educated people from the West openly praised the Upanishads, the Gita and Buddhist philosophy, it boosted the **self-confidence** of educated Indians in their own tradition. Historians count this as one strand of the **Indian renaissance**. On the other hand, critics said the society laid too much stress on mysterious things such as secret 'Mahatmas' and miracles; in the 1880s there was a serious controversy over Blavatsky's alleged miracles. In 1895 the American section under William Judge split away, which is why 'Adyar' began to be added to the name of the Adyar-based body.

## Annie Besant Comes to India

**Annie Besant** was born in London on **1 October 1847**. After the breakdown of her marriage to the clergyman Frank Besant, she became a well-known speaker of the **National Secular Society**, and in **1877** she was prosecuted together with **Charles Bradlaugh** for publishing a pamphlet on family planning. She became a Fabian socialist and in **1888** stood by the women workers in the London 'matchgirls' strike. In **1889**, after reading Blavatsky's book 'The Secret Doctrine' and meeting her, she joined the Theosophical Society.

In **1893** Besant came to India for the first time and then made it her home. She adopted Indian dress, gave many lectures on Hindu scriptures and in **1895** published an English translation of the Bhagavad Gita, **'The Lord's Song'**. In **1898** she played a leading role in founding the **Central Hindu College** in **Banaras**, where Indian religion and ethics were taught alongside modern education. Later this college became the core of **Banaras Hindu University**, founded in **1916** through the efforts of **Pandit Madan Mohan Malaviya**.

## Besant as President

After Olcott's death in **1907**, Annie Besant was elected **president** of the society and held the post for the rest of her life, that is, until **1933**. In her time the society's branches spread around the world. In **1909** her associate **Charles Leadbeater** noticed the fourteen-year-old **Jiddu Krishnamurti** on the beach near Adyar and declared that he would become the future 'World Teacher'. Besant took Krishnamurti and his brother into her care and had them educated in England. But in **1929** Krishnamurti himself rejected this role, dissolved the organisation created for him and said that truth cannot be reached through any organisation or guru. This was a major blow for the society.

Besant gradually entered Indian politics too. She ran the newspaper **'New India'**, launched the **Home Rule League** from Adyar in **1916**, was interned in **1917**, and in December of the same year became the first woman president of the **Indian National Congress** at its Calcutta session. (The full story of the Home Rule movement is in a separate article.) Later she disagreed with Gandhiji's Non-Cooperation Movement and her influence in the Congress declined. She died at Adyar on **20 September 1933**.

## Legacy

The Theosophical Society never became a very large mass movement, yet its influence was far greater than its size. It popularised ideas such as Indian philosophy, yoga and rebirth in the West, and in India it taught the educated class to take pride in its cultural heritage. Many of its members later became involved in the Congress and in social reform. Critics kept questioning its mysticism and its claims about secret masters, but its contribution in education, libraries and translation is regarded as lasting.

Even today, the society's green campus at Adyar in Chennai, its old library and the famous **banyan tree** recall an era when thinkers of East and West sat together to discuss religion and philosophy. India remembers Annie Besant as a foreign woman who made this country her home and struggled for education and self-rule.
