## A Boy King and Early Troubles

**Amoghavarsha I** was born about 800 CE. He was the son of the mighty Rashtrakuta emperor **Govinda III**, who had led campaigns as far as north India (see: the Tripartite Struggle). After his father's death, around **814–815 CE**, at only 13–14 years of age, he ascended the throne. Sources differ slightly on the year his reign began.

Some relatives and feudatories rebelled against the boy king and removed him from power for a time. With the help of his cousin from the Gujarat branch, **Karka (Suvarnavarsha)**, he regained the throne by about **821 CE**.

## Manyakheta and the Extent of the Kingdom

Amoghavarsha shifted the capital from **Mayurkhandi** to **Manyakheta** (today's Malkhed, Kalaburagi district, Karnataka). Inscriptions claim that the city was built so as to rival 'Indra's city'. For the next roughly one hundred and fifty years Manyakheta remained the capital of the Rashtrakutas.

In the east the **Eastern Chalukyas of Vengi** were his main rivals. The Chalukya king **Vijayaditya II** had seized some territory, but Amoghavarsha defeated the Chalukyas in the battle of **Vinguvalli** and took the title 'Viranarayana'. During his reign conflicts with the Gangas and other neighbours also continued. Even so, historians remember him mainly not as warlike but as a peace-loving ruler fond of learning. One of his famous titles was **'Nripatunga'**.

## The Kavirajamarga and Kannada Literature

The **'Kavirajamarga'** (the Royal Road of Poets) of about **850 CE** is the **oldest available literary work** in the Kannada language. It is a treatise on poetics, figures of speech and grammar, part of which is believed to be based on the Sanskrit teacher Dandin's 'Kavyadarsha'. It describes the Kannada country as stretching **from the Kaveri to the Godavari** and praises the poetic skill of its people.

There is **disagreement** about its author — tradition regards it as Amoghavarsha's work, while according to many scholars the court poet **Srivijaya** was its author or co-author. The Sanskrit ethical work **'Prashnottara Ratnamalika'** is also attributed to him, but its authorship too is disputed.

## A Court of Scholars

Amoghavarsha's court was a centre for many scholars:
- **Jinasena** — a Jain teacher, author of the 'Adipurana', considered Amoghavarsha's guru.
- **Gunabhadra** — Jinasena's disciple, who completed the later part of the Adipurana.
- **Mahaviracharya** — a mathematician, who wrote the **'Ganita Sara Sangraha'** and praised Amoghavarsha in it.
- **Virasena** and the poet **Srivijaya** were also connected with this milieu.

Amoghavarsha had a deep inclination towards **Jainism**, but he also gave donations to Hindu temples — a donation of his to the Mahalakshmi temple of Kolhapur is mentioned.

## The Rashtrakutas in Foreign Eyes

In the Arabic account of 851 CE, the **'Silsilat-ut-Tawarikh'**, which is linked with the merchant **Sulaiman**, the Rashtrakuta kingdom is counted among the **four major empires** of the world of that time. Arab writers called the Rashtrakuta king 'Balhara' — historians consider this probably a form of the title 'Vallabharaja'.

## Final Years and Legacy

According to tradition, toward the end of his life Amoghavarsha several times left the work of government and adopted a religious life; some scholars believe that he undertook **sallekhana** in the Jain manner — this is not certain. He died in **878 CE** and his son **Krishna II** succeeded him.

In a reign of more than 60 years, Amoghavarsha kept the Rashtrakuta kingdom stable and made it a centre of literature and learning. In the history of Kannada literature his name is still cited as a beginning.
