## Background

In the latter half of the 19th century **Sir Syed Ahmad Khan** and the Aligarh Movement spread modern education among Muslims. Sir Syed advised Muslims to stay away from the Congress, because he feared that in electoral politics based on majority the interests of Muslims would be suppressed.

The **Partition of Bengal** of 1905 gave this debate a new turn. The new province of 'Eastern Bengal and Assam' was Muslim-majority and Dhaka became its capital. Many Muslim leaders, including the Nawab of Dhaka, **Salimullah**, supported the partition, while the Congress and the Swadeshi movement strongly opposed it. At this time news came that the British government was preparing reforms of the councils.

## The Simla Deputation (1 October 1906)

On **1 October 1906** a delegation of Muslim representatives led by **Aga Khan III** met **Lord Minto** at the Viceregal Lodge in Shimla. Sources give different figures for the number of members; the most common number is 35.

Their demand was that Muslims should get representation not only in proportion to population but on the basis of their 'political importance', and that their representatives be chosen through separate electorates. Minto expressed sympathy for these demands. The same demands were included as separate electorates in the **Morley-Minto Reforms** of 1909.

**Controversy:** For a long time many historians believed that this deputation was sponsored by British officials under the 'divide and rule' policy; the mediation of W. A. J. Archbold, principal of Aligarh College, is often mentioned. On the other hand, scholars such as the historian Syed Razi Wasti have argued that the initiative was the Muslim leaders' own and the government merely adopted a favourable attitude.

## Founding at Dhaka (30 December 1906)

From 27 to 30 December 1906 the annual session of the **All-India Muhammadan Educational Conference** was held at Dhaka, attended by about three thousand delegates. Nawab Salimullah was the host and the main venue was his family palace, **Ahsan Manzil** (some sources mention other buildings too).

On **30 December 1906**, at the end of the conference, a political session was held, presided over by **Nawab Viqar-ul-Mulk**. Nawab Salimullah proposed a separate political organisation for Muslims, which was supported by **Hakim Ajmal Khan**, **Mohammad Ali Jauhar** and other leaders. The proposal was passed and the **'All-India Muslim League'** came into existence.

## Objectives

The initial declared objectives were:

- to increase loyalty to the British government among Indian Muslims;
- to protect the political rights and interests of Muslims and convey them to the government;
- without harming these aims, not to let hostility develop towards other communities.

The League's constitution was framed in 1907. The Aga Khan became its first honorary president and the headquarters were first at Aligarh, then at Lucknow from 1910.

## The Road Ahead and Assessment

In its early years the League was a small organisation of mainly landlord and educated upper-class Muslims. The annulment of the Partition of Bengal in 1911 disappointed many of its leaders and gradually the League's course changed. **Mohammad Ali Jinnah** joined the League around 1913 (he also remained in the Congress), and in the **Lucknow Pact** of 1916 the Congress and the League together put forward constitutional demands.

The assessment of the League's founding depends on one's viewpoint. Its supporters regard it as the natural attempt of a minority community to have its own voice and protect its interests. Critics regard it as a step that benefited from the colonial 'divide and rule' policy, which put religious identity at the centre of politics. Four decades later, with the Lahore Resolution of 1940, this same organisation became the carrier of the demand for Pakistan.
