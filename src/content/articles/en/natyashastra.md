## One Text, a Whole Theatre

The **Natyashastra** is the oldest surviving Sanskrit treatise on the performing arts. It contains not just rules for writing plays but the whole world of the theatre — acting, dance, music, costume, the structure of the stage, the conduct of the audience, and even the training of performers. Most editions today have **36 chapters** and about **6,000 verses**; some manuscripts have 37 or 38 chapters.

## Bharata Muni and the Date of the Text

Tradition regards this text as the work of **Bharata Muni**. But there is no historical information about Bharata's life, and scholars also disagree on whether it is the work of one person or a compilation of many generations. Its date is also **disputed** — estimates range from 500 BCE to 500 CE, but most scholars place its first complete compilation between **c. 200 BCE and 200 CE**. Scholars such as P. V. Kane held that additions and deletions went on in it between the third and eighth centuries.

In the first chapter of the text there is a story: the gods asked Brahma for an entertainment that would please both the eye and the ear and be for everyone. Then Brahma took elements from the four Vedas and created the **Natyaveda** — the 'fifth Veda'. This is a **mythological story**, but it shows how high a status was given to drama.

## The Theory of Rasa

The most influential idea of the Natyashastra is **rasa**. According to Bharata, the aim of a good performance is not mere entertainment but to awaken in the spectator's mind a deep, savourable emotion. Its famous formula is — *vibhavanubhavavyabhicharisamyogadrasanishpattih* — that is, rasa arises from the combination of causes (vibhava), the signs that appear from them (anubhava) and fleeting feelings (vyabhichari bhava).

The text lists **eight rasas**:
- **Shringara** (love), **Hasya** (humour), **Karuna** (pathos), **Raudra** (anger)
- **Vira** (heroism), **Bhayanaka** (fear), **Bibhatsa** (disgust) and **Adbhuta** (wonder)

Along with these, eight sthayi bhavas (lasting emotions) and 33 sanchari (vyabhichari) bhavas are described. Later teachers added a ninth, **Shanta rasa** (peace), strongly supported by the Kashmiri philosopher Abhinavagupta. This is how the idea of the 'Navarasa' (nine rasas) became popular.

## Dance, Music and the Stage

The scope of the Natyashastra is very wide:
- The **fourth chapter** describes the 108 basic dance movements — the **karanas**.
- **Chapters 28 to 34** are on music — notes, shrutis, instruments and tala.
- Four kinds of acting are described: angika (body), vachika (speech), aharya (costume and make-up) and sattvika (inner feeling).
- Three shapes of the playhouse (rangashala) are described — rectangular, square and triangular.

## Commentaries and Tradition

For centuries commentaries were written on this text. The most famous among them is the **Abhinavabharati** of the Kashmiri teacher **Abhinavagupta** of the 10th–11th centuries. He explained rasa as a universal, almost spiritual experience taking place within the spectator. The tradition reached even distant regions — in Nepal in the 17th century King Jagajjyotir Malla composed works on music inspired by this very tradition.

## Importance Today

Classical dance styles such as Bharatanatyam, Kathakali, Kuchipudi and Odissi trace their roots to the Natyashastra, and a large part of Indian poetics rests on the theory of rasa. In April 2025, the manuscripts of the Natyashastra were included in UNESCO's **'Memory of the World'** register. This text, nearly two thousand years old, remains a reference for artists and scholars even today.
