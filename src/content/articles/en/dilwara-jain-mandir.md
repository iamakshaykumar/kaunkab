## Mount Abu and the Five Temples

**Mount Abu**, located on the highest hill of the Aravallis in Sirohi district of Rajasthan, has been regarded as a pilgrimage place since ancient times under the name 'Arbuda'. In the village of **Dilwara (Delwara)** here, within a single enclosure, stand five **Shvetambara Jain temples**, built between the 11th and 16th centuries:

- **Vimala Vasahi**: dedicated to the first Tirthankara, Rishabhanatha (Adinatha); the oldest.
- **Luna Vasahi**: dedicated to the 22nd Tirthankara, Neminatha; 1230 CE.
- **Pittalhar**: so named because of its largely brass metal image of Adinatha.
- **Parshvanatha Temple** (Kharatara Vasahi): three storeys, the tallest in the complex; it is built of grey stone, not white.
- **Mahavira Swami Temple**: the smallest; 1582 CE.

From outside these temples look quite plain, but once you step inside, the marble carving astonishes.

## Vimala Vasahi: A Gift of the Chaulukya Age

At that time the Abu region was under the influence of the **Chaulukya (Solanki)** kingdom of Gujarat. **Vimala Shah**, minister and commander of King **Bhima I** (c. 1022–1064), built this temple. Its date is usually given as **1031 CE** (Vikram Samvat 1088), although different sources give dates from 1026 to 1032. The open pavilion in front was added in the following century.

A popular **legend** says that Vimala Shah bought the land for the temple by covering it with silver coins; there is no contemporary evidence for this. In the open courtyard around the main sanctum is a row of small devakulikas (subsidiary shrines). The nine ceilings of the **navachowki** and the circular, layer-upon-layer descending ceiling of the **rangamandapa** (the large hall for dance and music) are its hallmarks.

## Luna Vasahi: The Contribution of Vastupala and Tejapala

About two hundred years later, in **1230 CE**, two brothers of the Porwad community, **Vastupala and Tejapala**, built the Luna Vasahi. Both were ministers of the **Vaghela** ruler of Gujarat, **Viradhavala**. The temple was built in memory of their late brother **Luniga**, and its plan is inspired by the Vimala Vasahi.

In the middle of the dome of its rangamandapa hangs a large lotus-like **pendant**. In a circular band 72 Tirthankaras are carved, and below them 360 small figures of Jain monks. The marble has been cut so thin that in many places it looks almost transparent.

## Features of the Carving

The art of Dilwara is considered the pinnacle of the **Maru-Gurjara** (Solanki) style. On the ceilings, pillars, arches and doorways are:
- scenes from Jain mythological tales, episodes from the lives of the Tirthankaras,
- Vidyadevis, dancers and musicians,
- bands of elephants, horses, battles and processions,
- fine webs of lotuses and geometric floral and leaf patterns.

All this work is on white marble, though marble is not found on Mount Abu; it was brought up the hill from distant quarries (usually said to be the Arasana quarries near Ambaji).

## Damage, Repair and the Present Form

In about 1311 CE, the temples were damaged in an attack in the time of **Alauddin Khilji**. In 1321 CE merchants of Mandor named **Bijada and Lalaga** had them repaired. In the twentieth century too, large-scale restoration was carried out (re-consecration in 1906, then between 1950 and 1965).

Even today these temples are Jain places of worship, managed by a Jain trust. Photography is not allowed inside, so the picture given here is a historic photograph taken in 1882 by Lala Deen Dayal.

## Significance

The Dilwara temples show that in medieval Gujarat and Rajasthan the **merchant and ministerial class** were great patrons of art: these temples were built not by kings but by their Jain ministers. The Solanki tradition of which the Sun Temple at Modhera and the Rani ki Vav at Patan are examples finds its finest expression at Dilwara.
