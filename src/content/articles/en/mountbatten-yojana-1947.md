## Background: Announcing a Deadline

By **1946** the Cabinet Mission Plan had failed, and the violence in Calcutta, Noakhali and Bihar had made the atmosphere very tense. In the Interim Government the Congress and the Muslim League were unable to work together. In this situation, on **20 February 1947**, British Prime Minister **Clement Attlee** announced that Britain would leave India by **June 1948** at all costs.

Along with this, **Lord Mountbatten** was made the new Viceroy in place of Lord Wavell. He reached Delhi on **22 March 1947** and took the oath of office on **24 March**. Within a few weeks he held long talks with Nehru, Patel, Gandhi, Jinnah and other leaders and concluded that a plan for a united India acceptable to both sides was no longer possible.

## How the Plan Was Made

Mountbatten's early plan, known as **"Plan Balkan"**, spoke of transferring power to the provinces separately. On seeing it at Simla in May 1947, Nehru strongly objected, because it could break the country into many pieces. After this, on the suggestion of the Viceroy's constitutional adviser **V. P. Menon**, a new plan was drawn up under which power was to be handed to two **dominions**. This plan received the approval of the British government in London.

On **2 June 1947** Mountbatten discussed it with representatives of the Congress, the Muslim League and the Sikhs. On the evening of **3 June 1947**, on **All India Radio**, Mountbatten first announced the plan, and then **Nehru**, **Jinnah** and the Sikh representative **Baldev Singh** also broadcast. That is why it is also called the **"3 June Plan"**.

## Main Provisions of the Plan

- Two **dominions** — India and Pakistan — would be created in British India, and power would be handed to their constituent assemblies.
- Members of the **Punjab and Bengal** legislative assemblies would sit separately in two parts — those of the Muslim-majority districts and those of the remaining districts. If either part voted by a majority in favour of division, the province would be divided. Bengal decided in favour of division on **20 June**, and Punjab by the end of June.
- **Sindh** and **Baluchistan** would make their own decisions; the Sindh Assembly decided to join Pakistan.
- A **referendum** would be held in the **North-West Frontier Province**. This referendum, held in July 1947, was boycotted by Khan Abdul Ghaffar Khan's Khudai Khidmatgars; among those who voted, a huge majority favoured Pakistan.
- A referendum was also held in the Muslim-majority **Sylhet** district of Assam, in which it voted in favour of joining East Bengal.
- **Boundary commissions** would be set up to fix the new borders of Punjab and Bengal.
- British paramountcy over the **princely states** would end; they could decide to join either India or Pakistan.

The Congress Working Committee and then the **All India Congress Committee** accepted the plan with a heavy heart on **14–15 June 1947**. The Muslim League council also accepted it, even though it was getting a "moth-eaten" Pakistan. Gandhiji was deeply saddened by Partition, but he did not openly oppose the Congress decision. The date of the transfer of power was brought forward from June 1948 to **15 August 1947**.

## The Indian Independence Act 1947

To give the plan legal form, the **Indian Independence Act** was brought before the British Parliament. The House of Commons passed it on **15 July** and the House of Lords on **16 July**, and on **18 July 1947** it received **royal assent**. Its main provisions were:

- From **15 August 1947**, India and Pakistan would be two independent dominions.
- The provinces of Punjab and Bengal would be divided.
- Each dominion would have its own **Governor-General**, and their **constituent assemblies** would have full legislative power.
- British paramountcy over the princely states would end.
- The British monarch's title **"Emperor of India"** would be dropped, and the office of **Secretary of State for India** would be abolished.

To arrange the division, a **Partition Committee** was formed on **7 June 1947**, which carried out the division of the army, the railways, the treasury and government property.

## The Radcliffe Line

The task of fixing the border was given to the British lawyer **Cyril Radcliffe**, who came to India on **8 July 1947**. He had never been to India before. Two commissions were formed under his chairmanship — the **Punjab Commission** (Mehr Chand Mahajan, Teja Singh, Din Mohamed, Muhammad Munir) and the **Bengal Commission** (C. C. Biswas, B. K. Mukherjea, Abu Saleh Mohamed Akram, S. A. Rahman). Because the members from the two sides could not agree, Radcliffe himself had to make the final decision.

The boundary awards were ready by **9 and 12 August**, but they were made public on **17 August 1947**, that is, two days after independence. Many of the decisions were disputed. Three of the four tehsils of Punjab's **Gurdaspur** district went to India, giving India road access to Kashmir; Pakistan alleged that Mountbatten had influenced this, which Mountbatten and Radcliffe denied. The **Chittagong Hill Tracts**, with a population about 97 per cent non-Muslim, went to Pakistan. It is said that Radcliffe destroyed his papers before leaving India.

## Significance and Legacy

The Mountbatten Plan and the Indian Independence Act brought a legal end to nearly two hundred years of British rule, and on **15 August 1947** India became independent. But this hurried division came at a heavy price. Because the border was announced late, millions of people lived in uncertainty, and during Partition an estimated **12 to 20 million** people were displaced and between **200,000 and 2 million** people were killed. Supporters argued that quick decisions were necessary to avoid a civil-war-like situation, while critics say that such haste further increased the violence and disorder. The Radcliffe Line remains the basis of the India–Pakistan and India–Bangladesh borders today.
