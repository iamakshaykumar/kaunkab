## An Extraordinary Childhood

Ramabai was born on **23 April 1858** near Mangalore into a Chitpavan Brahmin family. Her father, **Ananta Shastri Dongre**, was a Sanskrit scholar and, contrary to the custom of that time, taught Sanskrit to his wife and daughter. The family wandered across the country on pilgrimage and lived by reciting the Puranas.

In the famine of the 1870s, first her father, then her mother and sister died. Ramabai wandered on foot with her brother Srinivas and reached **Calcutta**. There, impressed by her knowledge of Sanskrit, scholars gave her the titles **'Pandita'** and **'Sarasvati'** in 1878. For a woman to receive such an honour was unusual at that time.

## Marriage, Widowhood and Pune

After her brother's death, in 1880 Ramabai married **Bipin Behari Medhvi**, a lawyer from Bengal, in a civil marriage. It was an inter-caste marriage, so it caused much discussion. Their daughter **Manorama** was born in 1881, but in February 1882 her husband died of cholera and Ramabai herself became a widow.

She moved to Pune and founded the **Arya Mahila Samaj** there, whose aim was to promote women's education and oppose child marriage. She gave testimony before the **Hunter Commission** on education, stressing the need for girls' education, women teachers and women doctors.

## England, America and Conversion

In 1883 Ramabai went to **England** to study medicine, but because of growing deafness her dream of becoming a doctor could not be fulfilled. There she adopted **Christianity**. In 1886 she went to America, and in 1887 her English book **The High-Caste Hindu Woman** was published, which gave a frank picture of child marriage, the plight of widows and the lack of education among women. Through this book and her lectures she raised the money to open schools for widows in India.

## Sharada Sadan (1889)

Returning to India, Ramabai opened the **Sharada Sadan**, a residential school for child widows, in **1889**. It began in Bombay and was soon moved to Pune. Here widows were taught skills along with their studies so that they could become self-reliant. In the beginning the institution had assured that it would not interfere with the religion of Hindu students, and many reformers of Pune were on its advisory board.

But when some of the students adopted Christianity, controversy flared up. **Bal Gangadhar Tilak** sharply criticised it in his paper 'Kesari', and many reformers also withdrew their support. Ramabai's supporters called it the free will of the students, while critics saw it as a means of conversion — historians even today view this question from different angles.

## The Mukti Mission and Famine Relief

Ramabai acquired land at **Kedgaon** near Pune and set up a large ashram, which became known as the **Mukti Mission**. In the terrible famine of **1896–97** she rescued hundreds of destitute women and children from central India and gave them shelter here. By 1900 the number of residents had reached about fifteen hundred. The mission provided training in schooling, farming, printing, weaving and other work.

## Legacy

Ramabai died at Kedgaon on **5 April 1922**; a few months earlier her daughter Manorama had died. The Mukti Mission still runs today. Ramabai is counted among the women of India's first generation who publicly questioned patriarchy, child marriage and the oppression of widows. Like Savitribai Phule, she too saw women's education as the key to social change — although her religious path also set her apart from many reformers of her time.
