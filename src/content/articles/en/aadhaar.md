## The Problem of Identity

Even after independence, crores of people in India had no reliable document to prove their identity. Poor families, migrant workers and people in remote villages had to gather different documents again and again for rations, pensions or bank accounts. On the other hand, complaints of **fake and duplicate beneficiaries** in government schemes were common.

The review committee set up after the 1999 Kargil War had suggested issuing identity cards in border areas. In the following years work was also done on a national register of citizens and a multipurpose identity card. From these ideas there later emerged the vision of a system in which every resident would get a **unique number** that could not be duplicated.

## UIDAI and Nandan Nilekani

On **28 January 2009** the **Unique Identification Authority of India (UIDAI)** was created by a notification of the Planning Commission. On **23 June 2009** **Nandan Nilekani**, co-founder of the information technology company **Infosys**, was appointed its first chairman and given the rank of a Cabinet minister. In April 2010 the project got the name **'Aadhaar'** and a new logo.

The basic idea of Aadhaar was simple: take each person's **ten fingerprints**, scans of **both irises** and a photograph of the face, along with information such as name, date of birth and address. A computer would compare this biometric data to make sure that one person got only one number. In return the person gets a **12-digit** number with which they can have their identity verified online anywhere.

## A Beginning at Tembhli

On **29 September 2010** Prime Minister **Manmohan Singh** and UPA chairperson **Sonia Gandhi** launched the Aadhaar scheme nationally from **Tembhli**, a tribal village in Maharashtra's **Nandurbar district**. On the same day **Ranjana Sonawane**, a tribal woman of the village, became the first person in the country to receive an Aadhaar number. That day ten people of the village were given Aadhaar letters.

After this, enrolment grew rapidly. Long queues formed at banks, post offices and government centres. Within a few years crores of people had joined, and by 2018 about **122 crore** people had Aadhaar. Today almost all adults in India have Aadhaar, and it is considered the **world's largest biometric identity system**.

## The Aadhaar Act and Links to Schemes

At first Aadhaar ran only on the basis of a government order; there was no law for it. In March 2016 Finance Minister **Arun Jaitley** introduced the **Aadhaar Bill** in Parliament, which became the **Aadhaar Act, 2016**. It was brought as a **Money Bill**. Opposition parties said this was done to limit the role of the Rajya Sabha; the government argued that the bill was mainly about distributing government subsidies and benefits. This Act gave UIDAI the status of a statutory body.

The government expanded the use of Aadhaar for **Direct Benefit Transfer (DBT)** in schemes such as the cooking gas subsidy, MGNREGA wages, scholarships and pensions. According to the government this removed fake beneficiaries and saved thousands of crores of rupees. Critics said that when biometric verification failed, many poor people were denied essential benefits such as rations, and that such a large database created risks to **privacy** and of **surveillance**.

## The Supreme Court: Privacy and Aadhaar

Several petitions challenging the validity of Aadhaar reached the Supreme Court. The main one among them was filed in 2012 by **Justice K. S. Puttaswamy**, a retired judge of the Karnataka High Court. During the hearing the basic question arose whether the **right to privacy** is a fundamental right under the Indian Constitution.

On **24 August 2017** a nine-judge Constitution Bench ruled **unanimously** that the right to privacy is part of Article 21 of the Constitution (life and personal liberty) and is a **fundamental right**. This judgment overturned earlier decisions in which privacy had not been treated as a fundamental right.

After this a five-judge bench heard the case on the validity of the Aadhaar Act. On **26 September 2018**, by a **4:1** majority, the court held the Aadhaar Act to be **constitutional**, but also set several limits. The majority judgment was written by **Justice A. K. Sikri**. According to the verdict:

- Aadhaar can be required for government subsidies and welfare schemes, and linking **PAN** with Aadhaar and giving Aadhaar in income tax returns would remain mandatory.
- Aadhaar would not be mandatory for opening a **bank account** or getting a **mobile SIM**.
- Private companies cannot demand Aadhaar from customers — this part of **Section 57** of the Act was struck down.
- Aadhaar cannot be made mandatory for services such as school admissions either.

**Justice D. Y. Chandrachud**, dissenting, wrote that passing the Aadhaar Act as a Money Bill was unconstitutional and that the whole Act should be struck down.

## Legacy

Aadhaar laid the foundation for digital services in India. With the 'trinity' of bank accounts, mobile phones and Aadhaar, government money began reaching beneficiaries' accounts directly, and online identity verification (e-KYC) became easier. At the same time, the Aadhaar debate prompted the country to think seriously about the right to privacy — the 2017 Puttaswamy judgment is still regarded as a guide in cases related to data protection and civil liberties. The story of Aadhaar is a story of balance between technology, governance and individual rights.
