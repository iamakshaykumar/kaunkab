## Challenge and Response

In November 1927 the British government appointed the **Simon Commission** to examine constitutional reforms in India, and it did not have a single Indian member. The Congress decided to boycott it at its Madras session in December 1927. The Secretary of State for India, **Lord Birkenhead**, had taunted that Indians should come together and produce a constitution acceptable to all. Indian leaders took this as a challenge and called an **All Parties Conference**.

## The Motilal Nehru Committee

In February 1928 the meetings of the conference began in Delhi, and on **19 May 1928** a committee to draft the constitution was formed in Bombay under the chairmanship of **Motilal Nehru**. It included **Tej Bahadur Sapru**, **Ali Imam**, **Shuaib Qureshi**, **M. S. Aney**, **Mangal Singh**, **G. R. Pradhan** and **Subhas Chandra Bose**, and **Jawaharlal Nehru** was secretary. (The list of members differs slightly in some sources.) The committee's report was placed before the All Parties Conference held at Lucknow from **28 August 1928**.

## Main Points of the Report

- India should get **dominion status** — that is, a status like the self-governing colonies such as Canada and Australia.
- The source of authority should be the people; there should be no state religion; men and women should have equal rights.
- A detailed list of **fundamental rights**, which also included adult suffrage.
- A federal structure, but with the remaining (residuary) powers with the **Centre**.
- **Separate electorates** should end; in their place there should be **joint electorates**, and where minorities are at least 10 percent, seats should be reserved for them in proportion to their population.
- Formation of provinces on the basis of **language**, and the establishment of a Supreme Court.

## Voices of Disagreement

There was not agreement on the report from every side. Within the Congress, young leaders such as **Jawaharlal Nehru** and **Subhas Bose** wanted **complete independence** instead of dominion status. At the **Calcutta session** of December 1928, whose president was Motilal Nehru, a compromise was reached on Gandhiji's initiative: if the government accepted the report within one year, well and good; otherwise the Congress would adopt the demand for Purna Swaraj and the path of civil disobedience.

At the All Parties Conference held in Calcutta at the same time, **Muhammad Ali Jinnah** put forward amendments — one-third of the seats in the central legislature for Muslims, residuary powers to the provinces, and reservation in proportion to population in Punjab and Bengal. Amid opposition from many groups including the Hindu Mahasabha, these amendments were rejected. A large part of the Muslim League, some leaders of the Sikh League and other groups also remained dissatisfied with the report.

## Jinnah's Fourteen Points (1929)

In response, Jinnah put forward his **'Fourteen Points'** before the Delhi session of the Muslim League in **March 1929** (the exact date, 9 March or 28 March, differs among sources). They included demands such as a federal constitution with residuary powers to the provinces, continuation of separate electorates, at least one-third representation for Muslims at the Centre, separation of Sind from Bombay, and a share for Muslims in the cabinets. In later negotiations these points became the basis of the Muslim League's politics.

## Significance

The British government did not accept the Nehru Report, and when the one-year deadline ended, at the **Lahore session** of December 1929 the Congress declared the goal of **Purna Swaraj**. Even so, this report was the first major attempt by Indians to write a constitution for themselves. Its ideas such as fundamental rights, adult suffrage and linguistic provinces were seen later in the Indian Constitution. On the other hand, the disagreement over it also exposed how big a challenge the question of communal representation was for the national movement.
