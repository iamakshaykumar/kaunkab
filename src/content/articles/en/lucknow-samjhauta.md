## Background: Seeking Unity after the Split

At the **Surat session** of 1907 the Congress had split into moderates (Gokhale, Pherozeshah Mehta) and extremists (Tilak, Lala Lajpat Rai, Bipin Chandra Pal). After this the extremists stayed outside the Congress for almost a decade, and **Tilak** was in Mandalay jail from 1908 to 1914.

Meanwhile the **All India Muslim League**, formed in 1906, at first followed a policy of loyalty to the British government. But with the annulment of the Partition of Bengal in 1911, questions connected with Turkey and growing discontent during the First World War, young leaders with a nationalist leaning became influential in the League. In 1913 the League also declared 'self-government' as its goal. In the same year **Muhammad Ali Jinnah** — who was already in the Congress — also joined the League.

## Reunion of the Moderates and Extremists

After his release from jail in 1914, Tilak expressed a wish to return to the Congress. **Annie Besant** and other leaders also pressed for reconciliation. After the deaths of the moderate leaders Pherozeshah Mehta and Gokhale in 1915, the opposition weakened and the way opened for the extremists' return. At the **Lucknow session** of December 1916, Tilak and his associates were again seen on the Congress platform — for the first time since Surat both factions were together.

## The Joint Sessions at Lucknow

In December 1916 the Congress and the Muslim League held their sessions at Lucknow almost simultaneously. The Congress session was presided over by **Ambica Charan Mazumdar** and the League session by **Jinnah**. Committees of both parties had already been discussing a joint reform scheme. According to the usual account, the Congress accepted the scheme on **29 December** and the League on **31 December 1916**. On the Congress side, **Tilak** is regarded as the chief architect of the pact.

## Main Points of the Pact

- The Congress accepted **separate electorates** for Muslims in the central and provincial legislative councils — which had already been introduced in the Morley-Minto Reforms of 1909.
- **One-third** of the elected Indian seats in the central council were to go to Muslims. Fixed proportions were also kept in the provinces; in provinces where Muslims were a minority they got more representation than their population (weightage), while in Muslim-majority provinces like Punjab and Bengal they got less than their population.
- A bill concerning a community would be passed only if **three-fourths** of the members of that community supported it.
- It was also demanded that elected members have a majority in the councils, that at least half the members of the executive council be Indians chosen by the elected members of the council, that provinces have more autonomy, and that the executive be separated from the judiciary.

## Significance

The Lucknow Pact was the first occasion when the Congress and the League put **a joint political demand** before the British government. It gave strength to the Home Rule movement and created confidence in the country that self-government was possible on the strength of Hindu-Muslim unity. The increased pressure on the government is counted in the background of the Montagu Declaration of 1917 and the later **Government of India Act of 1919**. The same unity was also seen later in the joint front of the Khilafat and Non-Cooperation movements.

## Criticism and Limitations

Historians regard the pact as double-edged. On one side it was a symbol of unity; on the other, in it the Congress for the first time formally recognised **separate electorates**, which further entrenched representation on the basis of religious identity in politics. Many Muslim leaders of Punjab and Bengal were dissatisfied with the lower representation in their provinces, and some Hindu leaders were against 'weightage'. In the 1920s this unity kept breaking down, and some scholars believe that accepting separate electorates inadvertently strengthened communal politics later on.
