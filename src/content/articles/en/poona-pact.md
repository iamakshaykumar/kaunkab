## Background: The Question of Representation

By the end of the 1920s the question had come to the centre of how different communities should be represented in the legislatures under the new constitutional reforms. Muslims had had separate electorates since 1909. At the **Round Table Conferences** (1930–32) held in London, **Dr. Bhimrao Ambedkar** argued that the Depressed Classes, regarded as untouchable, were a separate and deprived group even while living within Hindu society, and therefore should get the right to elect their own representatives — that is, **separate electorates**.

**Gandhi** opposed this sharply at the Second Round Table Conference (1931). His view was that giving separate electorates to the Depressed Classes would divide Hindu society permanently, and that the problem of untouchability should be solved by reform within Hindu society.

## The Communal Award (16 August 1932)

When no agreement was reached at the conferences, the British Prime Minister **Ramsay MacDonald** announced the **'Communal Award'** on **16 August 1932**. In it, along with Muslims, Sikhs, Christians and others, the Depressed Classes were also given separate electorates for some seats (71 in all) of the provincial legislatures. In addition, voters of the Depressed Classes could also vote in the general constituencies — this was called the 'double vote'.

## The Fast in Yerawada Jail

Gandhi, who was then imprisoned in **Yerawada Jail** in Pune, announced that if the separate electorate for the Depressed Classes was not withdrawn he would fast unto death. His fast began on **20 September 1932**. Anxiety spread across the country, and heavy pressure fell on Ambedkar, because responsibility for Gandhi's life was being placed on him.

For several days difficult negotiations went on between **Madan Mohan Malaviya** and other Hindu leaders and Ambedkar. In exchange for giving up the separate electorate, Ambedkar asked for far more reserved seats and an arrangement by which the candidates of the Depressed Classes would truly be the choice of their own community.

## The Main Terms of the Agreement

On **24 September 1932** the agreement was signed in Yerawada Jail. The main points were:

- Separate electorates would end; the Depressed Classes would vote only in **joint electorates**.
- **148** seats would be reserved for the Depressed Classes in the provincial legislatures — nearly double the 71 seats of the Communal Award.
- **18 per cent** of the general seats of British India in the central legislature would be reserved for the Depressed Classes.
- For every reserved seat, voters of the Depressed Classes would first elect a panel of four candidates through a primary election; this arrangement was for ten years.
- It was also stated that there would be no discrimination against the Depressed Classes in government jobs and education, and that efforts would be made to ensure fair representation for them.

The British government accepted the agreement, and on 26 September 1932 Gandhi broke his fast.

## Results and Differing Assessments

After the Poona Pact Gandhi intensified the campaign against untouchability, and in 1932 itself the organisation was formed that later came to be called the **Harijan Sevak Sangh**. This same system of reserved seats later became the basis of the Government of India Act 1935 and then of the reserved constituencies for Scheduled Castes in the Constitution of independent India.

The assessment of this agreement is still divided today. Many regard it as a step that saved the unity of Hindu society and got more seats for Dalits. Ambedkar, however, wrote later that because of the influence of upper-caste-dominated voters in joint electorates it became difficult to elect true representatives of the Dalits, and that the agreement had been made under pressure. In this way the Poona Pact remains a central milestone in the debate on representation and social justice in Indian democracy.
