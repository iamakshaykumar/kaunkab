## The Boy King of Fergana

**Zahir-ud-din Muhammad Babur** was born on **14 February 1483** at Andijan in the Fergana Valley (present-day Uzbekistan). On his father **Umar Shaikh Mirza**'s side he was a descendant of Timur, and on his mother **Qutlugh Nigar Khanum**'s side of Genghis Khan.

After his father's sudden death in 1494, Babur became ruler of Fergana at about twelve years of age. His childhood was spent in struggle, surrounded by uncles, cousins and Uzbek chiefs.

## The Dream of Samarkand and Kabul

**Samarkand**, Timur's capital, was Babur's greatest dream. He conquered it three times, in **1497**, **1501** and **1511**, but lost it each time. There was a period when he had neither a kingdom nor a settled home.

In **1504** he took **Kabul**, which remained his base for the next twenty-two years. From here he made several expeditions towards Hindustan, and finally, with his victories at Panipat in 1526 and Khanwa in 1527, laid the foundation of Mughal rule in India.

## The Baburnama: A Unique Book

Babur wrote his memoirs in his mother tongue, **Chagatai Turkic**, which he called "Turki". It is also called the *Tuzuk-i-Baburi* or the *Waqai*.

- It begins with his accession in 1494 and runs up to the events of 1529, though there are gaps of several years in between.
- Babur writes openly about his defeats, his mistakes, his drinking and his later decision to give it up, and about his personal feelings. The official histories of that period were generally full of praise for the ruler, so this frankness is regarded as extraordinary.
- He sketches the character of both friends and opponents — whose handwriting was good, who was a good poet, who turned out a coward.

## Babur's View of Hindustan

A large part of the Baburnama is devoted to a description of India. Babur described in fine detail its trees and plants, fruits (such as the mango and the banana), animals and birds, rivers, climate and methods of counting. Many historians regard these passages as written with the eye of a naturalist.

At the same time he also criticised Hindustan — he complained about the lack of gardens with running water, of good horses and of cold fruits. To make up for this lack he had Persian-style charbaghs built on the bank of the Yamuna at Agra. This criticism was the view of a Central Asian outsider and should be read in that context.

## Translations and Illustrated Manuscripts

On Akbar's order, **Abdul Rahim Khan-i-Khanan** made a Persian translation of the Baburnama in 1589–90. After this, Akbar's court painters prepared several illustrated copies of it, whose miniatures are scattered today in many museums of the world.

The first English translation was made in 1826 by Leyden and Erskine from the Persian text. Later the translations of **Annette Beveridge** (1922) and Wheeler Thackston (2002) were considered more authentic.

## Last Days and Legacy

Babur died at Agra on **26 December 1530**. His daughter **Gulbadan Begum** wrote in the *Humayun-nama* a famous episode that Babur walked around the sickbed of the ailing Humayun and prayed that his illness be transferred to himself; Humayun recovered and Babur fell ill. This should be read as a family tradition, not as an established fact.

Babur was also a poet, and wrote ghazals in Turkic and Persian and a work on prosody. His image as a conqueror is a mixed one, but the Baburnama presents him as a human being who was curious, sensitive and reflective.
