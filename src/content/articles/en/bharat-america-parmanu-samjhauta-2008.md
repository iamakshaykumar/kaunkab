## The Background of Isolation

India never signed the **Nuclear Non-Proliferation Treaty (NPT)**, because it believed the treaty divided the world in a discriminatory way into 'nuclear-weapon' and 'non-nuclear-weapon' states. After the first nuclear test of **1974**, countries with nuclear technology formed the **Nuclear Suppliers Group (NSG)** and almost stopped supplying nuclear fuel and equipment to India. After the **1998** Pokhran tests, several countries including the United States also imposed sanctions on India.

This isolation affected India's nuclear power programme. The country has little uranium, so several reactors were running below capacity. On the other hand, a growing economy needed more electricity.

## The Joint Statement of 18 July 2005

On **18 July 2005** in Washington, Prime Minister **Dr. Manmohan Singh** and US President **George W. Bush** issued a joint statement. The United States said it would change its laws to allow full civil nuclear cooperation with India and would ask friendly countries to do the same. In return India promised to **separate its civil and military nuclear facilities**, to place its civil facilities under the safeguards of the **International Atomic Energy Agency (IAEA)**, and to continue its voluntary moratorium on nuclear testing.

On **2 March 2006**, during Bush's visit to India, agreement was reached in New Delhi on the **separation plan**. Under it India agreed to place **14** of its 22 operating and under-construction power reactors under international safeguards in phases by 2014. Facilities linked to the military programme and the fast breeder reactors were kept out of safeguards. In December 2006 the US Congress passed the **Hyde Act**, which created the legal path for cooperation with India. In 2007 the two countries finalised the text of the **123 Agreement**.

## Storm in Parliament

The agreement set off a fierce political debate in India. The government argued that it would give India energy security, end nuclear isolation and keep its strategic programme safe. The opposition **BJP** said that the agreement and the Hyde Act placed an indirect curb on India's freedom to conduct nuclear tests. The **Left Front**, which was supporting the UPA government from outside, objected that it would tilt India's foreign policy towards the United States.

When the government moved ahead with talks with the IAEA, the Left parties withdrew support from the government in **July 2008**. On **22 July 2008** a confidence vote was held in the Lok Sabha, which the government survived by **275** votes to **256**. The Samajwadi Party sided with the government this time. Before the vote, some BJP MPs displayed wads of currency notes in the House and alleged that they had been offered bribes to vote for the government. This **"cash-for-votes"** affair was investigated by a parliamentary committee and later in court.

## IAEA, NSG and the Signing

After the confidence vote was won, the way opened quickly. On **1 August 2008** the IAEA Board approved a special safeguards agreement for India. The hardest step was the **NSG**, whose decisions are taken by consensus. Some countries such as Austria, Ireland and New Zealand expressed concerns about non-proliferation. After long negotiations, and India's assurance that it would continue its voluntary moratorium on nuclear testing and would not join an arms race, the NSG granted India a **special waiver** in Vienna on **6 September 2008**.

The US Congress then approved the agreement, and on **8 October 2008** President Bush signed the related law. On **10 October 2008** in Washington, External Affairs Minister **Pranab Mukherjee** and US Secretary of State **Condoleezza Rice** signed the **123 Agreement**. Pakistan called it discriminatory, while China, despite initial objections in the NSG, did not ultimately block the waiver.

## Outcome and Legacy

After the NSG waiver, India signed nuclear cooperation agreements with France, Russia, Canada, Kazakhstan, Australia and several other countries. Uranium began to arrive from abroad, allowing the safeguarded reactors to run at full capacity. Diplomatically, the agreement took India-US relations to a new height and won India wide recognition as a responsible nuclear power.

But plans for American companies to build reactors in India could not move forward for a long time. A major reason was India's **Civil Liability for Nuclear Damage Act 2010**, which fixed liability on equipment suppliers in the event of an accident; foreign companies saw this as a major risk for themselves. In **December 2025** Parliament passed the **SHANTI Act**, which replaced the Atomic Energy Act of 1962 and the liability Act of 2010 and opened the way for private-sector participation in nuclear power generation. The government called it a historic reform, while some opposition parties expressed concern over the reduced supplier liability.

In this way the process that began in 2005 is counted among the biggest turning points in India's foreign and energy policy.
