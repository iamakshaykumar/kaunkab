## From England to Baroda

**Aurobindo Ghose** was born on **15 August 1872** in **Calcutta**. His father wanted him raised entirely in the English manner, so in **1879**, at about seven years of age, he was sent to England. There he studied at St Paul's School in London and, from **1890**, at **King's College**, Cambridge. He sat the Indian Civil Service (ICS) examination but deliberately failed the horse-riding test, and in **1892** returned without the ICS.

From **February 1893** until about **1906** he was in the service of the **Baroda** princely state and also taught at Baroda College. Here he read Sanskrit, Bengali and Indian texts deeply, and secretly made contact with the revolutionary organisations of Bengal.

## The Voice of Extremist Nationalism

After the Partition of Bengal in **1905**, Aurobindo came fully into politics. In **1906** he became the first principal of the **National College** in Calcutta. The policy of the English daily **'Bande Mataram'**, started by **Bipin Chandra Pal** in **August 1906**, soon began to be set by Aurobindo, though because of his Baroda job he did not allow his name to be printed as editor. According to accounts from the ashram, he was among the first Indian leaders to publicly declare **complete independence** as the political goal. In a series of articles he explained swadeshi, boycott, national education and the doctrine of "passive resistance".

On **16 August 1907** he was arrested on a charge of sedition, but it could not be proved that he was the editor, and on **23 September 1907** he was acquitted; Bipin Chandra Pal was sentenced to six months in jail for refusing to testify. In the same year, at the **Surat Congress**, he sided with **Tilak** and the extremist party. His younger brother **Barindra Kumar Ghosh** was running a secret revolutionary group in Calcutta, and Aurobindo is regarded as its ideological inspiration.

## The Alipore Bomb Case (1908–09)

After the bomb attack by Khudiram Bose and Prafulla Chaki at Muzaffarpur on **30 April 1908**, the police on **2 May 1908** arrested about three dozen people. Barindra and his associates were caught at the garden house in Maniktala, while **Aurobindo** was arrested from Grey Street in Calcutta.

The trial began on **19 October 1908** before Judge **C. P. Beachcroft**. Aurobindo was defended by the young lawyer **Chittaranjan Das**, whose long and impassioned argument became famous. In the verdict of **6 May 1909**, seventeen accused, including Aurobindo, were acquitted, while Barindra Ghosh and Ullaskar Dutta were sentenced to death, later commuted to life imprisonment.

Historians do not agree on Aurobindo's real role in this case. Sufficient evidence against him was not found in court, but many historians believe that he was certainly the ideological guide of Bengal's revolutionary movement.

## A Year in Jail and the Uttarpara Speech

The year of about twelve months in Alipore Jail proved a turning point in Aurobindo's life. According to accounts from the ashram, he spent this time mostly in studying the **Gita** and the **Upanishads**, in meditation and yoga practice. After his release, in a speech at **Uttarpara** on **30 May 1909**, he said publicly for the first time that in jail he saw **Vasudeva (Krishna)** everywhere — in the walls, the trees and his fellow prisoners. These are descriptions of his own spiritual experience, which devotees and historians view each in their own way.

After his release he started the paper **'Karmayogin'**, but his mind had begun to turn away from politics.

## Pondicherry: The Philosopher Sri Aurobindo

In **1910**, amid fears of a new sedition case, he secretly reached the French settlement of **Chandernagore** and then **Pondicherry**. A warrant was issued against him on **4 April 1910**, but by then he was outside British territory. After this he never took part in active politics.

From **1914** to **1921** he published the philosophical monthly **'Arya'**, in which his major works appeared in serial form. In **1920** **Mirra Alfassa** of France (later called "The Mother") settled in Pondicherry. After **24 November 1926** Aurobindo went into seclusion and the Mother took over the running of the **Sri Aurobindo Ashram**. His major works are **'The Life Divine'**, **'The Synthesis of Yoga'** and the epic **'Savitri'** of about 24,000 lines. The central idea of his "Integral Yoga" was that the evolution of human consciousness continues further.

He died at Pondicherry on **5 December 1950**. A young man with an English education, a leader of the extremist party, an accused in a sedition case and a yogi-philosopher, all in one life — Aurobindo's journey is unique in Indian history.
