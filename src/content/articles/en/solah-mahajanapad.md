## From Janapada to Mahajanapada

In the Vedic period there were "janas", that is, tribes, and the land on which a jana settled was called its **janapada** — literally "the place where the jana set its foot". By about the sixth century BCE, with the growth of iron tools, paddy cultivation, trade and new cities in the Gangetic plain, some of these janapadas became large and powerful. They were called **Mahajanapadas**.

Historians also call this period the "**second urbanisation**", because after Harappa, big cities arose again in India for the first time. At this time silver **punch-marked coins** came into use and Northern Black Polished Ware (NBPW) began to be made.

## The List in the Anguttara Nikaya

The Buddhist text **Anguttara Nikaya** has a list of sixteen Mahajanapadas. Their capitals (as Buddhist and other sources tell us) were as follows:

- **Anga** — Champa
- **Magadha** — Rajagriha (Girivraja), later Pataliputra
- **Kashi** — Varanasi
- **Kosala** — Shravasti (Ayodhya/Saketa was also a major city)
- **Vajji** — Vaishali
- **Malla** — Kushinagar and Pava
- **Chedi** — Shuktimati (Sotthivati)
- **Vatsa** — Kaushambi
- **Kuru** — Indraprastha
- **Panchala** — Ahichchhatra (northern) and Kampilya (southern)
- **Matsya** — Viratanagara (present-day Bairat, Rajasthan)
- **Shurasena** — Mathura
- **Ashmaka** — Potana (Godavari region)
- **Avanti** — Ujjayini (Mahishmati was the capital of the southern part)
- **Gandhara** — Taxila
- **Kamboja** — Rajapura (location uncertain)

Scholars disagree in many places about the dating of these lists and the identification of the capitals.

## Monarchies and Gana-sanghas

Most Mahajanapadas were **monarchies**, where a hereditary king ruled. But some were **gana-sanghas**, where power lay in the hands of one clan or a group of clans and decisions were taken in an assembly. The **Vajji** confederacy (in which the Licchavis were prominent) and the **Mallas** are the most famous examples. Both the Buddha and Mahavira had a close connection with gana-sanghas — Mahavira was born in the Vajji region and the Buddha's Mahaparinirvana took place at Kushinagar of the Mallas.

## The Four Great Powers

By the time of the Buddha, four of the sixteen states had become the most powerful — **Magadha, Kosala, Vatsa and Avanti**. Kosala had absorbed Kashi. The stories of **Udayana**, king of Vatsa, and **Chanda Pradyota** of Avanti became very popular in later literature. Among these states both continuous wars and marriage alliances went on.

## The Victory of Magadha

In the end the prize went to **Magadha**. **Bimbisara** of the Haryanka dynasty (about the sixth century BCE) conquered Anga and formed marriage ties with Kosala. His son **Ajatashatru** defeated the Vajji confederacy and also fought Kosala. Later the Shishunaga dynasty merged Avanti into Magadha, and from about 345 BCE the **Nanda dynasty** established sole rule over a large part of the Ganga valley.

Many reasons are given for Magadha's success — fertile land, nearby iron reserves, trade through rivers such as the Ganga and the Son, an army of elephants, and ambitious rulers. On this very foundation the Mauryan empire later arose, and the age of the sixteen Mahajanapadas turned into a vast empire.
