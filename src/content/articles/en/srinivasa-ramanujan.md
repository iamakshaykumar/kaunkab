## The Boy from Kumbakonam

**Srinivasa Ramanujan** was born on **22 December 1887** at **Erode** in Tamil Nadu. His childhood was spent in **Kumbakonam**, where his father was a clerk in a sari shop. His talent for mathematics began to show while he was still at school.

At about the age of **16** (1903) he got hold of **G. S. Carr's** book *A Synopsis of Elementary Results in Pure and Applied Mathematics*. It gave about five thousand theorems and formulas without detailed proofs. Ramanujan tried to prove them one by one himself, and from here began the series of his own discoveries. His notebooks are filled in this same style — many results, few proofs.

## Failure in College, Yet Mathematics

Being absorbed in mathematics also had a cost. At the Government College in Kumbakonam he failed in other subjects and lost his scholarship. Later at **Pachaiyappa's College** in Madras too he could not pass the Fellow of Arts examination in **1906** and then in 1907, and he left his studies without a degree. These years were spent in poverty and illness.

In **1909** he married **Janaki** (Janaki Ammal). In search of work he met many people, and some officials who loved mathematics helped him. In **1911** his first research paper (on Bernoulli numbers) was published in the *Journal of the Indian Mathematical Society*, and from **1 March 1912** he began working as a clerk at the **Madras Port Trust** at 30 rupees a month.

## The Letter to Hardy (1913)

On **16 January 1913** Ramanujan sent a long letter to the famous Cambridge mathematician **G. H. Hardy**, in which he had written a great number of theorems without proofs. Hardy examined them together with his colleague **J. E. Littlewood**. Some results were already known, some were even wrong, but many were completely new and extraordinary. Hardy replied on **8 February 1913** and began efforts to bring Ramanujan to Cambridge.

For religious reasons, Ramanujan and his family were at first hesitant about a sea voyage. In the end he sailed on **17 March 1914** and reached London on **14 April 1914**.

## The Cambridge Years

At Cambridge the partnership of Ramanujan and Hardy produced many important research papers. Hardy favoured modern rigorous proofs, while Ramanujan reached results through intuition — each learned from the other. Their joint work on the partition function (the number of ways a number can be split into a sum of smaller numbers) is especially famous.

In **March 1916** he received the degree of **B.A. by Research** for his work on "highly composite numbers", which was later called a Ph.D. On **2 May 1918** he was elected a Fellow of the **Royal Society** (FRS), and in **October 1918** he became the first Indian to be a Fellow of **Trinity College**.

A famous incident belongs to this period. Hardy, who had come to see the ailing Ramanujan, said that the taxi he came in had the number **1729** — "a rather dull number". Ramanujan at once said that it was very interesting: it is the smallest number that can be written as the sum of two cubes in two different ways (1³ + 12³ and 9³ + 10³). Since then 1729 has been called the "Hardy–Ramanujan number".

## Illness and the Last Years

The cold of England, the difficulty of vegetarian food in wartime, and loneliness ruined his health. At that time doctors suspected tuberculosis (TB); some later researchers have suggested the possibility of hepatic amoebiasis — even today there is no full agreement on the real illness.

In **1919** he returned to India. Despite his illness he continued his work and in his last year wrote on deep concepts such as the **"mock theta functions"**. On **26 April 1920**, at the age of only **32**, he died at Kumbakonam.

## Legacy

In his short life Ramanujan recorded about **3,900 results** (mainly formulas and identities). Mathematicians have been working on his notebooks for decades, and many of his conjectures were later proved correct. Today his ideas are considered useful from number theory to physics.

On the occasion of his 125th birth anniversary, the Government of India declared his birthday, **22 December**, as **National Mathematics Day**, and 2012 was celebrated as National Mathematics Year. Ramanujan's story also reminds us how important people are who recognise and support talent — whether the officials of Madras or Hardy of Cambridge.
