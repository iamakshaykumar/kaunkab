## Background: From Sistan to Ajmer

Khwaja Moinuddin Chishti was born in the mid-12th century (generally said to be between about 1141 and 1143) in the **Sistan** region on the Iran-Afghanistan border. His father died when he was young. According to tradition, he gave up his property and, in search of knowledge, travelled to centres such as Khurasan, Bukhara and Samarkand.

In the Nishapur region he became a disciple of the Sufi master **Khwaja Usman Harooni** and stayed with him for a long time. This teacher-disciple link ties him to the **Chishti order** — a Sufi tradition named after the town of Chisht in Afghanistan.

## Settling in Ajmer

On coming to India he first went to Lahore, where his stay at the tomb of the Sufi saint Data Ganj Bakhsh (Ali Hujwiri) is well known by tradition. After this he reached **Ajmer** via Delhi and settled there. The year of his arrival in Ajmer is not certain; it is generally placed at about 1192, that is, close to the time of the Second Battle of Tarain.

Many miracle stories linked with him — such as stories of a clash with the local ruler on reaching Ajmer — are found in biographies written centuries later. Historians regard these as a tradition of faith, not contemporary history. Contemporary evidence is scarce, so many details of his life rest on conjecture.

## Teachings of the Chishti Order

The Chishti Sufis became known for certain special features:
- **Simplicity and renunciation** — keeping away from the courts of rulers and from government grants.
- **Khanqah** — a hospice where people of every religion and class could come.
- **Langar** — feeding the hungry and the needy.
- **Sama** — songs of love for God and qawwali, which the Chishti tradition regarded as a means of devotion.
- Adopting local languages and customs, so that ordinary people could connect with them.

Because of his compassion towards the poor and helpless he was called **'Gharib Nawaz'** (one who shows kindness to the poor).

## The Line of Disciples: From Ajmer to Delhi

Khwaja Moinuddin's chief disciple **Qutbuddin Bakhtiyar Kaki** settled in Delhi, where Sultan Iltutmish held him in great respect. His disciple was **Baba Farid** (of Ajodhan/Pakpattan), whose compositions were later included in the Guru Granth Sahib too. Baba Farid's disciple **Nizamuddin Auliya** took this tradition to its peak in Delhi. Disciples such as Hamiduddin Nagauri at Nagaur also spread it.

## Death, Dargah and Urs

Khwaja Moinuddin died in Ajmer in **1236**. On his death anniversary every year, in the month of Rajab of the Islamic calendar, the **Urs** is celebrated, to which lakhs of devotees come from across the country and abroad.

The dargah received the patronage of many rulers. A visit by Sultan Muhammad bin Tughluq in 1332 is mentioned. In the Mughal period **Akbar** visited many times, and later Mughal rulers added buildings and donations to the complex. Today's dargah complex is a meeting of buildings made in different centuries.

## Significance

Khwaja Moinuddin Chishti is counted among the most influential figures of the Sufi tradition in India. At his dargah Hindus, Muslims, Sikhs and people of other religions come with equal reverence. Historians regard the Chishti order as an important part of the shared culture of medieval India, in which the Bhakti and Sufi currents gave a message of love, equality and service.
