## What Was a Mansab?

"Mansab" is an Arabic-Persian word meaning post or rank. In the Mughal Empire every major officer — whether a general, a governor or some officer of the court — was a **mansabdar**, that is, he held a numerical rank. This number decided what his standing at court was, how much salary he would get, and how many horsemen he had to maintain for the Emperor's service. The same person could be given military work at one time and administrative work at another. It is worth noting that the qazis of the judicial department remained outside this system.

## Establishment by Akbar

The tradition of ranks based on the decimal system had come down from Mongol and Timurid rule, but it was **Akbar** who gave it the form of an organised system. Historians do not agree on the exact date of its start — different scholars give 1573–74 and the years around it, and the system went on developing until about 1595. The Ain-i-Akbari lists ranks from ten to ten thousand. In Akbar's time the mansab of most senior nobles was limited to five thousand (in his last years a few nobles such as Raja Man Singh received seven thousand); under later rulers seven thousand became the usual upper limit for nobles, and princes received far higher ranks.

## Zat and Sawar

From about 1595 every mansab came to be written in two numbers:

- **Zat** — the officer's personal rank and the salary attached to it.
- **Sawar** — how many horsemen he had to maintain.

Historians debate the exact interpretation of this double number. In Jahangir's time came the system of **du-aspa sih-aspa** (two-horse, three-horse), by which a trusted noble could be made to maintain more horsemen without raising his zat. To stop fraud, the practices of putting the royal brand on horses (**dagh**) and recording soldiers' descriptions (**chehra**) were enforced, and the Mir Bakhshi checked them. The tradition of branding horses is found in the time of Alauddin Khalji and Sher Shah too.

## Jagir: The Way of Paying Salary

A mansabdar was paid either in cash (**naqdi**) or in the form of a **jagir** — that is, the right to collect the estimated land revenue from an area. The jagirdar was not the owner of the land; land that yielded income to the Emperor himself was called **khalisa**. There was often a gap between the estimate of revenue (**jama**) and the actual collection (**hasil**), so salaries and responsibilities were raised or lowered in proportion. Raja Todar Mal's land revenue system prepared the basis for these estimates.

Jagirs were generally changed every few years, so that no noble could build permanent power in any area. The ancestral lands of Rajput kings (**watan jagir**) were an exception to this. This transfer also had a bad effect — with a jagir held for a short time, jagirdars tried to extract as much as possible, and the burden fell on the peasants.

## Importance and Crisis

This system bound Iranian, Turani, Afghan, Rajput, Maratha and Indian Muslim nobles into a common framework and connected them directly to the Emperor. Their advancement depended on the Emperor's favour and service, not on lineage.

At the end of the seventeenth century, especially during Aurangzeb's Deccan campaigns, the number of mansabdars rose rapidly but there were not as many good jagirs to give out. Historians such as Satish Chandra, Athar Ali and Irfan Habib hold different views on the causes of this **jagirdari crisis**. After 1707 mansabs gradually became merely honorific and hereditary, and the empire's central grip loosened.
