## Background: The Search for Spices

In the 15th century there was heavy demand in Europe for spices such as black pepper, cinnamon, cloves and ginger. These spices came from India and Southeast Asia through Arab, Persian and Gujarati traders, passing through West Asia to Italian cities such as Venice and Genoa. Because they passed through so many hands in between, their prices in Europe were very high.

To bypass this chain and reach India directly, Portugal sent sea expeditions for decades along the western coast of Africa. In 1488 Bartolomeu Dias rounded the southern tip of Africa and showed that the Indian Ocean could be reached by sea.

## The Voyage from Lisbon to Calicut

On the orders of King **Manuel I**, **Vasco da Gama** set out from Lisbon in July 1497 with a fleet of four ships. He passed the Cape of Good Hope and, going up the east coast of Africa through Mozambique and Mombasa, reached **Malindi** (in present-day Kenya).

At Malindi he got an experienced local guide (pilot), who took the fleet across the Arabian Sea with the help of the monsoon winds. Historians disagree about the identity of this pilot; he is often said to be the famous Arab navigator Ahmad ibn Majid, but most scholars do not regard this as proven.

On **20 May 1498** the fleet arrived near Calicut. The ships dropped anchor near **Kappad**, a little north of the city of Calicut; some sources also give the date of reaching the coast as 17 or 18 May.

## The Zamorin's Court

At that time Calicut was the most prosperous port of the Malabar coast, where Arab, Gujarati, Chinese and other traders came and went. The rulers here were called **Samuthiri**, written as **Zamorin** in European sources.

The Zamorin welcomed Vasco da Gama, but relations soon became tense. The gifts brought by the Portuguese — cloth, hats, honey and so on — were considered ordinary for a rich trading city like Calicut. The established Arab merchants were also wary of the new competitors. Even so, Vasco da Gama returned at the end of 1498 with some spices and reached Lisbon in 1499, where the voyage was regarded as a great success.

## The Portuguese Presence in India

After this voyage Portugal sent fleets to India continuously:
- In 1500 **Pedro Álvares Cabral** came to Calicut; after clashes the Portuguese built relations with the king of **Cochin**.
- In 1502 Vasco da Gama came to India a second time. On this expedition he committed severe violence at sea and at Calicut, which historians describe.
- In 1510 **Afonso de Albuquerque** took **Goa** from the Bijapur Sultanate, and it later became the centre of Portuguese power in India.
- To control sea trade the Portuguese introduced the **cartaz** (permit) system, and ships sailing without one were attacked.

In 1524 Vasco da Gama came to India a third time as viceroy, but died in Cochin within a few months.

## Historical Significance

The sea route from Europe to India directly connected the Indian Ocean with European trade. After this, Dutch, English and French companies also came to India, and this rivalry that began with trade later led to colonial rule. Goa, Daman and Diu remained under Portuguese control until 1961.

At the same time it is important to remember that Indian Ocean trade had been flourishing for centuries before Vasco da Gama. The Portuguese were newcomers to that rich trading world, which Indian, Arab, African and Southeast Asian traders had built together.
