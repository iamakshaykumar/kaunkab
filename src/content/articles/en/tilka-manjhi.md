## Background: The Company Eyes the Hills

After receiving the Diwani in **1765**, the East India Company began collecting revenue from Bengal, Bihar and Orissa. The **Rajmahal Hills** around Bhagalpur and the jungle-terai below them were until then largely the domain of independent tribal communities. The main inhabitants were the **Pahariya** people, who lived by shifting cultivation, hunting and forest produce. As zamindars and traders from the plains began clearing forests to extend cultivation, conflict grew between the hill people and the plains.

The terrible **famine of 1770** weakened the region further. The Company, treating the Pahariyas as "plunderers", kept sending military expeditions against them. Amid this tension grew the resistance whose hero, in folk tradition, is **Tilka Manjhi**.

## Who Was Tilka Manjhi

According to popular accounts, Tilka Manjhi was born on **11 February 1750** in the village of **Tilakpur** near **Sultanganj** in present-day Bihar. Historians consider so precise a date unreliable. His identity is also disputed: in folk tradition and in the memory of Jharkhand and Bihar he is regarded as a **Santhal**, while several historians describe him as belonging to the **Pahariya** community. British records mention a rebel of this area named **"Jabra Pahariya"**, and many writers consider the two to be the same person.

It is said that from childhood Tilka saw his people's land being taken in the forests and the excesses of zamindars and Company agents. He organised tribal youths and made the forest his battlefront. Bows and arrows and the slingshot (gulel) were his main weapons, and his knowledge of the dense forest and hill paths was his greatest strength.

## Cleveland's Policy and the Struggle

In **1779** **Augustus Cleveland** became Collector of Bhagalpur and Rajmahal. Instead of force alone, he adopted a policy of "conciliation". Pahariya chiefs were given allowances, exempted from some taxes, and a local military unit was raised from Pahariya youths, later known as the **"Bhagalpur Hill Rangers"**. For the Company, this policy was a way to gain control over the hills.

People like Tilka Manjhi did not accept this arrangement. According to folk tales his companions raided Company posts and treasuries and distributed the seized grain among the poor. The most famous story is that in **1784** Tilka, hiding in a palm tree, wounded Cleveland with a poisoned arrow, causing his death.

Historians treat this story with caution. According to contemporary records, Cleveland fell ill in **January 1784** and died aboard a ship near the Hooghly during a sea voyage taken for treatment; he was buried in Calcutta. Historian **Dinesh Narayan Verma** has written that government records up to 1965 do not mention the name "Tilka Manjhi", and that the name first appears in books from around 1970. On the other hand, the memory of Tilka is said to have survived for generations in the oral tradition of tribal society.

## The Story of Capture and Sacrifice

According to tradition, the Company sent troops to crush this resistance and surrounded Tilka's base in the **Tilapore forest**. For several weeks he kept evading the British force, but was finally captured when supplies ran out under the siege. The story goes that he was tied to a horse's tail and dragged to **Bhagalpur**, and hanged from a **banyan tree** near the Collector's residence. Popular accounts give the date as **13 January 1785**.

This account is not fully confirmed by written British documents, but the story has become an inseparable part of the memory of both tribal and non-tribal society in Bihar and Jharkhand. Tilka is often called the **"first tribal freedom fighter"**, since his struggle is believed to have taken place about seventy years before **1857**.

## Legacy

The memory of Tilka Manjhi lives on in many forms. In **1984–85** his statue was installed in Bhagalpur at the spot where, by tradition, he was hanged; the place is now called **Tilka Manjhi Chowk**. In **1991** **Bhagalpur University** was renamed **Tilka Manjhi Bhagalpur University**. There is also a statue of him in **Dumka** in Jharkhand.

In literature, **Mahasweta Devi** wrote a novel on his life, and his name appears in tribal songs as a symbol of bravery. This resistance in the Rajmahal Hills is regarded as part of the background to the larger movements that later took place in the same region, such as the **Santhal Hul of 1855**.

From the point of view of historiography, the Tilka Manjhi episode also shows that a large part of tribal history was never recorded in colonial papers and survived through oral tradition. So, in understanding his story, it is important to keep both folk memory and archival evidence in view together.
