## Childhood: Manu of Varanasi

Rani Lakshmibai was born in Varanasi. Her childhood name was **Manikarnika**, and people lovingly called her **Manu**. The year of her birth differs in the sources — most historians place it between 1827 and 1830; in India her birth anniversary is traditionally celebrated on 19 November.

Her father was **Moropant Tambe**, and her mother **Bhagirathi Bai** died when Manu was only four years old. Moropant later became attached to the Bithoor court of Peshwa Baji Rao II. There Manu learned horse riding, swordsmanship and marksmanship along with the boys.

## Rani of Jhansi and the Doctrine of Lapse

In 1842 Manu was married to King **Gangadhar Rao** of Jhansi and received a new name — **Lakshmibai**. In 1851 a son was born to her, but he died within a few months. Later the king adopted a child of a relative, **Anand Rao**, who was named **Damodar Rao**. Gangadhar Rao died on 21 November 1853.

According to the **Doctrine of Lapse** of Governor-General **Lord Dalhousie**, if the ruler of a dependent state had no son of his own, an adopted heir was not recognised and the state passed to the Company. The queen sent several petitions for Damodar Rao's right, but they were rejected. In 1854 Jhansi was merged into the Company's territory, and the queen was given a pension and asked to leave the fort.

## 1857: The Fire of Revolt

In June 1857 the sepoys stationed at Jhansi also rose in revolt. After this, for several months the queen managed the rule of Jhansi — she organised an army, strengthened the fortifications of the fort and faced attacks by neighbouring chiefs. At first there was confusion among the British too about her role, but soon they began to regard her as a leading figure of the revolt.

## The Siege of Jhansi (March–April 1858)

In March 1858 the army of the British general **Sir Hugh Rose** besieged Jhansi. From 22 March a heavy bombardment began. Tatya Tope came with an army to help the queen, but the British pushed him back near the Betwa river. On 3 April 1858 the British stormed the city and, after fierce fighting, captured most of it.

The same night the queen, taking her adopted son Damodar Rao with her, rode out of the fort with a few trusted companions and, travelling continuously, reached **Kalpi**, where Tatya Tope and other rebel leaders had gathered.

## Gwalior and the Final Battle

In May 1858 Kalpi too passed into British hands. Then the queen, Tatya Tope and Rao Sahib took a bold step — they marched towards **Gwalior**. The army of Gwalior sided with the rebels, and around 1 June 1858 the fort of Gwalior came into their hands.

Hugh Rose at once marched on Gwalior. In the battle near **Kota-ki-Serai**, Rani Lakshmibai, leading the cavalry, was gravely wounded and was martyred on **18 June 1858**. The details of the exact circumstances of her death differ in different sources. Within a few days Gwalior again passed into British hands.

## Legacy

Rani Lakshmibai became the most popular symbol of courage and self-respect in the Indian freedom struggle. The line from Subhadra Kumari Chauhan's poem "Jhansi Ki Rani" — "Khoob ladi mardani woh toh Jhansi wali rani thi" (She fought valiantly like a man, that queen of Jhansi) — has been memorised by Indian children for generations. In the 1940s the women's unit of Subhas Chandra Bose's Indian National Army was also named the "Rani of Jhansi Regiment".
