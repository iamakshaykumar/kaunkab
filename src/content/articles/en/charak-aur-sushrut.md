## The Foundation of Ayurveda

The medical system of ancient India is known as **Ayurveda** ("the knowledge of life"). Its oldest surviving and authoritative texts are the **Charaka Samhita** and the **Sushruta Samhita**. Both are in Sanskrit, and the present form of both took shape not at one time but over many centuries through revisions, additions and deletions. It is therefore difficult to give their exact date; scholars link their early forms to about the last centuries BCE.

## The Charaka Samhita

According to tradition, the teachings of the teacher **Atreya Punarvasu** were written down in one text by his disciple **Agnivesha**. Later **Charaka** revised it, and still later the Kashmiri scholar **Dridhabala** completed its unfinished parts. Today's manuscripts are based on Dridhabala's edition. Some scholars hold that "Charaka" may not be the name of one person but of a tradition of wandering physicians. A Buddhist tradition describes Charaka as the court physician of the Kushana king Kanishka, but this is not proven.

The Charaka Samhita has **8 sthanas** (sections) and **120 chapters**. Its main points:
- Regarding the balance of the three **doshas** of the body — vata, pitta and kapha — as the basis of health.
- Emphasis on the **diagnosis** (identification) of disease, and treatment that takes into account the patient's constitution, age, season and diet.
- Daily routine, seasonal regimen and balanced food for **prevention** of illness.
- Rules for the conduct and ethics of the physician; the four pillars of treatment — physician, medicine, attendant and patient.

## The Sushruta Samhita

The Sushruta Samhita presents itself as the work of **Sushruta**, who describes the teaching he received from **Divodasa Dhanvantari**, king and physician of Kashi (Varanasi). It has a total of **186 chapters** in six parts; the last part, the **Uttaratantra**, is believed to have been added later. Its oldest known manuscript is in Nepal and dates from 878 CE.

## Surgery and Training

The Sushruta Samhita gained special fame for its accounts of **surgery**:
- It describes many kinds of **yantras** (blunt instruments) and **shastras** (sharp instruments); the traditional count gives 101 yantras and 20 shastras.
- Students were first made to practise cutting, stitching and piercing on gourds, cucumbers, water-filled leather bags and dead animals.
- The method of studying a dead body (dissection) to understand anatomy is also described.
- The description of rebuilding a cut-off nose with skin from the cheek (**nasika-sandhana** or rhinoplasty) is regarded as among the oldest written accounts of this technique.
- The Uttaratantra also mentions a surgical method for the treatment of cataract.

## Influence and Legacy

These texts travelled beyond the borders of India. The Sushruta Samhita was translated into Arabic in Baghdad in the early eighth century. The **Bower Manuscript** found at Kucha in Central Asia also contains Ayurvedic prescriptions. Later commentators — such as Chakrapanidatta on the Charaka Samhita and Dalhana on the Sushruta Samhita — kept the tradition of these texts alive.

Modern historians also remind us that these texts should be understood in the context of their own time, not judged by the standards of today's medical science. Even so, their emphasis on observation, practice, ethics and prevention makes them important texts in the history of world medicine.
