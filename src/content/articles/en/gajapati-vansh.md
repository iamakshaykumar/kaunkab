## From the Eastern Gangas to the Gajapatis

In the twelfth and thirteenth centuries Odisha was ruled by the **Eastern Ganga dynasty**, which built the Jagannath temple at Puri and the Sun Temple at Konark. By the fifteenth century this dynasty had become weak. In **1434**, when the last Ganga king Bhanudeva IV was on a campaign in the south, his minister and commander **Kapilendra Deva** took power into his own hands. He was crowned on 29 June 1435. His dynasty was called **Suryavamsi**, and because of the kings' title **"Gajapati"** (lord of elephants) it became famous as the Gajapati dynasty. The capital was **Cuttack**.

## Kapilendra Deva: From the Ganga to the Kaveri (1434–1467)

Kapilendra Deva is counted among the most glorious rulers of Odisha. In the north he faced the sultans of Bengal and the armies of Jaunpur, and in the south he fought the Reddi kingdom of Andhra (Rajahmundry, Kondavidu), the Bahmani Sultanate and Vijayanagara. Under his son **Hambira Deva** the Odia armies reached very far south. By about 1464 his influence is held to have extended from near the Ganga to the Kaveri (Tiruchirappalli). After his conquests he took the long title **"Gajapati Gaudeshwara Navakoti Karnata Kalavargeshwara"**.

Under his rule **Odia** was promoted as a language of administration. The Odia *Mahabharata* of the poet **Sarala Dasa** of this period is considered the cornerstone of Odia literature.

## Purushottama Deva and the Kanchi-Kaveri Story (1467–1497)

Kapilendra chose his younger son **Purushottama Deva** as successor instead of the elder son Hambira. This led to a civil war; Hambira took the help of the Bahmani sultan and for some years the southern territories slipped out of hand. Later Purushottama won back a large part of the Godavari-Krishna region.

The most famous story of Purushottama in Odisha's folk memory is **"Kanchi-Kaveri"**. According to the tale, the king of Kanchi refused to marry his daughter **Padmavati** to him because he sweeps the chariots in the Rath Yatra; then Jagannath and Balabhadra themselves went with his army as horsemen, and on the way gave their ring to the milkmaid **Manika** to pay for the curd. This is a devotional tale, not a historical account, but it appears again and again in Odia literature and pattachitra art. Purushottama himself was a scholar of Sanskrit and also had construction work done in the Jagannath temple complex.

## Prataparudra Deva: Devotion and Defeat (1497–1540)

In the time of **Prataparudra Deva** the Gajapati kingdom came under pressure on several fronts. The army of the Husain Shahi sultan of Bengal reached Puri; according to tradition the priests hid the images of Jagannath on an island in Chilika Lake. In the south **Krishnadevaraya** of Vijayanagara, after a long siege, won **Udayagiri** in 1514 and then **Kondavidu**. Finally a treaty was made and Prataparudra's daughter was married to Krishnadevaraya; the region south of the Krishna river passed to Vijayanagara. Later Quli Qutb Shah of Golconda also seized southern territories.

Culturally this period was very rich. **Chaitanya Mahaprabhu** lived in Puri from about 1510 and Prataparudra became his devotee. The **Panchasakha** poets of Odia, Balarama Dasa, Jagannatha Dasa, Achyutananda, Yashovanta and Ananta, were active at this time; Jagannatha Dasa's Odia *Bhagavata* is still read in every home.

## The End and the Legacy

After Prataparudra's death (about 1538–1540), his two young sons could rule only for a short time. His minister **Govinda Vidyadhara** removed them and took power in **1541**, and the Bhoi dynasty began. With this the rule of the Suryavamsi Gajapatis ended, and a few decades later, in 1568, Odisha passed under the Karrani sultans of Bengal.

The legacy of the Gajapati dynasty is alive even today: the royal family of Puri is traditionally called "Gajapati", many services of the Jagannath temple and the singing of the Gita Govinda are traditions of that period, and Odia language and literature received a great foundation in this age.
