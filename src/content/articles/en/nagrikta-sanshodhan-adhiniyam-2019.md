## Background

India's basic law on citizenship is the **Citizenship Act, 1955**. Under it, foreigners who come to India without valid documents are considered "illegal migrants" and are normally not eligible for citizenship. Many Hindu, Sikh and other minority families who had come to India from Pakistan, Bangladesh and Afghanistan because of religious persecution had been living without citizenship for years. In 2015–16 the government had exempted such people from the passport and foreigners laws.

In **2016** a Citizenship Amendment Bill was first brought to Parliament and sent to a joint parliamentary committee. In January 2019 it was passed by the Lok Sabha, but since it could not go to the Rajya Sabha, it lapsed when the 16th Lok Sabha was dissolved. After the 2019 general election it was introduced again.

## Becoming Law and Main Provisions

Home Minister **Amit Shah** introduced the new bill in December 2019. The Lok Sabha passed it on **10 December 2019** by 311–80 votes and the Rajya Sabha on **11 December** by 125–105 votes. On **12 December 2019** it received the assent of President **Ram Nath Kovind**, and the law was deemed to be in force from **10 January 2020**. Its main provisions were:

- People of the **Hindu, Sikh, Buddhist, Jain, Parsi and Christian** communities from **Afghanistan, Bangladesh and Pakistan** who came to India on or before **31 December 2014** would not be treated as "illegal migrants".
- For these people, the period of residence in India required to obtain citizenship by naturalisation was **reduced from 11 years to 5 years**.
- The tribal areas of Assam, Meghalaya, Mizoram and Tripura under the **Sixth Schedule** of the Constitution, and the states with an **Inner Line Permit**, were kept outside the law.

This law does not concern taking away or granting the citizenship of any Indian citizen; it applies only to migrants of the specified communities from these three countries.

## The Arguments of the Government and the Critics

**According to the government**, these three countries are constitutionally or in practice Muslim-majority, where religious minorities have faced persecution; therefore the law is a **humanitarian step** to give them refuge and fulfils assurances given at the time of India's Partition. The government said that Muslims are not religious minorities in these countries, and that any foreigner, of any religion, can apply for citizenship through the normal process.

**Critics said** that linking citizenship to religion for the first time went against **Article 14** of the Constitution (equality before the law) and the principle of secularism. They asked why persecuted groups such as the Ahmadiyya of Pakistan, the Hazara of Afghanistan, the Tamils of Sri Lanka or the Rohingya of Myanmar had been left out. The biggest fear expressed was that if a **National Register of Citizens (NRC)** were implemented across the country, non-Muslims unable to produce documents could get relief through the CAA, but Muslims could not. The government said that no decision had been taken on a nationwide NRC and that the CAA had no connection with the NRC.

## Nationwide Protests

As soon as the bill was passed, intense protests broke out in **Assam** and other northeastern states, where the main concern was a rise in the number of outside migrants and local identity; a curfew was imposed in Guwahati and several people died. On **15 December 2019**, after police action at **Jamia Millia Islamia** in Delhi, the protests spread to many universities and cities across the country. At **Shaheen Bagh** in Delhi a sit-in led by women went on for about three months and became a symbol of the movement. In several places, including Uttar Pradesh, many people lost their lives in violence. Many rallies were also held in support of the law.

In **February 2020**, after tension between supporters and opponents of the CAA in north-east Delhi, communal riots broke out in which more than 50 people were killed. In March 2020 most sit-ins ended because of the COVID-19 pandemic and the lockdown. The Kerala Assembly passed a resolution against the law and some other states also voiced opposition, while the Centre said that citizenship is a subject in the Union List and states cannot refuse to implement it.

## Rules, the Court and Significance

Framing the rules took more than four years after the law was made. The Ministry of Home Affairs notified the **Citizenship (Amendment) Rules, 2024** on **11 March 2024**, under which applications began to be accepted online. On **15 May 2024** the first applicants were handed citizenship certificates.

**More than 200 petitions** challenging the constitutionality of the law were filed in the Supreme Court. On **19 March 2024** the Court refused to impose an interim stay on the rules and sought the Centre's response. In 2026 the Court fixed a schedule for the final hearing of these petitions; at the time of writing this article, no information on a final verdict on the validity of the law was available.

The CAA is one of the most debated laws of independent India. For its supporters it fulfils India's responsibility towards the persecuted minorities of neighbouring countries, while for its critics it raises serious constitutional questions about the relationship between citizenship and religion. This debate gave rise to a wide public discussion in the country on citizenship, refugee policy and constitutional values.
