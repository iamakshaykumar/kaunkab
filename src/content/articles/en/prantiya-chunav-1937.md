## The Government of India Act 1935

After the Round Table Conferences and the Simon Commission, the British Parliament passed the **Government of India Act 1935**, which received royal assent on **2 August 1935**. It was the longest constitutional law made by the British for India. Its main points:

- **Dyarchy** in the provinces was ended and **provincial autonomy** was given — now provincial subjects were to be in the hands of elected ministers. This part came into force from **1 April 1937**.
- Governors kept 'special responsibilities' and the powers to take over the whole administration in an emergency.
- At the centre a **federation** of the British provinces and the princely states was to be formed, but it never came into force because the princely states did not join.
- The number of voters rose to a little over thirty million, but the franchise was still bound by conditions of property and education.

The Congress sharply criticised this Act, but still decided to contest the elections so that it could show its strength among the people.

## The 1937 Elections

Voting for the assemblies of eleven provinces was held in **January–February 1937**. Out of about 1,585 seats in all, the **Congress** won **706**. It got a full majority in Madras, the United Provinces, Bihar, the Central Provinces and Orissa, and became the largest party in Bombay. The **Muslim League** got only 111 seats and its performance on the Muslim reserved seats was also weak. In Punjab the **Unionist Party** of **Sikandar Hayat Khan** and in Bengal the **Krishak Praja Party** of **A. K. Fazlul Huq** formed coalition governments.

## The Congress Ministries

The Congress first wanted an assurance that the governors would not use their special powers in the day-to-day work of the ministers. After a statement by the Viceroy **Lord Linlithgow**, the Congress agreed in **July 1937** to form governments. Ministries were formed in six provinces — under **C. Rajagopalachari** in Madras, **B. G. Kher** in Bombay, **Govind Ballabh Pant** in the United Provinces, **Sri Krishna Sinha** in Bihar, and so on. Later a government under **Dr. Khan Sahib** was formed in the North-West Frontier Province and in 1938 one under **Gopinath Bordoloi** in Assam, bringing the number to **eight**.

These governments worked on the release of political prisoners, restoration of civil liberties, relief in peasants' debts and rents, conditions of workers, primary education, and prohibition in some provinces. On the other hand, because of limited powers and weak finances they could not carry out very deep reforms, and with coming to power complaints of office-seeking also arose within the party.

## Growing Distance from the League

After the election, the talk of a joint Congress-League government in the United Provinces came to nothing, which worsened the relations between the two. The Muslim League made allegations of discrimination against Muslims in the Congress provinces and in 1938 issued reports such as the **Pirpur Report**; the Congress denied these charges and offered an inquiry. Historians believe that it was in this period that Jinnah rapidly organised the League as the representative body of Muslims.

## The Resignation (1939)

When the Second World War began in September 1939, Viceroy Linlithgow declared India a party to the war without consulting Indian representatives. In protest the Congress ministries resigned in **October–November 1939** and governor's rule was imposed in many provinces. Jinnah appealed to Muslims to observe **22 December 1939** as a **'Day of Deliverance'**.

## Significance

This experiment of twenty-eight months showed that Indian leaders could run an administration, and the Congress's strength of popular support also came out. At the same time, in this period the gulf between the Congress and the Muslim League deepened, the effect of which was seen in the Lahore Resolution of 1940 and later in the politics of Partition. A large part of the 1935 Act also became the basis of the framework of the Indian Constitution after independence.
