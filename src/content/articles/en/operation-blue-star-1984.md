## Background: Rising Tension in Punjab

In the 1970s and 1980s many demands were being raised in Punjab's politics. In **1973** the Akali Dal passed the **Anandpur Sahib Resolution**, which included greater autonomy for the states and a number of religious, economic and territorial demands. The central government saw it as having a separatist direction, while Akali leaders said it was a demand for rights within the federal framework. In **1982** the Akali Dal launched the **Dharam Yudh Morcha** to press these demands.

In the same period the influence of **Jarnail Singh Bhindranwale**, head of the Damdami Taksal, grew rapidly. Incidents of violence increased in Punjab, including the killings of many innocent people and officials. Bhindranwale began living in the Golden Temple complex from **1982** and moved into the **Akal Takht** in **December 1983**. According to the government, the complex had been fortified with weapons. Former Major General **Shabeg Singh** helped prepare the defences there. When talks failed to produce a solution, the government decided to send in the army.

## Operation Blue Star (1–10 June 1984)

The operation began on **1 June 1984**. On **3 June** a curfew was imposed across Punjab and communications and travel were halted. That day was the martyrdom day of **Guru Arjan Dev**, so a large number of pilgrims were present in the complex. The Army Chief was **General A. S. Vaidya**; **Lt Gen K. Sundarji** of Western Command planned the operation, and command of the ground action was in the hands of **Maj Gen K. S. Brar**.

On the night of **5 June** the army entered the complex. There was fierce resistance from inside and the fighting was very intense. To capture the Akal Takht the army finally used tanks. On **6 June** Bhindranwale, Shabeg Singh and **Amrik Singh** were killed, and by the morning of **7 June** the army controlled the complex. The action ended by about **10 June**. The Akal Takht was heavily damaged and the **Sikh Reference Library**, which held many rare manuscripts, was destroyed by fire.

## Figures and Reactions

The number of casualties is still disputed. According to the government **White Paper** of **July 1984**, **83 soldiers** were killed and **249** wounded, and **493** "civilians and militants" were killed. Estimates by independent journalists, Sikh organisations and human rights groups are far higher, running into thousands. Critics said that carrying out the operation amid crowds of pilgrims on a holy day cost ordinary people their lives; the government's position was that, given the militants' occupation and their stockpile of weapons, no other option was left.

The operation caused deep anger among a large part of the Sikh community. At some places Sikh soldiers mutinied. The writer **Khushwant Singh** returned his Padma Bhushan. After this, **Operation Woodrose** was carried out in Punjab's villages. Militancy in Punjab continued for roughly the next decade. In **1986** General Vaidya was assassinated by militants in Pune.

## The Assassination of Indira Gandhi (31 October 1984)

On the morning of **31 October 1984**, in the garden of the Prime Minister's residence in New Delhi, two of her Sikh bodyguards, **Beant Singh** and **Satwant Singh**, opened fire on **Indira Gandhi**. She was taken to hospital, where she died. Beant Singh was killed on the spot. The killing was described as revenge for Operation Blue Star. The **Thakkar Commission** was set up to investigate. After a trial, Satwant Singh and **Kehar Singh**, who was convicted of conspiracy, were hanged on **6 January 1989**. The same evening **Rajiv Gandhi** was sworn in as Prime Minister.

## The Anti-Sikh Riots and the Long Road to Justice

After the assassination, from **31 October to 3 November 1984**, terrible violence against Sikhs took place in Delhi and many other parts of the country. Mobs burned homes, gurdwaras and shops and burned people alive. According to the government's estimate, about **3,350** people were killed nationwide, about **2,800** of them in Delhi; the **Ahuja Committee** put the Delhi figure at **2,733**. Other estimates are much higher.

Several commissions and committees were set up to investigate — the **Marwah Commission** (1984), the **Ranganath Misra Commission** (1985), the Kapur-Mittal Committee, the Jain-Banerjee Committee and others. The report of the **Nanavati Commission**, set up in **2000**, was tabled in Parliament in **2005**. The commission found that local Congress leaders and workers had incited or helped the mobs and that the Delhi Police had been passive, and it said the roles of **Jagdish Tytler** and **Sajjan Kumar** needed further investigation; it cleared Rajiv Gandhi and the senior leadership of the charge of organising the riots. On **11 August 2005** Prime Minister **Manmohan Singh** apologised in the Lok Sabha.

In **2015** the central government set up a Special Investigation Team (SIT). In **December 2018** the Delhi High Court sentenced former MP **Sajjan Kumar** to life imprisonment, and on **25 February 2025** a Delhi court also sentenced him to life imprisonment in the Saraswati Vihar case. Victims' families also describe these verdicts, which came decades later, as incomplete justice.

## Legacy

These events of 1984 are counted among the most painful chapters of independent India. They raised questions about military action at religious sites, security arrangements, the state's responsibility for communal violence and delays in justice. Despite political efforts such as the Rajiv-Longowal Accord of **1985** and the return of peace to Punjab in later years, the memories of June and November 1984 remain a matter of deep feeling for the Sikh community and for the whole country.
