## Rajendra I: Going Beyond His Father

**Rajendra I** was the son of the famous Chola emperor **Rajaraja I**. From 1012 CE he was co-ruler with his father and became emperor in **1014 CE**. His rule lasted until **1044 CE**. From his father he received a strong kingdom and a powerful army, which he took far beyond the borders of south India.

Information about his campaigns comes mainly from Chola **inscriptions** and grant records such as the **Tiruvalangadu copper plates**. These are court sources, so the praise of the king in them may be exaggerated.

## The Complete Conquest of Sri Lanka

Rajaraja I had taken northern Sri Lanka. In about **1017–1018 CE** Rajendra marched over the whole island and took the Sinhalese king **Mahinda V** prisoner and brought him to India, where he died in 1029 CE. This victory strengthened Chola influence over the southern seas.

## The Campaign to the Ganga (c. 1019–1021)

The most famous campaign of Rajendra's reign was northward to the **Ganga**. The Chola army reached Bengal by the eastern coast through Kalinga (today's Odisha). According to the inscriptions it defeated several kings on the way:
- the Somavamshi king **Indraratha** in the Kalinga region,
- **Dharmapala** of Dandabhukti,
- **Govindachandra** of the **Chandra dynasty** of Bengal,
- and the Pala king **Mahipala**.

Rajendra himself came as far as the area of the Godavari, while the army that went to the Ganga was led by a commander — according to some studies his younger brother Araiyan Rajarajan. This campaign was more one of **prestige and a show of power** than of permanent conquest; the Cholas did not rule Bengal. According to the Tiruvalangadu copper plates, the defeated kings were made to carry golden pitchers filled with **Ganga water** to the Chola capital.

## Gangaikonda Cholapuram: The Capital of Victory

In memory of this victory Rajendra took the title **"Gangaikonda Chola"** (the Chola who conquered the Ganga) and founded a new capital named **Gangaikonda Cholapuram**. Here he built a grand Shiva temple, in the tradition of his father's Brihadisvara temple at Thanjavur. Near the city a huge reservoir named **Chola Gangam** was built, into which Ganga water was mixed. The temple of Gangaikonda Cholapuram is today part of UNESCO's "Great Living Chola Temples" World Heritage Site.

## The Naval Raid on Srivijaya (1025)

At that time the **Srivijaya** kingdom of Sumatra and the Malay peninsula controlled the sea trade route between India and China — especially the Strait of Malacca. In **1025 CE** the Chola navy crossed the sea and attacked several ports of Srivijaya.

A Tamil inscription of the Chola period has a list of the places conquered, which includes **Srivijaya**, **Pannai**, **Malaiyur**, **Ilangasokam** (Langkasuka), **Talaittakkolam**, **Nakkavaram** (Nicobar) and **Kadaram** (today's Kedah, Malaysia). The modern identification of these names is not fully certain. According to the inscription the king of Kadaram, **Sangrama Vijayottungavarman**, was taken prisoner.

Historians differ on the reasons for this campaign. Some regard it as an attempt to establish Chola supremacy over sea trade routes, some as a plundering expedition, and some link it to the internal politics of South-East Asia. The Cholas did not rule there directly, but the raid dealt a deep blow to Srivijaya's power.

## Significance

Rajendra's campaigns show that in the eleventh century a south Indian kingdom was able to send armies as far as north India and to run naval expeditions across the sea. In his time the Chola kingdom became a major power in Indian Ocean trade, and the influence of Tamil merchant guilds spread to South-East Asia. He died in 1044 CE.
