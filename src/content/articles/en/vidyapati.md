## The Poet of Mithila

**Vidyapati** was born around **1352** in the Mithila region of North Bihar, in the village of **Bisfi** in today's Madhubani district, and his death is placed around **1448**. (Some sources give his time as slightly later, about 1374–1460.) He was everything — poet, playwright, musician, scholar and court priest. In Mithila he is respectfully called the "**Maithili Kavi Kokil**" (the cuckoo-poet of Mithila).

## The Court of the Oinwar Kings

Vidyapati served at the courts of seven kings and two queens of the **Oinwar** dynasty of Mithila. His early patron was **Kirtisimha**, in whose praise he wrote the **Kirtilata**. Later he was at the courts of **Devasimha** and especially **Shivasimha**, in whose time his poetry reached its peak.

Around **1406**, during a battle with a Muslim army, King Shivasimha **disappeared**. After this Vidyapati and the court took refuge at **Rajbanauli** (now in Nepal), and Shivasimha's chief queen **Lakhima Devi** ruled for about 12 years. The names of Shivasimha and Lakhima Devi appear in many of Vidyapati's verses.

## Three Languages, Many Works

Vidyapati wrote in three languages — **Sanskrit**, **Avahattha** (a later form of Apabhramsha) and **Maithili**. His major works are:

- **Kirtilata** — a poem in Avahattha in praise of Kirtisimha.
- **Purushaparikshā** — a book of stories on ethics and politics.
- **Likhanavali** — on the art of letter-writing.
- **Bhuparikrama** — on pilgrimage sites and geography.
- **Gorakshavijaya** — a musical play.
- Religious works such as the **Shaivasarvasvasara** (on the worship of Shiva) and the **Durgabhaktitarangini** (on the method of worshipping Durga).

These works show that Vidyapati was not only a poet but also a scholar knowledgeable in dharmashastra, ethics and statecraft. As a court priest and courtier he wrote on practical subjects from letter-writing to methods of worship, which gives a glimpse of the society and administration of Mithila at that time.

But his greatest identity rests on his Maithili **songs**, which were later compiled under the name **Padavali**.

## Padavali: Love and Devotion

Vidyapati wrote about **500** Maithili love songs, with tender portrayals of the love, separation and union of **Radha and Krishna**. Most of these were composed between about **1380 and 1406**. In later years he turned to more devotional compositions and wrote verses on **Shiva**, **Durga**, **Vishnu** and **Ganga**.

Vidyapati himself was a devotee of **Shiva**, yet he wrote beautiful songs on Radha and Krishna — this shows the religious openness of that time. Before him, serious literature was written in Sanskrit, but he gave the people's language, Maithili, the status of literature through songs. For this reason his role in the development of the languages of eastern India is sometimes compared to that of Dante in Italy and Chaucer in England.

## Influence in Bengal

Vidyapati's songs soon became popular in **Bengal**. It is said that **Chaitanya Mahaprabhu** loved to hear his verses. The Vaishnava kirtan singers of Bengal adopted them, and in the singing their language kept changing. From this imitation and mixing an artificial literary language called **Brajabuli** arose — a blend of Maithili and Bengali — and Vaishnava verses continued to be written in it until the 19th century. **Rabindranath Tagore's** *Bhanusimha Thakurer Padavali* belongs to this tradition.

## Legends and Memory

Many **folk tales** are told about Vidyapati. The most famous is that Lord Shiva himself lived in his house as a servant named **Ugna**. Another tale says that at the end of his life the river Ganga herself came to him. These are stories of the devotional tradition, not history.

In Mithila, Vidyapati is still known in every home; his songs are sung at weddings and festivals. The Government of India issued postage stamps on him in **1965** and **2016**, and in **December 2018** Darbhanga airport was named "**Kavi Kokil Vidyapati Airport**".
