## Background: The Clash over the Cabinet Mission

In **1946** the British government's **Cabinet Mission** put forward a plan for a loose union in which provinces were to be formed into groups. The Muslim League accepted it in June, but on **10 July** **Jawaharlal Nehru** said that in the Constituent Assembly the Congress was not bound by the grouping arrangement. The League regarded this as backing away from the plan.

On **29 July 1946**, in Bombay, the League's council withdrew its acceptance of the Cabinet Mission Plan and decided on **"Direct Action"** to press the demand for Pakistan. **16 August** was declared a day of strikes and meetings across the country. **Muhammad Ali Jinnah** said at the time that there would now be "either a divided India or a destroyed India". The League said it would be a day of peaceful protest, while its opponents called this call and the speeches linked to it inflammatory.

## 16 August: Calcutta

At that time Bengal had a Muslim League government and its Premier was **Huseyn Shaheed Suhrawardy**. At his request, Governor **Sir Frederick Burrows** declared 16 August a public holiday. In the afternoon a large League meeting was held near the **Ochterlony Monument** (today the **Shaheed Minar**), with estimates of the crowd ranging from 30,000 to 5 lakh.

Clashes had already begun in many parts of the city in the morning, and after the meeting the violence spread rapidly. For the next three days arson, looting and killing went on in Calcutta. People of both communities were killed; after the initial attacks, retaliatory violence also took place on a large scale. In **72 hours** more than **4,000** people were killed and about **one lakh** were made homeless; some estimates put the death toll as high as 10,000. After the army was called in, the situation came under control around **22 August**. It came to be called the **"Great Calcutta Killings"**.

## Differing Views on Responsibility

There were differences over responsibility for this violence then, and there still are today.

- The **Congress** mainly blamed the League and the Suhrawardy government — questions were raised about the declaration of the holiday and the slow action of the police.
- Supporters of the **Muslim League** held the Congress and Hindu organisations responsible.
- The dominant view among **British officials** was that troublemakers of both communities were responsible.
- Scholars such as the historian **Joya Chatterji** regard Suhrawardy's role as the major one, but also hold Hindu leaders culpable.

The government administration, the police and the army were also criticised for their delay — for not taking firm steps before the situation got out of hand.

## Noakhali, Bihar and Garhmukteshwar

In reaction to the Calcutta violence, riots broke out in many places afterwards. In the **Noakhali** and **Tippera** districts of East Bengal, attacks on Hindus began on **10 October 1946**, the day of Kojagari Lakshmi Puja. There were killings, looting, arson, abductions and **forced conversions**, and thousands of people were displaced. The official estimate was about 200 deaths, while other estimates are much higher; the figures from different sources vary greatly.

In reaction to this, fierce violence against Muslims took place in **Bihar** between **30 October and 7 November 1946**. The official figure up to 3 November was 445 deaths, but some independent estimates put it at around 8,000. In **November 1946** violence also broke out during a fair at **Garhmukteshwar** in the United Provinces, in which an estimated 1,000 to 2,000 people were killed.

## Gandhi's Peace Mission

Distressed by the violence, **Gandhiji** set out from Calcutta for Noakhali on **6 November 1946**. Walking barefoot from village to village, he appealed to both communities for reconciliation, assured refugees that they could return home, and organised relief work through a "one village, one worker" plan. In about seven weeks he walked **116 miles** and visited **47 villages**. On **2 March 1947** he left for Bihar, where he appealed to Hindus to atone for the violence committed against Muslims.

## Impact and Legacy

Direct Action Day and the violence that followed changed the atmosphere of Indian politics. Distrust between the two communities deepened, and many leaders and British officials began to feel that a transfer of power to a united India was now difficult. Only a few months later, in **February 1947**, Britain announced a deadline for leaving, and in **June 1947** the plan for partition came out. The violence of 1946 proved to be a forewarning of the far greater tragedy at the time of Partition in 1947. The memory of these events is still remembered in different ways in India, Pakistan and Bangladesh.
