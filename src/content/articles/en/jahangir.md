## Prince Salim

Jahangir was born on **31 August 1569** at Fatehpur Sikri to Akbar and Maryam-uz-Zamani. He was named Salim. In Akbar's last years relations between father and son were tense — around 1600 Salim had set up an almost independent court at Allahabad, and the murder of Abu'l Fazl in 1602 is also believed to have been at his instigation. In the end they were reconciled and, after Akbar's death, on **3 November 1605** Salim ascended the throne under the name 'Nur-ud-din Muhammad Jahangir' (Conqueror of the World).

## Khusrau's Rebellion

As soon as he took the throne, a challenge came from within the family. A faction at court wanted Jahangir's eldest son **Khusrau** to be made Akbar's successor. In **April 1606** Khusrau left Agra on the pretext of visiting Akbar's tomb and moved towards Punjab. He was defeated near Bhairowal and was captured while crossing the Chenab. His supporters were given harsh punishments. In this episode the fifth Sikh Guru, **Arjan Dev**, was accused of helping Khusrau and was martyred in 1606. Khusrau was later blinded and kept imprisoned; he died in the Deccan in **1622**, for which Prince Khurram (Shah Jahan) is generally held responsible.

## The Chain of Justice and the Twelve Edicts

In the Tuzuk Jahangir writes that after taking the throne he had a golden chain made so that anyone who did not get justice from the officials could shake it and reach the emperor directly. According to popular accounts, one end was tied to the Shah Burj of the Agra Fort and the other to a stone pillar planted on the bank of the Yamuna. There is no solid proof of how much the chain was actually used — it is also regarded as a symbol for creating the image of a just ruler.

He also issued **twelve edicts**, which included orders such as a ban on the making and sale of liquor and intoxicants, the abolition of certain illegal taxes, and the appointment of hospitals and physicians in big cities.

## The Tuzuk, Painting and Nature

In the tradition of Babur, Jahangir wrote his autobiography in Persian, the **Tuzuk-i-Jahangiri** (Jahangirnama). Alongside the court and campaigns, it has fine descriptions of trees and plants, animals and birds, and the weather. He was also a curious experimenter — the Tuzuk records experiments such as examining the organs of animals and comparing the air of two cities.

Under his patronage Mughal painting reached its peak. **Ustad Mansur** made accurate pictures of creatures such as falcons, turkeys and the zebra, while **Abu'l Hasan** and **Bichitr** created the emperor's spiritual-political image in allegorical paintings. Jahangir claimed that he could recognise the hands of different painters in a single picture.

## Politics and War

- **Mewar:** After a long struggle, in 1615 Rana Amar Singh made a treaty with Prince Khurram; in return the Rana of Mewar was exempted from attending the court in person.
- **Kangra:** After a long siege, the fort of Kangra came into Mughal hands in 1620.
- **Kandahar:** In 1622 Shah Abbas of Iran seized Kandahar.
- **Deccan:** **Malik Ambar** of Ahmednagar repeatedly halted the Mughal advance with guerrilla warfare.
- **The English:** In 1615–19 the English ambassador Sir Thomas Roe stayed at his court.

## Nur Jahan, Rebellion and the End

In 1611 Jahangir married Mehr-un-Nisa, who came to be called **Nur Jahan**. Her father Itimad-ud-Daula and brother Asaf Khan rose to high posts, and with Jahangir's failing health Nur Jahan's influence grew. In the clash over succession Khurram rebelled from 1622, and in 1626 the general **Mahabat Khan** held the emperor captive for a time on the bank of the Jhelum; through Nur Jahan's stratagem the crisis passed in about a hundred days. While returning from Kashmir to Lahore, Jahangir died near Bhimber on **28 October 1627**. He was buried at Shahdara near Lahore.
