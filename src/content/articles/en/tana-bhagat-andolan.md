## The Background in Chhotanagpur

At the start of the twentieth century, the Adivasis of **Chhotanagpur** (today's **Jharkhand**) were in deep crisis. Under the new land system that came with British rule, zamindars, contractors and moneylenders were seizing Adivasi land. Rents kept rising — the **Oraon** (Kurukh) farmers of western Chhotanagpur often had to pay higher rent than the Munda farmers in the east. On top of this came burdens such as **forced labour (begar)**, being made to beat the forest for hunts, and carrying police baggage.

Earlier, in **1899–1900**, **Birsa Munda's** 'Ulgulan' had erupted against these very grievances. About a decade and a half later a new movement arose among the Oraons, one that carried religion, society and politics together.

## Jatra Bhagat's Message

**Jatra Oraon** — known as **Jatra Bhagat** or **Jatra Tana Bhagat** — was born in the village of **Chingri Nawatoli** in Ranchi district (today's **Gumla** district). In **April 1914**, at about 25 years of age, he announced that **Dharmesh**, the supreme god of the Oraons, had sent him a message. **Turia Bhagat** was also among the early leaders of this movement alongside him.

Jatra called for "purifying" the Oraon religion. His main teachings were:

- giving up belief in **ghosts, witch-doctors and exorcism**;
- ending **animal sacrifice**;
- giving up **liquor and meat** and adopting vegetarianism, restraint and simplicity;
- challenging the authority of the village's traditional **pahan** (priest) and **mahto** (headman).

It is said that the word 'Tana' is linked to the meaning "to pull" in the Kurukh language — the devotees spoke of "pulling" evil forces out through prayer. The followers came to be called **Bhagats** (devotees), and the movement became famous as the **Tana Bhagat movement**.

## From Religion to No-Rent

This religious reform soon turned into an economic and political revolt. Jatra said that land was a **gift of God** and that no outsider had any right over it. He told his followers that they should:

- **not pay rent** to the zamindars;
- **not plough** the zamindars' fields;
- **not do coolie labour or begar** for non-Adivasis or the government.

Secret meetings were held at night, at which songs and mantras were sung. The First World War was going on at the time, and a rumour also spread in the villages that a "German Baba" would come and defeat the British. About **26,000** people are estimated to have gathered around Jatra. The movement spread among the Oraons of Ranchi, **Palamau** and **Hazaribagh**, and some Mundas and Kharias also joined it.

Panic spread among the zamindars and the government. According to **Banglapedia**, on **23 April 1914** Jatra and his leading disciples were arrested on charges of inciting the Adivasis to refuse to work for the zamindars and the government and of endangering the peace (some sources give the year of the arrest as 1915). After a trial they were jailed. After his release on **2 June 1915**, Jatra gave up the leadership of the movement; according to some sources he died in **1916**.

## New Links in the Leadership

The movement did not stop after Jatra. A woman named **Litho Oraon** preached in the same way as he had; she too was sent to jail. In **November 1915** **Mangor Oraon** took over the leadership, but the same happened to him. By **1916** the movement had reached Oraon labourers who had gone to work in the tea gardens of **Jalpaiguri** in Bengal. In **1919** **Sibu Oraon** and **Maya Oraon** breathed new life into it.

## Gandhi's Soldiers

From **1921** the movement took a new turn. During the **Non-Cooperation Movement** the Tana Bhagats became followers of **Mahatma Gandhi**. Their rules now included carrying the **Congress flag**, wearing **khadi** and taking vows in Gandhi's name. Many stories spread in the villages about Gandhi, his **charkha** and **swaraj** — Gandhi was seen as a saint who would drive out the British without guns and bring about a "dharma raj".

The Tana Bhagats adopted **non-violence** and kept up their refusal to pay rent. They paid a heavy price for this: because of rent arrears, much of their land was **auctioned** on court orders. According to historians, after joining the Congress the radical character of the movement softened somewhat and it became part of the mainstream of the national movement.

## After Independence and Legacy

After independence the Bihar government recognised the sacrifice of the Tana Bhagats. The **Ranchi District Tana Bhagat Raiyats' Agricultural Lands Restoration Act, 1947** received assent on **30 December 1947**. Under it, provision was made to return the land of those Tana Bhagat farmers whose land had been auctioned because they had not paid rent on account of the freedom movement. Later the law was amended and the deadline for applications was also extended.

Even today the Tana Bhagat community lives in the Gumla, Lohardaga, Latehar, Chatra and Ranchi districts of Jharkhand. Their identity is **white clothes**, the Gandhi cap, vegetarianism and non-violence; many families still worship the **tricolour and the charkha**. In **2014**, when the movement completed a hundred years, a centenary celebration was held, and a statue of Jatra Bhagat also stands in Ranchi district. The Tana Bhagat movement shows how Adivasi society shaped religious reform, economic resistance and Gandhian satyagraha into its own tradition and made a unique contribution to the freedom struggle.
