## 'The Abode of Saints' on a Hill

About **20 kilometres** north-west of the town of **Pudukkottai** in Tamil Nadu lies the village of **Sittanavasal**. The name is believed to come from a Tamil phrase meaning **'the abode of great saints'**. Near the village is a rocky hill about 70 metres high, on which **Jain monks** lived, practised austerities and preached for centuries. Today the place is known for the murals in its small cave, which many art historians count among the most important examples of ancient Indian painting after **Ajanta**.

The whole complex is protected by the **Archaeological Survey of India (ASI)**, and visitors reach it by climbing a few hundred steps.

## Eladipattam — The Beds of the Monks

On the hill is a natural cavern called **Eladipattam** (or Ezhadippattam). In it, **17 beds** have been made by smoothing the rock, each with a pillow-like raised headrest. Here Jain monks rested and meditated. The largest bed bears an inscription in **Tamil-Brahmi script**, which most scholars date to the **1st century BCE** (some consider it older). According to the inscription, a person had this seat made for a monk. The other beds carry short inscriptions from the 7th to the 10th century, some of which give the names of monks who undertook **sallekhana** (giving up the body through fasting). This shows that the hill remained a centre of Jain practice for about a thousand years.

## Arivar Koil — A Temple Cut into the Rock

On the western part of the hill is the rock-cut cave temple **Arivar Koil** ('temple of the Arhats'). It has a small **ardhamandapa** (verandah) and behind it a square **sanctum**. The niches of the verandah hold relief images of **Parshvanatha** (with the canopy of a five-hooded serpent) and a Jain acharya, and the sanctum contains figures of three **Tirthankaras**. A **Dharmachakra** is carved on the ceiling of the sanctum.

Scholars have differed over the date of this cave. It was first attributed to the time of the Pallava king **Mahendravarman I** (c. 580–630 CE), who is said to have followed Jainism before becoming a Shaiva. Later, on the basis of inscriptions found here, it was linked to the **Pandya** kings. One inscription records that the temple was **renovated** under Pandya rule in the 9th century, and most of the paintings seen today are attributed to this period — about **850 CE**.

## The Lotus-Pond Murals

Sittanavasal's real fame lies in its **murals**. On the ceiling of the ardhamandapa is a vast **lotus pond** — round green leaves, blooming and half-open red-pink lotuses, **fish** swimming in the water, **geese**, **elephants** and **buffaloes** drinking, and **people** plucking lotuses. On the pillars are graceful figures of two **dancers**. According to scholars, the scene depicts part of the Jain **Samavasarana** — the divine assembly hall where the Tirthankaras preach after attaining omniscience, described as surrounded by ponds full of lotuses.

These paintings were made by the **fresco-secco** method — that is, with **mineral colours** (ochre, yellow, green, black, white) on a thin layer of dry lime plaster. The lines are very assured and the figures show an Ajanta-like delicacy. Praising these paintings, the writer **Mulk Raj Anand** wrote that here lotuses seem to spring up from imaginary ponds.

## Discovery, Conservation and Significance

Scholars' attention turned to this site at the beginning of the 20th century. In **1920**, a publication by the French scholar **Jouveau-Dubreuil** and **Gopinatha Rao** brought Sittanavasal to the notice of the international archaeological world. In **1937–39** the Pudukkottai princely state had the paintings cleaned and conserved, and in **1942**, during cleaning, **S. Paramasivan** and **K. R. Srinivasan** discovered an older layer beneath the upper paintings. Excavations nearby (1934–35) also found **megalithic** burials, which show that the area had been settled long before.

Unfortunately, because of damp, dust and careless visitors, much of the paintings has flaked away. Even so, Sittanavasal shows how ancient and influential **Jainism in the Tamil country** was, and that South India too had a tradition of painting as refined as that of Ajanta and Bagh. The ASI has placed it among its 'Adarsh Smarak' (model monument) sites, and it remains one of Tamil Nadu's most important monuments for art lovers and students of history.
