## Paithan and Childhood

**Eknath** was born around **1533** in the ancient city of **Paithan** (Pratishthana) on the banks of the Godavari, in a Deshastha Brahmin family. His parents died while he was a child, and his grandfather raised him. His great-grandfather **Bhanudas** was himself a Warkari saint; tradition says he brought the idol of Vithoba of Pandharpur back from Vijayanagara.

At that time Paithan and the surrounding region were under the sultanates of the Deccan, mainly the Nizam Shahi of Ahmednagar. Persian administration, Sanskrit learning and Marathi folk culture existed side by side here, and this mixture can be seen in Eknath's works too.

## Guru Janardan Swami

In his teens Eknath went to **Devagiri** (Daulatabad) and became a disciple of **Janardan Swami**. Janardan Swami was a worshipper of Dattatreya; according to tradition he was also an official in the fort of Daulatabad. Eknath stayed in his service for several years and learned Vedanta and devotion. For this reason he often calls himself "Eka Janardani" (Eka of Janardan) at the end of his compositions. At his guru's bidding he went on pilgrimage and then returned to Paithan, where he lived as a householder while spreading devotion.

## The Corrected Text of the Dnyaneshwari and the Eknathi Bhagavat

After being copied by hand for about three hundred years, the **Dnyaneshwari** had accumulated many errors and interpolations. Eknath gathered many copies, compared them and prepared a corrected text. According to tradition, this work was completed in Shaka year 1506 (about **1584** CE). The text of the Dnyaneshwari in use today is believed to rest on this foundation.

His greatest work is the **Eknathi Bhagavat**, a detailed commentary in Marathi ovis on the **eleventh book** of the Bhagavata Purana (the Krishna-Uddhava dialogue), containing about 18,800 ovis. It was begun at Paithan and completed at Varanasi. Some scholars who held Sanskrit alone to be the language of religion opposed such a work in Marathi, but Eknath argued that God understands every language. He also wrote the **Bhavartha Ramayana** on the Ramayana, which a disciple completed after his death, and composed poems such as *Rukmini Swayamvar*.

## Bharud: Devotion in Folk Theatre

Eknath's most unusual contribution is the **bharuds**, about 300 short, dramatic and often humorous songs. In them he speaks in the voice of characters such as the jogi, vasudev, gondhali, fakir, ghost, scorpion or snake, and uses allegory to strike at ego, greed and hypocrisy. Ordinary village people would laugh as they listened to them and also grasp the deeper point. Even today bharuds are sung in the Ashadhi Wari and in village kirtans.

## Questions on Caste and Legacy

Many stories about Eknath concern opposition to untouchability, such as picking up a crying Mahar child on the scorching sands of the Godavari, or feeding Mahars on the day of a shraddha ritual, which angered the Brahmins of Paithan. These stories come from later saint biographies and their details cannot be tested historically, but they show how the tradition remembers him. Although a Brahmin himself, Eknath built a bridge between classical scholarship and devotion in the language of the people.

According to tradition, Eknath took water samadhi in the Godavari on Phalguna Krishna Shashthi in **1599** (in February 1600 according to some calculations). In the Warkari tradition he is counted among the chief saints along with Dnyaneshwar, Namdev and Tukaram, and his samadhi temple at Paithan is still a major place of pilgrimage.
