## Background: Rivalry at Sea

In 1600 the **East India Company** was formed in London. At that time the **Portuguese** dominated India's western coast. In 1612 at Suvali (Swally) near Surat English ships defeated a Portuguese fleet, after which the English received initial permission to trade at **Surat**. But this permission depended on the whims of local officials. The Company wanted a direct, written and permanent promise from the emperor. For this task King **James I** sent an experienced diplomat, **Sir Thomas Roe**, as his ambassador.

## From Surat to Ajmer

Roe left England in early 1615 and reached Surat in **September 1615**. From there he made a long journey to **Ajmer**, where Jahangir's court was then camped. On **10 January 1616** he was presented before the emperor.

Roe insisted that he was the representative of a king, not an ordinary merchant, and that he should be treated as an equal. This seemed strange to the Mughal court — to one of the richest empires in the world, England was a small, distant island kingdom.

## The World of the Court

For about three years Roe travelled along with the emperor — from Ajmer to **Mandu** and then to **Ahmedabad**. He gave the emperor gifts brought from England, including an English coach and pictures. In his diary he gave detailed descriptions of Jahangir's court, the daily jharokha darshan, the emperor's weighing ceremony, drinking parties and court politics.

Roe soon realised that real power lay not only with the emperor but also with Nur Jahan's faction — her brother **Asaf Khan** and Prince **Khurram** (later Shah Jahan). Khurram was then governor of Gujarat, so the matter of Surat came under his authority.

## The Result of the Mission

The formal treaty of equals that Roe had come for was not achieved. Mughal emperors did not think a treaty with a foreign merchant group suited their dignity. Even so, in **1618** an order came from Khurram assuring English merchants at Surat the right to live there, to buy and sell goods, and protection from the arbitrariness of local officials. In **1619** Roe returned from India.

Roe advised the Company to focus on maritime and peaceful trade rather than building forts and fighting wars on land in India, because the cost of land armies would eat up the profits. This advice remained the basis of the Company's policy for about a century.

## Two Perspectives

It is necessary to see this episode from two sides. In English historical writing, Roe's mission is often shown as the 'foundation of empire'. But for the Mughal side it was no great event — Roe's name does not even appear in Jahangir's autobiography. At that time India was among the world's largest producers, and European merchants were only buyers of its cloth, indigo and spices.

## Significance

Thomas Roe's diary today is an important European source on Jahangir's reign, though it also reflects the preconceptions of a foreigner. The English factory at Surat later became the Company's main centre in western India, until Bombay replaced it. This same small beginning in trade led, a hundred and fifty years later, to events such as Plassey (1757), when the Company began to turn from merchant into ruler.
