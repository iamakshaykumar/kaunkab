## Background — Winds of Reform in Bombay

By the middle of the nineteenth century Bombay had become a major commercial and educational centre. A new generation of English-educated young men was emerging from **Elphinstone College** and from **Bombay University**, founded in **1857**. On the one hand these young people were influenced by Western reason and science; on the other they were troubled by the evils of their own society — child marriage, the plight of widows, untouchability and caste discrimination. In Bengal, **Raja Rammohan Roy**'s **Brahmo Samaj** had already shown such a path, and in Maharashtra secret reformist groups such as the 'Paramhansa Sabha' had also been formed.

In **1864** and again in **1867** the Brahmo leader **Keshub Chandra Sen** came to Bombay. Inspired by his speeches, on **31 March 1867** Dr **Atmaram Pandurang**, **Waman Abaji Modak** and other colleagues founded the **Prarthana Samaj**. 'Prarthana Samaj' means 'the society of those who pray'.

## Ideas and Programme

Members of the Prarthana Samaj were **monotheists**. They worshipped one formless God and did not accept idol worship, ritualism, the monopoly of priests or the doctrine of incarnations. Although inspired by the Brahmo Samaj, it did not declare itself a separate religion; instead it spoke of reform while remaining within the Hindu tradition. In its weekly prayer meetings, along with the **Upanishads** and the **Bhagavad Gita**, the abhangas of **Marathi bhakti saints** such as **Tukaram** and **Namdev** were sung. In this way it linked reform with Maharashtra's own devotional tradition.

The society's emphasis was less on religious debate and more on **social reform**. Its main programmes were:

- opposition to the **caste system** and untouchability;
- support for **widow remarriage** and opposition to **child marriage**;
- the spread of **women's education**;
- night schools, orphanages and relief institutions for workers and the poor;
- the uplift of Dalit and deprived classes.

Its leading figures included the Sanskrit scholar **Ramakrishna Gopal Bhandarkar**, the judge **Narayan Ganesh Chandavarkar** and, above all, **Mahadev Govind Ranade**. Later its missionary **Vitthal Ramji Shinde** started the **Depressed Classes Mission** in **1906**. In the south, **Kandukuri Veeresalingam** spread its ideas in Andhra.

## Mahadev Govind Ranade — His Life

**Mahadev Govind Ranade** was born on **18 January 1842** at **Niphad** in Nashik district. He was among the first students of Bombay University and took his B.A. in **1862**, then his M.A., and his LL.B. in **1866**. In **1871** he became a judge in Pune. British officials were suspicious of his political activity, so his promotion was delayed; finally, in the **1890s**, he became a judge of the **Bombay High Court**. This is why he is respectfully called **'Justice Ranade'** (Nyayamurti Ranade).

He was active in reform from very early on. In **1861** he was among the founders of the **Widow Marriage Association**. After joining the Prarthana Samaj he became its greatest thinker. He played a major role in guiding the **Poona Sarvajanik Sabha**, formed in **1870**, which conveyed people's grievances to the government. He was among the early leaders of the **Indian National Congress**, founded in **1885**, and alongside it he took the lead in laying the foundation of the **Indian National Social Conference**, so that social reform would also be discussed every year along with political demands. He also helped found the **Huzurpaga** girls' school in Pune.

## Thinker, Economist and Historian

Ranade believed that **"the reformer must attempt to deal with the whole man and not to carry out reform on one side only"** — that is, religious, social, economic and political progress are interlinked. He favoured gradual, peaceful reform rather than sudden upheaval.

In economics, his book **'Essays on Indian Economics' (1899)** argued that England's free-trade theory could not be applied as it was to Indian conditions; he stressed the development of Indian industries and government support for them. In history, his book **'Rise of the Maratha Power' (1900)** saw the rise of Maratha power as a popular movement linked to the inspiration of the bhakti saints. **Gopal Krishna Gokhale** regarded him as his guru.

There was also a controversy over Ranade's personal life. After the death of his first wife, under family pressure, he married not a widow but **Ramabai**, a girl of about eleven. Critics said this went against his own principles. But Ranade educated Ramabai, and **Ramabai Ranade** later became a well-known social reformer in her own right. Ranade died on **16 January 1901**.

## Legacy

The Prarthana Samaj was never very large in numbers, yet it created a lasting current of reform in Maharashtra. Its members built schools, orphanages, widows' homes and institutions for the uplift of Dalits. Its greatest feature was that it spoke of reform with the help of the humanist message of the bhakti saints, without breaking completely with tradition.

Ranade taught Indians that, along with demanding political rights, removing the evils of their own society was just as necessary. A whole generation of liberal leaders — from Gokhale onwards — learned this lesson from him. Today the **Ranade Institute** in Pune and many other institutions bear his name.
