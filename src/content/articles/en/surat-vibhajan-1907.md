## Two Paths, One Organisation

From 1885 the Congress was influenced by leaders called the **Moderates** — such as **Pherozeshah Mehta**, **Gopal Krishna Gokhale** and **Surendranath Banerjee**. They wanted gradual reform through petitions, deputations, speeches and constitutional methods.

In the early twentieth century the **Extremists** emerged, whose main faces were **Bal Gangadhar Tilak**, **Lala Lajpat Rai**, **Bipin Chandra Pal** and **Aurobindo Ghose**. They believed that nothing would be gained by pleading with the British; the people had to be brought onto the path of agitation, boycott and self-reliance.

## Conflict Sharpened by the Partition of Bengal

The **Partition of Bengal** in 1905 and the **Swadeshi Movement** that rose against it sharpened this difference. The Extremists wanted the boycott to be spread across the whole country and to government institutions, while the Moderates wanted to keep it limited.

At the **Calcutta session** of 1906, under the presidency of Dadabhai Naoroji, resolutions were passed on **swaraj, swadeshi, boycott and national education**. The Extremists feared that at the next session the Moderates would weaken these resolutions.

## The Surat Session, December 1907

The 1907 session was first to be held at **Nagpur**, where the Extremists had greater influence. The session was shifted to **Surat**, and it is believed that the thinking behind this was to keep Tilak from becoming president, because by rule a leader of the host province could not become president, and Surat was in Tilak's own Bombay province.

The Extremists wanted to make **Lala Lajpat Rai** president, but Lajpat Rai withdrew his name to avoid a clash. The Moderates proposed **Rash Behari Ghosh** as president.

On **26 December 1907**, when his name was proposed, the Extremists objected and an uproar began. The next day, on **27 December**, when Tilak tried to speak, the situation got out of hand — a shoe was thrown towards the platform, chairs and sticks were used, and the session had to be dissolved. According to accounts, the shoe hit Surendranath Banerjee and Pherozeshah Mehta; who threw it and who provoked first is described differently by the two sides.

## After the Split

The Moderates kept their control over the Congress. At the conference held at **Allahabad** in April 1908 they framed the Congress constitution, in which the aim was set as 'self-government within the Empire' and the method as only constitutional. This made it difficult for the Extremists to return to the Congress.

The British government took advantage of this split. Strict action was taken against Extremist newspapers, and in 1908 **Tilak** was sentenced to **six years** in a sedition trial based on articles written in 'Kesari' and sent to **Mandalay** (Burma). Aurobindo Ghose was caught up in the Alipore Bomb Case and later left politics and went to Pondicherry. Through the **Morley-Minto Reforms** of 1909 the government tried to win over the Moderates.

## Significance

The Surat split weakened the national movement for some years. But the roles of the two factions are also seen as complementary — the Moderates laid the foundation of economic criticism and constitutional demands, while the Extremists took the movement to ordinary people.

After Tilak's release in 1914 and the efforts of Annie Besant, reconciliation began and at the **Lucknow session of 1916** both factions came onto one platform again. This very experience later became the background to the mass movements of the Gandhi era.
