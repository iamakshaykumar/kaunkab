## Life: Tradition and Evidence

**Surdas** is considered the greatest poet of the Krishna-devotion branch of the Bhakti period of Hindi literature. Very little solid historical information about his life is available. Tradition places his birth at about **1478 CE** and his death at about **1583 CE**, that is, a life of more than a hundred years; some sources give slightly different dates. As his birthplace, both the village of **Sihi** near Delhi (now in Haryana) and **Runkata** on the Agra-Mathura road are claimed.

In popular memory Surdas was blind from birth, and the word "Surdas" is still used respectfully in Hindi for a visually impaired person. But many scholars point out that his verses contain extremely fine descriptions of colours and scenes, so whether he was born blind or lost his sight later is an open question.

## Vallabhacharya and the Ashtachhap

According to the varta literature of the Pushtimarg (Vallabha sect), especially the 'Chaurasi Vaishnavan ki Varta', Surdas lived at Gaughat on the bank of the Yamuna and sang verses of humility. There he met **Vallabhacharya**, who inspired him to sing of Krishna's pastimes. Later Vallabhacharya's son **Vitthalanatha** formed the group of eight poet-singers called the **Ashtachhap**, and Surdas was counted the foremost among them. He performed kirtan at the temple of Shrinathji at Govardhan.

Modern researchers — especially John Stratton Hawley — believe that the oldest manuscripts of Sur do not give clear signs of a link with the Vallabha sect, so this connection may have been created later by the sect's tradition. In the same way the story of Surdas's meeting with Akbar is also part of tradition, not proven history.

## Sursagar: A Growing Ocean

The collection of Surdas's verses is called **Sursagar**. Tradition says he composed a hundred and twenty-five thousand verses, and the printed editions (such as that of the Nagari Pracharini Sabha) have several thousand verses. But in his oldest dated manuscript — the **Fatehpur manuscript of 1582 CE** — there are only **239 verses** bearing the "Sur" signature. Scholars conclude that for centuries singers kept adding new verses in Sur's name, and "Sur" became the name not only of a poet but of an entire singing tradition. 'Sursaravali' and 'Sahitya Lahari' are also linked to his name, but there is disagreement about their authenticity.

## Krishna's Childhood and Separation

Surdas's greatest recognition comes from his verses of **vatsalya** (parental love) and **shringara** (romantic love). The episodes of Yashoda and the child Krishna — butter theft, complaints, sulking and coaxing — are so lively that in Hindi criticism Sur has been called the greatest poet of vatsalya. Among the verses on the love of Radha and the gopis and their separation when Krishna went away to Mathura, the **Bhramargeet** episode is especially famous, in which the gopis silence Uddhava, who had come to preach knowledge, with the logic of love.

His verses are set to ragas and raginis — that is, they were composed more for singing than for reading. The old manuscripts also contain a large number of verses of the devotee's humility (vinaya).

## Legacy

Surdas raised **Braj Bhasha** from a folk speech into a poetic language that remained the main literary language of north India for the next three hundred years. Along with Mirabai, Tulsidas and Kabir, he is among those poets of devotional poetry whose lines are still on the lips of ordinary people. The haveli music tradition of the temples, classical singers and bhajan groups — all keep Sur's verses alive even today.
