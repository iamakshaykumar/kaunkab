## Background: Indigo and the Tinkathia System

From the nineteenth century, European planters (known as "nilahe") had indigo grown in the **Champaran** district of north Bihar. The **tinkathia system** was in force here, under which farmers were required to grow indigo on 3 kathas out of every bigha (20 kathas) of their land — and that too at a low price fixed by the planters.

At the beginning of the twentieth century, synthetic indigo began to be made in Germany, and the demand for natural indigo fell. Many planters then began to demand heavy rent increases or lump-sum payments from the farmers in return for 'releasing' them from the obligation to grow indigo. All kinds of illegal cesses (abwabs) were also imposed on the farmers.

## Rajkumar Shukla's Request

**Rajkumar Shukla**, a farmer of Champaran, had been raising his voice against this injustice for a long time. He met Gandhiji at the Congress session held in Lucknow in December 1916 and urged him to come to Champaran and see the condition of the farmers with his own eyes. Gandhiji did not know much about Champaran at that time, but Shukla's persistence persuaded him.

## Defying the Order at Motihari

In April 1917, Gandhiji travelled via Patna and Muzaffarpur to **Motihari**, the district headquarters of Champaran. He wanted to meet the farmers and record their statements. Citing the fear of a 'breach of the peace', the administration ordered him to leave the district immediately.

Gandhiji politely refused to obey this order and said he was ready to bear the punishment for it. His calm and firm statement in court left the administration baffled. A large number of farmers gathered outside the court. In the end the government withdrew the case, and Gandhiji was allowed to continue his inquiry.

## Farmers' Statements and the Inquiry Committee

After this, Gandhiji and his associates — **Rajendra Prasad**, **Brajkishore Prasad**, **Anugrah Narayan Sinha**, **J. B. Kripalani**, **Mahadev Desai** and others — went from village to village and recorded the statements of thousands of farmers. Complete transparency was maintained in this work, and every complaint was carefully recorded.

Because of the growing pressure, the government set up an **inquiry committee** in 1917, and Gandhiji was also made a member of it. The committee found most of the farmers' complaints to be true. On the basis of its recommendations, the **Champaran Agrarian Act** was passed in 1918, which abolished the tinkathia system. The planters had to return a part (about 25 percent) of the money they had illegally collected to the farmers.

## Constructive Work

In Champaran Gandhiji did not only lead an agitation; he also started opening schools in villages such as Bhitiharwa and began work on cleanliness and health improvement. Kasturba Gandhi and many volunteers joined in this constructive work.

## Historical Significance

The Champaran Satyagraha was the first successful trial of the method of satyagraha in India. It showed that nonviolent and disciplined resistance could make the government yield. Afterwards, in 1918, Gandhiji adopted the same method in the movements of the farmers of Kheda (Gujarat) and the mill workers of Ahmedabad. It was from Champaran that Gandhiji emerged as a leader of ordinary farmers, and the national movement began to reach the villages.
