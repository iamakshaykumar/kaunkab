## Rajaraja's 'Rajarajeswaram'

In the time of the Chola emperor **Rajaraja I** (reigned c. 985–1014), Chola power had become the greatest power in South India. In his capital **Thanjavur** he built a huge temple of Shiva, called **Rajarajeswaram** in inscriptions. Today it is known as the **Brihadeeswarar** (Great Lord) Temple, and local people call it **Peria Kovil** (the Big Temple).

Construction began in about **1003 CE** and in **1010 CE** the temple was consecrated by placing a gold-plated finial on the summit. That is, such a huge structure was ready in about seven years. For the broader history of the Chola Empire, see the separate article "The Chola Empire".

## The Vimana: A Mountain of Granite

In the Dravidian style, the tower above the sanctum is called the **vimana**. The vimana of Brihadeeswarar is more than **60 metres** high (different sources give 60 to 66 metres) and above its base it has **13 storeys** that grow progressively smaller. At that time it was among the tallest temple structures in India.

The entire temple is built mainly of **granite**, although there are no granite quarries around Thanjavur — the stone was brought from far away. At the top of the vimana is said to be a huge block of stone weighing about **80 tonnes**. There are many guesses about how it was raised to such a height (such as a long earthen ramp), but no contemporary account is found.

In the sanctum is a huge Shiva linga about **8.7 metres** high. In the courtyard is a **Nandi** carved from a single stone, whose weight is said to be about 25 tonnes.

## Inscriptions: The Temple as an Institution

The walls of the temple are full of **Tamil inscriptions**. They record the gifts of Rajaraja, his sister **Kundavai**, the queens and officials, the revenue coming from villages, and details of jewels and bronze images. An inscription of 1011 CE lists **more than 600 people** attached to the temple — dancers, dance teachers, musicians, priests and craftsmen.

This shows that a Chola temple was not merely a place of worship but a large economic and social institution that managed land, wealth and people.

## Murals and Later Additions

On the walls of the corridor around the sanctum are **Chola-period murals**, which were identified in **1931**; later, paintings of the Nayaka period had been painted over them. Most of them have Shaiva themes. Carvings of the **karanas** (dance poses) of Bharatanatyam are also found here.

Later **Nayaka** rulers and the **Marathas of Thanjavur** also added some temple pavilions in the temple complex and maintained it. The shrine of Brihannayaki (the goddess) is such a later addition.

## World Heritage and the Temple Today

In **1987** UNESCO declared it a World Heritage Site. In **2004** this listing was extended to include **Gangaikonda Cholapuram** (Rajendra Chola's temple) and the **Airavatesvara Temple at Darasuram**, and the three together were called the **'Great Living Chola Temples'** — 'living' because worship is still held here today.

In **September 2010**, on the completion of 1000 years of the temple, a celebration was held in which a thousand dancers performed Bharatanatyam and a postage stamp was issued. A thousand years later, this temple is still living proof of Chola engineering, art and administration.
