## The Legacy of Colonial Laws

Even after independence, India's criminal justice system rested on three old laws — the **Indian Penal Code (IPC), 1860**, the **Code of Criminal Procedure (CrPC)**, whose current form dated from 1973, and the **Indian Evidence Act, 1872**. The first defined which acts are crimes and what their punishment is; the second laid down how arrest, investigation, bail and trial would proceed; and the third decided which evidence would be accepted in court.

These laws were amended many times, but their foundation went back to the era of British rule. For a long time there had been a demand that they be changed to suit today's society, technology and new forms of crime — such as cybercrime and organised crime. From 2019 onwards the central government sought suggestions from states, judges, universities and experts, and also set up a committee for this purpose.

## Three Bills in Parliament

On **11 August 2023**, Home Minister **Amit Shah** introduced three bills in the Lok Sabha — the **Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita**, the **Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita** and the **Bharatiya Sakshya Bill**. They were sent to the Parliamentary Standing Committee on Home Affairs. After the committee's recommendations, on **12 December 2023** the government withdrew the old bills and introduced new ones in revised form.

The Lok Sabha passed them on **20 December 2023** and the Rajya Sabha on **21 December 2023**. On **25 December 2023** President **Droupadi Murmu** gave her assent. Allowing time to train police, lawyers and judicial officers, the government brought them into force from **1 July 2024**. Cases registered after this date proceed under the new laws, while cases from before it continue under the old laws.

## The Three Laws and Their Main Changes

The **Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita (BNS)** replaced the Indian Penal Code. Compared with the IPC's 511 sections, it has **358 sections**, because many similar sections were merged. For the first time, **organised crime** and **terrorist acts** were defined in the general criminal law. A separate provision was made for murder by a mob on grounds such as caste, religion or language — commonly called **mob lynching**. Offences against women and children were placed in a separate chapter. **Community service** was added as a punishment for minor offences.

The old offence of **sedition** (Section 124A) was removed, but in its place **Section 152** made "acts endangering the sovereignty, unity and integrity of India" an offence.

The **Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita (BNSS)** replaced the Code of Criminal Procedure. It has about **531 sections**. It gave a legal basis to the **Zero FIR** — that is, registering a complaint at any police station, wherever the incident took place — and to the electronic **e-FIR**. A **forensic investigation** at the crime scene was made mandatory for offences punishable with seven years or more, and **audio-video recording** of searches and seizures was provided for. Several time limits were set to reduce delays in trials, such as delivering judgment normally within 30 days (at most 45 days) after arguments are completed.

The **Bharatiya Sakshya Adhiniyam (BSA)** replaced the Evidence Act of 1872. It has **170 sections**. It gives **electronic and digital records** — such as emails, server logs and smartphone messages — the same status as evidence as paper documents.

## The Government's View

According to the government, these laws symbolise freedom from a colonial mindset. Home Minister Amit Shah said the aim of the old laws was to give "punishment", whereas the new laws are centred on the citizen and on **"justice"**. The government claimed that the use of technology, forensic investigation and fixed time limits would make trials faster and raise the conviction rate. Along with the commencement of these laws, several new apps and digital platforms were also launched for the police and the courts.

## Criticism and Debate

Opposition parties and several legal experts raised questions about these laws. Critics said the bills were passed in the winter session of Parliament when a large number of opposition MPs were suspended, so they could not be debated adequately. Some experts felt that a large part of the new laws was simply a renumbered copy of the old ones.

Particular concern was raised about some provisions — such as the wording of Section 152, described as vague, with fears of its effect on freedom of expression; the arrangement making the period of police custody more flexible; and the retention of marital rape as an exception. In January 2024 truck drivers went on strike against the provision of harsher punishment in **hit-and-run** cases, after which the government gave an assurance that this provision would be implemented only after discussion with transport organisations. Some states in South India and bar associations also objected to the laws having names only in Hindi.

## Significance

**1 July 2024** is a major turning point in the history of independent India's criminal justice system — for the first time the IPC, the CrPC and the Evidence Act were all changed together. Their real impact will depend on how efficiently the police, forensic laboratories and courts implement the new provisions on the ground, and on how the High Courts and the Supreme Court interpret them. From law students to ordinary citizens, everyone now needs to know that familiar numbers such as the old "Section 302" or "Section 420" have changed into new sections.
