## Who Were the Sannyasis and Fakirs

In eighteenth-century Bengal and Bihar there were two kinds of wandering groups of holy men in large numbers. On one side were the **Dashnami sannyasis** (of branches such as Giri and Puri) and Nath yogis, many of whom travelled in armed **Naga** bands. On the other side were the Sufi **fakirs** of the **Madariya order**. Both went on pilgrimages, took alms from villages and customary gifts from zamindars, and in many places also engaged in trade and moneylending. Their places of residence (the akharas of the sannyasis and the khanqahs of the fakirs) and their customs were somewhat similar, which created coordination between them.

## Why the Rebellion Happened

After Plassey in **1757** and Buxar in **1764**, in **1765** the East India Company received the **Diwani** (the right to collect revenue) of Bengal, Bihar and Orissa. The Company raised its tax demands rapidly. The pressed zamindars could no longer give the holy men gifts as before. The Company banned organised begging tours, regarding them as an unauthorised burden on villages, and viewed these bands of holy men as robbers.

Meanwhile the terrible **Bengal famine** of **1770** made matters worse. A large part of the population died, fields were laid waste, and many starving peasants joined these bands. Local peasants also helped the holy men out of religious reverence and gave them news of Company detachments.

## Major Events

According to Banglapedia, the resistance began around **1760** and intensified in **1763**. In that year the Company factories at **Bakarganj** and **Dhaka** were attacked, and the head of the factory at Ramapur Boalia in **Rajshahi** was taken prisoner and carried off to Patna. In the following decades raids took place as far as Rangpur, Dinajpur, Rajshahi, Purnea, Mymensingh, Comilla and Chittagong. The targets were Company factories, revenue offices and the strongholds of zamindars loyal to the Company.

Their main strategy was guerrilla-style sudden attacks and then slipping away towards the forests or the terai of Nepal and Bhutan. In **1767** a British detachment was defeated, while in **1771** the Company defeated them near Ghoraghat. In the same year, the killing of about 150 sannyasis further inflamed the violence. The number of rebels is given as anywhere from a few thousand to several tens of thousands in the 1770s, although these estimates are based on Company records and should be treated with caution.

## Majnu Shah and Bhawani Pathak

The most famous leader of this movement was the Madariya fakir **Majnu Shah**. He led organised bands on horses and camels carrying supplies. In **1772** he captured the revenue office of Rajshahi and took the treasury. In **1785** he was defeated near Mahasthangarh, and on **8 December 1786** he suffered heavy losses in the battle of **Kaleshwar** against the force of Lieutenant Brennan. It is believed that he died of the wounds received there on **26 January 1788**.

After him **Musa Shah**, **Chirag Ali Shah**, Karim Shah and others continued the struggle. On the sannyasis' side, the Bhojpuri Brahmin **Bhawani Pathak** was a major leader, who had contact with Majnu Shah. Because of the fragmentation of leadership and the Company's growing military control, the movement weakened in the 1790s and by **about 1800** had shrunk to scattered incidents.

## Rebellion or Plunder? The Historians' Debate

Historians do not agree on how to understand this movement. Company officials called them merely "robbers". Many Indian and Bangladeshi historians regard it as an early organised resistance to Company rule, in which Hindu and Muslim holy men fought together with the support of peasants. Some other historians say it was a struggle to protect their economic interests and the right to beg amid the disorder after the famine, which did not receive wide popular support. The truth probably lies somewhere between the two.

## Anandamath and Memory

About a hundred years later **Bankim Chandra Chattopadhyay** wrote the novels **"Anandamath"** (1882) and **"Devi Chaudhurani"** (1884) against the background of this rebellion and the famine of 1770. It was in "Anandamath" that the song **"Vande Mataram"** appeared, which went on to become a major slogan of the freedom movement. It is worth noting that the novel is a fictional version of history: it portrays the rebellion only as a struggle of sannyasis, whereas in the historical movement the role of the fakirs was equally large.
