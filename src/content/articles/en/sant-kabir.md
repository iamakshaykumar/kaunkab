## Birth and Life: History and Tradition

Very little is firmly known about **Kabir**'s life; most of it comes from later traditions and stories. Scholars also disagree about his time: tradition places his birth in 1398 CE, while many modern scholars place his lifetime roughly between 1440 and 1518.

It is almost certain that he lived in **Kashi (Varanasi)** and was a **julaha** (weaver), whose family followed Islam. According to a famous story, a weaver couple named Niru and Nima found him as an infant near the Lahartara pond and raised him. Metaphors of the loom, warp and weft, and weaving cloth come up again and again in Kabir's own compositions, recalling his trade.

## Guru Ramananda and Nirguna Bhakti

Tradition says Kabir accepted the famous Vaishnava saint of Kashi, **Ramananda**, as his guru. According to one story, he lay down at dawn on the steps of Panchganga Ghat; Ramananda, going to bathe, stepped on him, and the words "Ram-Ram" that came out of his mouth became Kabir's guru-mantra. Historians are not sure about this relationship, since the times of the two may differ.

Kabir's Rama was not an incarnation or an idol but **nirguna**, the Supreme Truth without form or attributes. He called upon Him by many names such as Ram, Hari, Allah and Sahib, and believed that He is within every human being.

## Utterances: Sakhi, Sabad and Ramaini

According to his tradition, Kabir himself was not literate. His utterances spread orally and were later recorded in different collections:
- **Bijak**: the main text of the Kabir Panth, linked to the tradition of eastern Uttar Pradesh and Bihar
- **Kabir Granthavali**: a collection linked to the Dadu Panthi tradition of Rajasthan
- **Guru Granth Sahib**: hundreds of Kabir's verses and hymns alongside those of the Sikh Gurus

His compositions are found in three forms: the **sakhi** (witness, that is, testimony to truth) in doha metre, the sung verses or **sabad**, and the **ramaini**. His language is called 'sadhukkadi' or 'panchmel khichdi' (a mixture of five), in which words of Khari Boli, Braj, Awadhi, Bhojpuri and Rajasthani are blended. One of his famous dohas (also found with variant readings) is:

> Pothi padhi padhi jag mua, pandit bhaya na koy.
> Dhai aakhar prem ka, padhai so pandit hoy.

That is, the world has tired itself out reading book after book, yet no one has become truly learned; whoever has understood the two and a half letters of love is the one who is truly wise.

## Attack on Hypocrisy

Kabir questioned without hesitation the outward show of both Hindu and Muslim communities: idol worship, pilgrimages, fasting, roza, the azan called in a loud voice, the sacred thread and the pride of caste. He believed that God is found not in the temple or mosque but in a sincere heart.

For this reason he faced opposition from both sides in his lifetime. Tradition also has stories of a sultan (in many tales Sikandar Lodi) punishing him, but there is no contemporary historical evidence for these.

## Maghar and Kabir's Legacy

At that time it was believed that dying in Kashi brings liberation, while one who dies at **Maghar** (in today's Sant Kabir Nagar district) is reborn in a wretched state. According to tradition, Kabir went to Maghar deliberately at the end of his life to challenge this superstition.

A famous **legend** says that after his death Hindus wanted to cremate him and Muslims wanted to bury him; but when the sheet was lifted, only flowers were found in place of the body, which the two groups divided. Today at Maghar Kabir's samadhi and mazar stand side by side.

Kabir's followers organised themselves as the **Kabir Panth**, which is still active in north and central India. His sakhis live on in school textbooks, among folk singers and in everyday speech; six centuries later they remain a voice of equality, simplicity and love.
