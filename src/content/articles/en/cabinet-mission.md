## Background: A Changing Britain, a Restless India

After the Second World War Britain was economically exhausted. In July 1945 the Labour Party of **Clement Attlee** came to power there, favouring independence for India. In India the trials of the Indian National Army and the naval mutiny of February 1946 had heated up the atmosphere. In the elections of 1945–46 the Congress won heavily in the general seats and the **Muslim League** in the Muslim seats, which strengthened the claims of both parties.

In this situation the Attlee government sent three members of its Cabinet to India — the Secretary of State for India **Lord Pethick-Lawrence**, the President of the Board of Trade **Sir Stafford Cripps**, and the First Lord of the Admiralty **A. V. Alexander**. The Viceroy **Lord Wavell** also took part in the talks. The Mission reached Delhi on **24 March 1946**.

## Talks and Shimla

For several weeks the Mission met leaders of the Congress, the Muslim League, the princely states and other groups. In May 1946 a tripartite conference between the two major parties was also held in Shimla, but no agreement was reached. The basic question was this — would India remain one, or would a separate Pakistan be created as the Muslim League demanded?

## The Plan of 16 May

When no agreement was reached, the Mission announced its plan on **16 May 1946**. Its main points:

- A **Union of India** would be formed from British India and the princely states; the demand for a separate Pakistan was rejected.
- The Union (Centre) would have only **defence, foreign affairs and communications**, and the power to raise funds for these; all other powers would remain with the provinces.
- There would be three groups of provinces: **Group A** (Madras, Bombay, United Provinces, Bihar, Central Provinces, Orissa), **Group B** (Punjab, Sind, North-West Frontier Province, Baluchistan) and **Group C** (Bengal and Assam).
- A **Constituent Assembly** elected by the provincial legislatures would be formed — 389 members in all, of whom 296 from British India and 93 from the princely states.
- Until the constitution was made, an **interim government** of the main parties would be formed.

## From Agreement to Conflict

The council of the Muslim League accepted the plan on **6 June 1946**, because in the system of groups it saw the seed of Pakistan. The Congress agreed to join the Constituent Assembly, but its interpretation was that provinces were not bound to enter the groups — this was especially important for Assam and the North-West Frontier Province. The two also differed deeply over the make-up of the interim government. The Mission returned to London at the end of June.

Elections to the Constituent Assembly were held in July 1946, in which the Congress won 208 seats and the League 73. On **10 July 1946**, Nehru said at a press conference that the Congress would not be bound by any condition in the Constituent Assembly. After this, on **29 July** the League withdrew its acceptance and announced the observance of 'Direct Action Day' on **16 August 1946**. From that day terrible communal violence broke out in Calcutta, in which thousands of people were killed. Historians still debate how much Nehru's statement contributed to the breakdown.

## The Interim Government

On **2 September 1946** the interim government led by Jawaharlal Nehru took charge; Nehru became Vice-President of the Viceroy's Executive Council. In October 1946 the Muslim League also joined it, but it continued to boycott the Constituent Assembly. Conflict between the two parties went on within the government, which strengthened the impression that joint rule was difficult.

## Significance

The Cabinet Mission is regarded as the last major constitutional attempt to save a united India. It failed, and within a year the country was partitioned. Even so, two of its gifts proved lasting: the **Constituent Assembly**, which made the Constitution of India, and the **interim government**, which prepared the ground for the transfer of power.
