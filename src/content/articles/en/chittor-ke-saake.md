## Chittorgarh and the 'Saka'

The **fort of Chittor**, built on a high hill in Chittorgarh district of Rajasthan, was the capital of **Mewar** for centuries. In the medieval period this fort endured **three great sieges**, and each time the fort finally fell into enemy hands.

In Rajasthani tradition such a last stand is called a **saka**: when defeat became certain, the women of the fort performed **jauhar** (collective self-sacrifice) to avoid falling into enemy hands, and the men, wearing **saffron** clothes, opened the gates of the fort and rode out for a last battle. The "three sakas" of Chittor are part of this memory. These events were deep tragedies, and today they are seen not only as acts of valour but also as a reminder of the horror of war.

## The First Saka — 1303: Alauddin Khalji

The Sultan of Delhi, **Alauddin Khalji**, turned towards Chittor after conquering Ranthambhore in 1301. On **28 January 1303** he besieged the fort, then ruled by the Guhila king **Ratnasimha**. The Sultan's army camped between the Berach and Gambhiri rivers. The court poet **Amir Khusrau**, who accompanied the campaign, says that direct assaults did not succeed and stones were hurled at the fort with siege engines.

After about eight months, on **26 August 1303**, the fort fell. After the victory a large number of people were killed, and the fort was renamed **Khizrabad** after Alauddin's son. Sources say different things about the end of Ratnasimha — according to some he surrendered, according to others he was killed fighting. Whether jauhar took place in this siege is also disputed; the historian Banarsi Prasad Saxena pointed out that Khusrau does not mention jauhar at Chittor. Later, according to tradition, **Hammir** of the Sisodia branch regained Chittor for Mewar.

## Padmini: History or Legend?

The story of Rani **Padmini (Padmavati)** — that Alauddin attacked Chittor because of her beauty — is the most famous of all. But this tale is found first in the Awadhi poem **Padmavat** by the Sufi poet **Malik Muhammad Jayasi**, which was written in **1540**, that is about 237 years after the event.

The accounts of **contemporary** writers such as Amir Khusrau make no mention of Padmini. Therefore most modern historians regard the tale as **literary tradition and legend**. Alauddin and Ratnasimha (Ratansen in the poem) are historical figures, but episodes such as Padmini, the parrot Hiraman and Gora-Badal are regarded as part of the poem; some scholars also regard it as Jayasi's allegory.

## The Second Saka — 1535: Bahadur Shah

After Rana Sanga, his teenage son **Vikramaditya** was on the throne of Mewar, and his mother **Rani Karnavati** was prominent in the governance. Sultan **Bahadur Shah** of Gujarat besieged Chittor in 1533, but a treaty was made then. In November 1534 he returned with a huge army.

The defenders resisted for months, but the Gujarati **artillery** led by **Rumi Khan** broke the walls. The fort fell on **8 March 1535**. According to tradition Rani Karnavati performed jauhar with the women of the fort. The story that Karnavati sent a **rakhi** to the Mughal emperor **Humayun** asking for help is very popular, but it is not mentioned in any contemporary source, and the historians Satish Chandra and Reema Hooja regard it as a later folk tale. Soon Humayun's attack on Gujarat forced Bahadur Shah to withdraw, and Vikramaditya retook Chittor.

## The Third Saka — 1567–68: Akbar

The Mughal emperor **Akbar** besieged Chittor on **23 October 1567**. Rana **Udai Singh II** went with his family to the hills in the west — where he later founded **Udaipur** — and entrusted the command of the fort to **Jaimal Rathore**, who had chiefs such as **Patta Chundawat** with him.

The siege lasted four months. On the night of **22 February 1568**, while having a wall repaired, Jaimal was hit by a bullet from Akbar's gun and died. That same night jauhar took place in the fort, and on **23 February 1568** the Rajputs, wearing saffron, rode out for the last battle. The same day the fort came into Mughal hands, and after the victory a large number of ordinary people were also killed. In his *Fathnama* of 9 March 1568, Akbar presented this victory in religious colours.

## Legacy

Chittor never again became the capital of Mewar; the capital moved to Udaipur. But the fort, Rana Kumbha's **Vijay Stambh** (Tower of Victory) and the jauhar sites still recall these events. In 2013 Chittorgarh was declared a UNESCO World Heritage Site as part of the group "**Hill Forts of Rajasthan**". When studying history it is important to remember that contemporary accounts, later tradition and poetic tales about these sakas all together shape our understanding, and they should be distinguished from one another.
