## The Right to Work: An Old Idea

Poverty and seasonal unemployment in villages have long been a problem in India. As soon as the farming season ends, lakhs of labourers have no work and are forced to migrate to the cities. Since Independence, governments ran many schemes such as 'Food for Work' and the Jawahar Rozgar Yojana, but these depended on the government's will; people had no legal right to demand work.

**Maharashtra** led the way in this direction. After a severe drought in the early 1970s, an **Employment Guarantee Scheme** was started there, and it was later given legal form. This scheme went on to inspire the national law. **Article 41** of the Directive Principles in the Constitution also expects the State to secure the right to work.

## The Making of the Law

The UPA government formed in 2004 under **Dr. Manmohan Singh** promised a rural employment guarantee law in its Common Minimum Programme. The **National Advisory Council**, chaired by **Sonia Gandhi**, played a major role in drafting it; people such as economist **Jean Drèze** and activist **Aruna Roy** were associated with the process. A people's campaign for the 'right to employment' also ran across the country.

The Lok Sabha passed the bill on **23 August 2005** and the Rajya Sabha on **25 August 2005**, and it received Presidential assent on **5 September 2005**. On **2 February 2006** Prime Minister Manmohan Singh, in the presence of Sonia Gandhi and Andhra Pradesh Chief Minister **Y. S. Rajasekhara Reddy**, launched it in 200 backward districts of the country from **Bandlapalli** village in Anantapur district. In 2007 it was extended to more districts, and from **1 April 2008** it applied to the whole of rural India. On **2 October 2009** it was renamed the **Mahatma Gandhi National Rural Employment Guarantee Act (MGNREGA)**.

## Main Provisions

- Every rural household whose adult members were willing to do unskilled manual work had the right to at least **100 days** of wage employment in a financial year.
- A household registered with the gram panchayat to get a **job card**, and work had to be provided within **15 days** of asking for it; if not, an **unemployment allowance** was payable.
- Work was generally to be given within a 5-kilometre radius of the village, and wages were to be paid within 15 days.
- At least **one-third** of those given work were to be **women**, and there was a provision for equal wages for women and men.
- The works were mostly ones that created durable assets, such as water conservation, ponds, irrigation canals, rural roads and land development. Contractors and machines were barred.
- The **gram sabha** and panchayats had a major role in choosing and overseeing projects, and a **social audit** was mandatory, so that villagers could themselves check the spending.

## Impact and Debate

MGNREGA came to be counted among the largest public employment programmes in the world. Every year crores of households got work under it. According to several studies it raised rural wages, reduced distress migration somewhat, and increased women's participation and economic independence. In times of drought and crisis, and especially for migrant workers who returned from the cities during the **2020** COVID lockdown, it became a major support, when the government raised its budget to a record level.

But it was also criticised. Complaints of delayed wage payments, fake job cards, corruption, poor-quality works and wage rates below market rates in several states were common. Some economists said it made it harder to find labour for farming and increased government spending, while supporters argued it was an essential safety net for the rural poor. Changes such as Aadhaar-based payments and digital attendance were also debated.

## The New Law and the Legacy

In **December 2025** Parliament passed the **Viksit Bharat — Guarantee for Rozgar and Ajeevika Mission (Gramin) Act, 2025** (VB-G RAM G for short), which replaced MGNREGA. It raised the guaranteed days of work from 100 to **125** and allowed states to pause work for up to a total of 60 days during the busy sowing and harvesting seasons. Funding was also moved from a demand-based system towards fixed state-wise allocations.

According to the government, the law is a reform that links rural employment with livelihoods and development. Opposition parties opposed it in Parliament; they said that removing Mahatma Gandhi's name and changing the demand-based guarantee weakened the right to work.

Running for nearly two decades, MGNREGA was the first national legal form of the 'right to work' in India. It made the gram sabha, social audits and transparency part of rural governance, and permanently changed India's debate on social security.
