## Studying Amid Humiliation

**Bhimrao Ramji Ambedkar** was born on **14 April 1891** in **Mhow**, a military cantonment in central India. His father **Ramji Sakpal** was a subedar in the army. The family belonged to the Mahar caste, which was then considered 'untouchable'. At school he was made to sit separately in a corner of the classroom, and even when thirsty he was not allowed to touch the water himself — these experiences became the foundation of his life's struggle.

After graduating from Elphinstone College in Bombay in 1912, he went on a scholarship from the Maharaja of Baroda, **Sayajirao Gaekwad**, to **Columbia University** in the United States, where he received an M.A. in 1915 and later (in 1927) a Ph.D. Then he took an M.Sc. in 1921 and a D.Sc. in 1923 from the **London School of Economics**, and in the same year became a barrister from Gray's Inn. In that period this was an extraordinary achievement for an Indian, and especially for a Dalit.

## Movements and Journalism

Returning to India, Ambedkar raised his voice for the communities called untouchable. In 1920, with the help of **Shahu Maharaj** of Kolhapur, the Marathi paper **'Mooknayak'** was started, and in 1924 the **Bahishkrit Hitakarini Sabha** was formed for education and welfare. His famous message 'Educate, Agitate, Organise' reflects this thinking. In March 1927 the satyagraha to take water from the Chavdar tank at **Mahad** was held, and on 25 December 1927 the 'Manusmriti' was publicly burnt there. In 1930 a movement was run for entry into the **Kalaram Temple** at Nashik. (For details see — 'Mahad Satyagraha and Dr. Ambedkar'.)

## The Round Table Conferences and the Poona Pact

At the Round Table Conferences in London (1930–32), Ambedkar demanded **separate electorates** for the Dalits (then called the 'Depressed Classes'). When this demand was accepted in the Communal Award of 1932, Gandhiji began a fast unto death in Yerwada jail. Amid heavy pressure, the **Poona Pact** was concluded on **24 September 1932**, in which, in place of separate electorates, the seats reserved for Dalits within a joint electorate were raised from 71 to 148. Ambedkar later expressed deep dissatisfaction with this agreement. (For details see — 'The Poona Pact'.)

In 1935, at Yeola, he declared that he had been born a Hindu but would not die a Hindu. In 1936 he formed the **Independent Labour Party** and published the famous essay **'Annihilation of Caste'**, which sharply criticised the caste system and the texts that gave it a religious basis. From 1942 to 1946 he was the **Labour Member** of the Viceroy's Executive Council.

## Architect of the Constitution

In 1946 Ambedkar became a member of the Constituent Assembly, and on **29 August 1947** he was elected **chairman of the Drafting Committee**. His role was central in shaping provisions such as the fundamental rights, the abolition of untouchability (Article 17), equality, and reservation for Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes. In his final speech in the Constituent Assembly on 25 November 1949, he warned that political democracy would last only if social and economic democracy came as well. (For details see — 'The Making of the Indian Constitution'.)

He became independent India's first **law minister** in Nehru's cabinet. When the **Hindu Code Bill**, which gave women equality in property, marriage and divorce, got stalled, he resigned in September 1951.

## Buddhist Dhamma and Legacy

On **14 October 1956** at **Nagpur**, Ambedkar took initiation into Buddhism along with his wife Savita Ambedkar and about half a million followers; this place is today called **Deekshabhoomi**. Only a few weeks later, on **6 December 1956**, he died in Delhi. His last book, 'The Buddha and His Dhamma', was published posthumously.

Many of Ambedkar's views were controversial in his time — his sharp disagreement with Gandhiji on the caste question, his criticism of the Congress, and his joining the British government during the war drew objections from nationalist circles, while his supporters saw this as independent politics for the interests of the Dalits. In 1990 he was posthumously awarded the **Bharat Ratna**. Today 'Babasaheb' is a source of inspiration for movements for social justice and equality not only in India but across the world.
