## Background: Rural India After the War

After the Second World War and the Bengal famine of 1943, discontent in the villages was at its peak. The All India Kisan Sabha and the Communist Party of India (CPI) organised the peasants. Two movements in particular grew large — the **Tebhaga** of Bengal and the **Telangana** struggle of Hyderabad State.

## Tebhaga: Two-Thirds of the Crop

In Bengal a great deal of land was cultivated by **sharecroppers** (bargadars), who gave half of the produce to the **jotedar**. In 1940 the Floud Commission recommended raising the sharecropper's share to two-thirds, but it was not implemented.

In **September 1946** the Bengal Provincial Kisan Sabha called on sharecroppers to give only one-third from now on and to keep the crop not in the jotedar's threshing floor but in their own. The movement gathered force from the paddy harvest of November 1946, especially in the districts of **Dinajpur, Rangpur, Jalpaiguri** and **Mymensingh** in North Bengal. Hindu, Muslim and tribal (Santhal, Rajbanshi) peasants came together, and women also played a big role. **Ila Mitra** and **Kansari Halder** were among its main leaders.

The Muslim League government led by Suhrawardy published a bill on the sharecroppers' share in early 1947, but it could not become law. Amid police repression, arrests and then the turmoil of Partition, the movement cooled in 1947. Its demands later echoed in West Bengal's Bargadar Act of 1950 and in 'Operation Barga' after 1977.

## Telangana: Against the Deshmukhs

In the Telugu-speaking Telangana region of the Nizam's Hyderabad State, land was in the hands of large **deshmukhs** and **jagirdars**. **Vetti** (forced labour), debt bondage and arbitrary taxes were common. The Andhra Mahasabha and the Communists organised the peasants on these very issues; the clash of **Chakali Ilamma**, of the washerman community, with a deshmukh over her land became its symbol.

On **4 July 1946**, in Kadavendi village in Warangal district, the deshmukh's men fired on a procession of peasants, in which the local leader **Doddi Komaraiah** was killed. After this the movement spread to Warangal, Nalgonda and Khammam.

## Razakars, the Indian Army and the Armed Struggle

In 1947–48 the paramilitary **Razakars** of the pro-Nizam **Qasim Razvi** committed heavy atrocities in the villages, and from September 1947 the Communists answered by forming armed squads. At its peak, peasant committees are said to have ruled about 3,000 to 4,000 villages, where forced labour was abolished and land was distributed; estimates of the land distributed go up to about a million acres.

In September 1948 Hyderabad was merged into India through the Indian Army's action (Operation Polo). After this, one section of the Communist Party favoured continuing the armed struggle against the Government of India as well. In the army and police campaign there were allegations of arrests, village burnings and extrajudicial killings; by one estimate, by August 1949 about 2,000 peasants were killed and 25,000 arrested. On the other side, the rebels too were accused of killing landlords, their agents and people regarded as 'informers'. There was sharp disagreement within the party over this, and in **October 1951** the CPI formally withdrew the struggle.

## Legacy and Assessment

Neither movement fully succeeded in its immediate aims, but they created an atmosphere against landlordism and forced labour and sharpened the debate on land reform and abolition of zamindari in independent India. Supporters count them among India's biggest peasant revolts; critics question the violence and the decision to take up arms against democratic India after 1948. It was after the Telangana struggle that Acharya Vinoba Bhave started the Bhoodan movement in 1951 from this very region.
