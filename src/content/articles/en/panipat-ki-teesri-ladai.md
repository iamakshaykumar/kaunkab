## Background: Who Dominated North India?

By the middle of the 18th century the Mughal emperors had become rulers in name only. The **Marathas**, who had come from the south, had become a decisive power in the politics of Delhi, while the Afghan ruler **Ahmad Shah Abdali** (Durrani) from the north-west was repeatedly attacking Punjab and Delhi. In 1758 the Maratha army under Raghunathrao reached Attock in Punjab, which Abdali regarded as direct interference in his territory.

In January 1760 the Maratha chief **Dattaji Shinde** was killed fighting Abdali's army at Burari Ghat near Delhi. After this Peshwa Balaji Bajirao sent his cousin **Sadashivrao Bhau** north with a large army. With him was the Peshwa's young son **Vishwasrao** too.

## The Campaign: From Delhi to Kunjpura

Bhau's army took Delhi in August 1760. In October 1760 the Marathas attacked and captured the Afghan outpost of **Kunjpura** on the bank of the Yamuna, and took possession of the grain and supplies there. But in the meantime Abdali crossed the Yamuna near Baghpat and came between the Marathas and Delhi.

On the political front too the Marathas were left alone. The Rohilla chief **Najib-ud-Daula** was already Abdali's ally, and the Nawab of Awadh, **Shuja-ud-Daula**, also eventually took Abdali's side. The Jat king of Bharatpur, **Suraj Mal**, separated from the Maratha camp because of differences.

## The Siege at Panipat

From November 1760 the two armies remained encamped facing each other near Panipat. For about two months small skirmishes went on. Abdali's cavalry detachments cut the supply routes to the Maratha camp. In the Maratha camp, besides soldiers, there were thousands of non-combatants — pilgrims, traders, families. By January there was a severe shortage of food and fodder, and Bhau decided on open battle to break the siege.

## 14 January 1761: The Decisive Day

In the morning the Maratha army advanced. The infantry trained in the European manner and the artillery of **Ibrahim Khan Gardi** at first inflicted heavy losses on the Rohilla front, and until afternoon the battle looked evenly balanced. But Abdali brought fresh reserve troops and light cannon (zamburaks) mounted on camels onto the field.

In the afternoon Vishwasrao was hit by a bullet and died. Some time after this Sadashivrao Bhau also went missing in the midst of the battle; many stories were later told about his death. The Maratha line broke. Some chiefs, including Malharrao Holkar, left with their detachments, and the wounded **Mahadji Shinde** also somehow escaped. Ibrahim Khan Gardi, who was captured, was put to death.

Estimates of the number of dead differ widely. Contemporary and later accounts say that many tens of thousands of people were killed in the battle and in the massacre that followed, but no firm figure is available.

## Reasons for the Defeat

Historians list several reasons, which are emphasised differently:

- With supplies cut off during the two-month siege, the army had become hungry and weak.
- The Marathas did not get the support of the main rulers of North India — the Rohillas, Awadh and the Jats.
- The Maratha heavy artillery was slow, while the Afghan side had fast-moving camel guns and a large cavalry.
- There were differences among the Maratha chiefs over battle strategy — the traditional guerrilla style versus a standing battle.

## Result and Significance

Abdali, though he won the battle, did not stay in India. In March 1761 he issued a decree recognising Shah Alam II as emperor and went back to Afghanistan. For the Marathas it was a deep blow, but not the end. In the time of the young Peshwa **Madhavrao I** Maratha power was reorganised, and about ten years later, through the efforts of Mahadji Shinde, Shah Alam returned to Delhi under Maratha protection.

Many historians believe that the power vacuum created in North India after Panipat benefited the English East India Company, which was at that very time consolidating its hold in Bengal. In the Marathi language, even today the phrase "Panipat hone" is used for a heavy loss.
