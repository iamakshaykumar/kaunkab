## The Morning of Republic Day

On **26 January 2001** the country was celebrating Republic Day. At **8:46 in the morning** the ground in **Kutch** district of Gujarat suddenly shook violently. The earthquake lasted about two minutes. The US Geological Survey (USGS) put its magnitude at **7.7**, while some other institutions gave it as 7.6. Its epicentre lay near Chobari village in **Bhachau** taluka, about 17 kilometres below the surface.

Kutch is counted among India's most earthquake-prone regions. A major earthquake had struck here in 1819 as well. Even so, the 2001 earthquake was so powerful that its tremors were felt in Delhi, Mumbai, Kolkata and in neighbouring Pakistan and Nepal. It occurred not at a plate boundary but on a hidden fault inside the Indian plate, which made it a major subject of study for scientists too.

## Towns Turned to Rubble

The worst devastation was in the towns of Kutch. In **Bhuj** about 10,000 people died, and most of the town's buildings collapsed or became uninhabitable. **Bhachau** was almost completely destroyed, and more than 5,000 lives were lost there. More than 2,000 people died in **Anjar** and about 2,000 in **Gandhidham**.

The tragedy at Anjar was especially heartbreaking. Schoolchildren taking part in the Republic Day parade were passing through a narrow lane when old buildings on both sides fell on them. About 185 children and 20 teachers were killed.

In **Ahmedabad**, about 300 kilometres away, several tall residential buildings also collapsed, and more than 750 people died there. This showed how weakly building regulations were followed in the cities.

## The Scale of the Damage

According to the Gujarat government's figures, more than about **13,800** people died, while some international estimates put the toll at about **20,000**. About **1.67 lakh** people were injured. More than 12 lakh houses in about 8,000 villages and hundreds of towns were damaged or destroyed. Total economic losses were estimated at about 10 billion US dollars.

Immediately after the earthquake the **Indian Army**, the Air Force, local voluntary organisations and rescue teams from India and abroad set to work pulling people out of the rubble. Many countries sent relief material, field hospitals and rescue teams. The **World Bank** and the **Asian Development Bank** gave large loans for reconstruction.

## A New Way of Rebuilding

After relief, the biggest challenge was to give lakhs of families homes again. For this the Gujarat government set up the **Gujarat State Disaster Management Authority (GSDMA)** in February 2001, which was given charge of rehabilitation and reconstruction.

Reconstruction followed an **"owner-driven"** approach. Instead of having contractors build ready-made houses, the government gave families grants in instalments, and the families built their own homes. At every stage engineers checked whether the house was being built with earthquake-resistant techniques. Villagers were free to build homes in their own style and according to their needs. Within two years more than one lakh houses had been rebuilt in rural Kutch.

New plans were drawn up for old towns such as Bhuj, including wider roads, open spaces and safe building rules. The government also encouraged investment in Kutch through tax concessions for industry, and the region later industrialised rapidly. The **GSDMA** received the United Nations **Sasakawa Award** in 2003 for its work.

## Lessons and Legacy

The Kutch earthquake played a major role in changing India's disaster policy. Earlier the emphasis after a disaster was only on distributing relief; now attention turned to preparedness, risk reduction and safe construction. In **2003** Gujarat enacted its own state disaster management law, among the first laws of its kind in the country. The Centre then passed the **Disaster Management Act 2005**, under which the **National Disaster Management Authority (NDMA)** was created.

**Smritivan**, built on **Bhujia Hill** in Bhuj, today keeps the memory of this tragedy alive. Spread over about 470 acres, the memorial bears the names of the dead and also houses a museum on earthquake science and disaster preparedness. Prime Minister **Narendra Modi** inaugurated it on **28 August 2022**. The story of Kutch rising again from the rubble shows that with sound planning and people's participation, life can be rebuilt even after the greatest of disasters.
