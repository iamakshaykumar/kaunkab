## Background

In the 18th and 19th centuries, the **East India Company** gradually took over a large part of India. Peasants were crushed by heavy land revenue, many princely states had been annexed, and traditional crafts and trade were being ruined. The Indian sepoys in the Company's army also had deep grievances about pay, promotion and religious feelings.

## The Spark

The cartridges of the new **Enfield rifle** had to be bitten with the teeth, and a rumour spread that they were greased with cow and pig fat. This angered both Hindu and Muslim sepoys.

On 29 March 1857, the sepoy **Mangal Pandey** attacked British officers at Barrackpore (Bengal); he was hanged. Then on **10 May 1857**, the sepoys at **Meerut** rose in open revolt and marched towards Delhi.

## Spread of the Revolt

On reaching Delhi, the sepoys declared the aged Mughal emperor **Bahadur Shah Zafar** their leader. Soon the revolt spread to many centres:

- **Jhansi** — Rani Lakshmibai
- **Kanpur** — Nana Sahib and Tatya Tope
- **Lucknow** — Begum Hazrat Mahal
- **Bihar (Jagdishpur)** — Kunwar Singh

Along with the sepoys, peasants, artisans and zamindars also joined in.

## Suppression

The British crushed the revolt with a large army and harsh repression. In September 1857, Delhi was again captured by the British. Bahadur Shah Zafar was arrested and, after a trial, exiled to Rangoon (Myanmar) in 1858. **Rani Lakshmibai** died fighting near Gwalior in June 1858.

## Results

- In 1858, **East India Company rule came to an end** and India came directly under the British Crown.
- The British made major changes in the army and in their policies.

## Significance

The Revolt of 1857 failed, but it inspired the generations that followed. In 1909, Vinayak Damodar Savarkar called it **"India's First War of Independence"**. In the next article of this series, read about Jallianwala Bagh.
