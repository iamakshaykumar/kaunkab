## Background of the Border

A large part of the roughly 3,488-kilometre border between India and China is still not settled. The line up to which the armies of the two countries exercise control is called the **Line of Actual Control (LAC)**, but at many places the two sides see its position differently. After the **1962 war** this border remained relatively calm for decades. In the agreements of **1993** and **1996** the two countries set confidence-building measures such as maintaining peace near the LAC and not opening fire on each other. Since **1975** no soldier had died on the border from a bullet.

The **Galwan Valley** lies in eastern Ladakh near Aksai Chin, where the Galwan river meets the Shyok river. Close by runs India's **Darbuk–Shyok–Daulat Beg Oldi (DS-DBO)** road, which reaches Daulat Beg Oldi, one of the highest airstrips in the world. According to analysts, China's concern over India's growing infrastructure in this area was one cause of the tension.

## The Start of the Standoff

In **April–May 2020**, while the world was struggling with the COVID-19 pandemic, China's **People's Liberation Army** massed large numbers of troops and heavy weapons near the LAC in eastern Ladakh. On **5–6 May 2020** there was a scuffle and stone-throwing between the two armies on the northern bank of **Pangong Tso** lake, in which several soldiers on both sides were injured. After this the two armies dug in face to face at several places, such as Galwan, **Hot Springs**, **Gogra** and the **Depsang** plains. India said that Chinese soldiers had come into areas where Indian soldiers used to patrol earlier; China said India was building in its territory. At a meeting of the corps commanders of the two countries on 6 June, agreement was reached on pulling the armies back.

## The Night of 15 June

To check compliance with this agreement, on **15 June 2020** the commanding officer of the **16 Bihar** battalion, **Colonel B. Santosh Babu**, reached the area near **Patrolling Point 14** in the Galwan Valley with his soldiers. A dispute arose there over removing a Chinese post, which by evening turned into a violent clash. Because of the agreements, no shots were fired from either side, but fighting went on for several hours, in darkness, with iron rods, clubs fitted with nails and barbed wire, and stones. Many soldiers fell from high cliffs or were swept away in the icy Galwan river.

According to the Indian Army, **20 Indian soldiers including Colonel Santosh Babu** were killed in this clash. China did not disclose its losses for a long time; in **February 2021** it officially acknowledged the deaths of **4 of its soldiers** and honoured them posthumously. Indian officials and some independent analyses put the number of Chinese casualties much higher, but this could not be independently confirmed. The two countries blamed each other for the clash — India said China had violated the understanding and tried to change the status quo, while China accused Indian soldiers of attacking first.

## The Country's Response and the Long Standoff

There was deep anger in the country after the clash. Prime Minister **Narendra Modi** said that the soldiers' sacrifice would not go in vain, and on 3 July he himself reached **Nimu** in Ladakh. On **29 June 2020**, citing security reasons, the government banned **59 Chinese apps including TikTok**; by the end of the year this number had risen to more than 200. Rules for scrutinising Chinese investment were also tightened. The opposition asked the government whether China had occupied Indian territory, and sought clarity on allegations of lost access to patrolling points.

The two countries deployed roughly 50,000–60,000 soldiers each, along with tanks and fighter aircraft, on the border, and neither abandoned its positions even in the bitter winters. On **29–30 August 2020** Indian soldiers took up positions on the heights of the **Kailash Range** south of Pangong Tso; in September, warning shots were fired there for the first time in 45 years.

## Talks and the Withdrawal of Forces

After many rounds of corps commander-level meetings and talks between the foreign ministers, the armies pulled back from both banks of Pangong Tso in **February 2021**, from Gogra in **August 2021** and from Hot Springs (Patrolling Point 15) in **September 2022**. At several places temporary **"buffer zones"** were created between the two, where neither side patrols. The hardest issues were those of **Depsang** and **Demchok**. On **21 October 2024** Foreign Secretary **Vikram Misri** announced that agreement had been reached on patrolling arrangements in these areas; after this the armies withdrew from there and verification patrols began. In December 2024 External Affairs Minister **S. Jaishankar** told Parliament that disengagement in eastern Ladakh had been completed, although the work of de-escalation and reducing troop numbers remained.

## Significance

The Galwan clash brought India–China relations to their lowest point in decades. After it, India speeded up the building of roads, tunnels and bridges on the border and permanently deployed more troops on the LAC. On the economic front Chinese apps and investment were restricted, although trade between the two countries kept growing. In memory of the Galwan martyrs, a war memorial was built in October 2020 near the **"KM-120"** post on the DS-DBO road. The event is a reminder of how quickly even a small dispute on an unsettled border can turn into a deadly conflict.
