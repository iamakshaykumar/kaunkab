## The Age of the Shramanas

In the sixth and fifth centuries BCE, many **shramana** sects (those who left home for austerity and contemplation) arose in the Ganges valley. Buddhism and Jainism were among them, but at that time they had many rivals. Of these the **Ajivika** sect was the most influential. Among the six famous "tirthankaras" or teachers of the Buddha's time mentioned in the Buddhist text *Samannaphala Sutta* is **Makkhali Gosala**, the most famous teacher of the Ajivikas.

A great difficulty is that no text of the Ajivikas' own has survived. What we know about them comes mainly from **Buddhist and Jain texts**, which were hostile to them. Many scholars therefore believe that the picture of their doctrines may be somewhat distorted or exaggerated.

## Makkhali Gosala

**Makkhali Gosala** is generally regarded as the founder of the Ajivika sect, although according to some sources he was the greatest leader of an already existing tradition. "Gosala" means "cowshed"; the Jain *Bhagavati Sutra* and the Buddhist commentator Buddhaghosa say that he was born in a cowshed. The historian **A. L. Basham** holds that such stories were probably invented by opponents to belittle him.

According to the *Bhagavati Sutra*, Gosala joined Mahavira in the third year of his austerities and stayed with him for about **six years**. Later the two differed and parted. Jain tradition also describes a bitter dispute between the two afterwards. Basham, on the basis of the Sri Lankan text *Mahavamsa*, took Gosala's death to be about **484 BCE**, but this date too is an estimate.

## The Doctrine of Niyati

The central idea of the Ajivikas was **niyati** — that is, the destiny of every being is completely fixed in advance. Human effort, action or will cannot change it. Modern scholars sum up the essence of this view roughly thus — man is powerless and his happiness and sorrow are predetermined. The Ajivikas held that every soul must pass through a fixed cycle of countless births, after which liberation comes of itself.

This is why the Buddhists and Jains, who stressed **karma** and individual effort, were sharp critics of the Ajivikas. Even so, the Ajivikas practised severe austerities and adopted practices such as going naked. One of their major centres was **Shravasti**.

## Mauryan Patronage and the Barabar Caves

In the Mauryan period the Ajivikas received royal patronage. According to the Buddhist text *Ashokavadana*, the emperor **Bindusara** and his queen were followers of the Ajivikas. Ashoka himself, though a Buddhist, respected all sects.

In **Jehanabad district** of Bihar, about 24 kilometres north of Gaya, four caves were cut into the hard granite rock of the **Barabar hill** — **Karan Chaupar, Lomas Rishi, Sudama** and **Vishvakarma**. They are among the oldest surviving rock-cut caves in India. According to Ashoka's inscriptions, in the **12th** and **19th** years after his coronation he donated some of these caves to the Ajivikas. The inscription in the Sudama cave says that King Priyadarshi gave this cave to the Ajivikas twelve years after his coronation.

On the nearby **Nagarjuni hill** three more caves — **Gopika, Vadathika** and **Vapiyaka** — were donated to the Ajivikas by Ashoka's grandson **Dasharatha Maurya** around 230 BCE. The inner walls of these caves are polished so smooth that they have a mirror-like shine, known as the **"Mauryan polish"**. Sound echoes loudly in them.

## Decline

After the Mauryan Empire, mentions of the Ajivikas gradually became fewer. By the Gupta period they had grown quite weak. Even so, they survived for a long time in South India — some inscriptions there mention a tax levied on the Ajivikas, the last of which is of the fourteenth century CE. After this the sect vanished completely from history, and today its memory survives mainly in the gleaming caves of Barabar and in the texts of its opponents.
