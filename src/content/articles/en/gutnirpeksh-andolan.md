## The Cold War and New Nations

After the Second World War the world began to divide into two blocs — the Western bloc led by the United States (such as NATO) and the communist bloc led by the Soviet Union. At the same time dozens of countries in Asia and Africa were being freed from colonial rule. Prime Minister **Jawaharlal Nehru** believed that newly independent countries should take independent decisions on every issue, according to their own interest and judgement, without joining any military bloc. The credit for popularising the term 'non-alignment' is often given to the Indian diplomat **V. K. Krishna Menon** and Nehru.

## Panchsheel and Bandung (1954–1955)

In 1954 India and China adopted **Panchsheel** — five principles — for their mutual relations: respect for each other's sovereignty and territorial integrity, non-aggression, non-interference in internal affairs, equality and mutual benefit, and peaceful co-existence.

The Asian-African Conference was held at **Bandung** in Indonesia on **18–24 April 1955**, hosted by President **Sukarno**. The sponsoring countries were Indonesia, Burma, Sri Lanka, India and Pakistan, and delegates from 29 countries took part, representing at that time more than half the world's population. The conference adopted a ten-point declaration on world peace and cooperation, which included the principles of the UN Charter and Panchsheel. Not all countries that took part in Bandung later remained non-aligned — some, like Pakistan, were tied to Western military pacts.

## The Brioni Meeting (1956)

On 19 July 1956 on the island of **Brioni** in Yugoslavia, Yugoslavia's President **Tito**, Egypt's President **Nasser** and Nehru signed a joint declaration. The three leaders came from different backgrounds — Tito's Yugoslavia, though communist, had broken away from the Soviet bloc, Nasser was a symbol of Arab nationalism, and Nehru was the architect of India's independent foreign policy. The Brioni declaration is considered an important step towards the organised form of the Non-Aligned Movement.

## The Belgrade Summit (1961)

From **1 to 6 September 1961** the first summit of heads of state and government of non-aligned countries was held in the Yugoslav capital **Belgrade**, in which about 25 countries took part as full members. Among its promoters Tito, Nasser, Nehru, **Kwame Nkrumah** of Ghana and Sukarno of Indonesia were prominent. The conference stressed the end of colonialism, disarmament and reducing tension between the superpowers. The formal start of the Non-Aligned Movement is regarded as dating from this conference.

## Expansion and Criticism

Later the movement became the largest forum of developing countries. Its seventh summit was held in New Delhi in March 1983. Today it has 120 members, 18 observer countries and 10 international organisations.

Critics pointed to several weaknesses. Wars among member countries — such as India–Pakistan and Iran–Iraq — kept occurring; the movement split over the Soviet intervention in Afghanistan in 1979; and it was accused of making only declarations rather than taking concrete action. In India too, after the China war of 1962 there was debate on the practicality of the non-alignment policy, and some regarded the Indo-Soviet Treaty of 1971 as a deviation from this policy, while the government described it as consistent with non-alignment.

## Significance

The Non-Aligned Movement gave newly independent countries an independent voice amid the pulls of the Cold War and carried the demands for anti-colonialism, anti-apartheid and a just world order to international forums. After the end of the Cold War questions were raised about its relevance, but the idea of 'strategic autonomy' is still present in discussions of Indian foreign policy today.
