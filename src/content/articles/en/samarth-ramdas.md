## Narayan of Jamb

Samarth Ramdas's original name was **Narayan Suryajipant Thosar**. He was born around **1608** in the village of **Jamb**, in today's Jalna district, in a Deshastha Brahmin family; according to tradition it was the day of Ramnavami. From childhood he is said to have been a devotee of Rama and Hanuman.

The most famous story concerns his wedding: at the age of twelve, when the priest in the wedding pavilion said "Shubhamangal Savdhan", Narayan heard the word "savdhan" (beware) and ran away from the pavilion. The story comes down through tradition, but it is clear that he chose the path of renunciation rather than household life.

## Spiritual Practice and Travels

According to tradition, Narayan practised severe austerities for about twelve years at **Takali near Nashik**, on the bank of the Godavari, and it was here that he came to be called "Ramdas". After this he travelled across India for about twelve years. His works *Asmani Sultani* and *Parchakra Nirupan* give a moving description of people suffering from famine, war and foreign invasions, which is believed to come from the experiences of these travels.

In the 1640s he returned to the Krishna valley and established a Rama temple at **Chaphal**. He built eleven Hanuman temples and set up **maths** (monasteries) in many places, where disciples learned exercise and discipline along with devotion. Among his disciples were women such as **Venabai** and Akkabai, whom he entrusted with the responsibility of the maths.

## Dasbodh

Ramdas's greatest work is the **Dasbodh**, a text written in the form of a dialogue between teacher and disciple, with **twenty dashaks**, ten **samasas** in each dashak, and about **7,751** ovis in all. It is believed that he dictated a large part of it to his disciple **Kalyan Swami** in a cave called **Shivthar Ghal** in Raigad district. Ramdas kept adding to it throughout his life.

The Dasbodh is not merely a spiritual text. Along with devotion, knowledge and detachment, it also explains how to become a good writer, a good leader or a sensible householder, why honest earning is necessary, and that it is not right to sit back relying only on God; one must also make effort. His famous idea is that the one who makes effort is the one who moves ahead.

## Manache Shlok and Other Works

The **Manache Shlok** (verses to the mind) are about 205 short poems in which Ramdas addresses his own "mind" and teaches good conduct, patience and devotion to Rama. Because of their simple language and rhythm, children have long been made to memorise them. His *Karunashtak*, *Maruti Stotra* and the famous aarti of Ganesh, "Sukhkarta Dukhharta", are also believed to be his, and it is sung everywhere today during Ganeshotsav.

## Relationship with Shivaji: A Debate

In popular stories Samarth Ramdas is described as the guru of **Shivaji**, but this subject is disputed among historians. The Bakhar of Hanumant Swami of the Ramdasi tradition places their first meeting in **1649**, for which no solid contemporary evidence is found. The historian G. H. Khare proposed a meeting at Shivthar Ghal in 1652, while writers such as Babasaheb Purandare and Setumadhavrao Pagdi place the first meeting around **1672**. Some scholars doubt any direct guru-disciple role at all, and many historians regard Shivaji's state-building as the result of his own political vision.

What is considered established is that in the latter part of his life Shivaji gave donations and grants to Ramdas's maths and respected him. A letter of advice written in Ramdas's name to **Sambhaji** after Shivaji's death is also well known, although its authenticity is also debated.

## Final Years and Legacy

Ramdas spent his last years at the fort of **Sajjangad** near Satara, where he died around **1681** (in early 1682 according to some calculations). His samadhi is still there. The **Ramdasi sect** he founded and the tradition of the Dasbodh and Manache Shlok are part of the religious and cultural life of Maharashtra. In the nineteenth and twentieth centuries, several nationalist leaders also drew inspiration from his message of "strength and organisation".
