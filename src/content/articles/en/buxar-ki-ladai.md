## Background: Bengal After Plassey

After the victory at Plassey in 1757, the East India Company made Mir Jafar the Nawab of Bengal, but soon replaced him on the throne with his son-in-law **Mir Qasim**. Mir Qasim did not want to remain a puppet. He shifted his capital from Murshidabad to Munger, tried to modernise the army and reformed the administration.

The biggest dispute was over **dastaks** (permits for duty-free trade). Company officials misused these permits for private trade, which harmed Indian merchants and reduced the Nawab's revenue. Fed up, Mir Qasim abolished internal customs duties for all merchants. The Company could not tolerate this, and war broke out in 1763. After losing several battles, Mir Qasim moved towards Awadh.

## The Alliance of Three Powers

Reaching Awadh, Mir Qasim formed a joint front with the Nawab of Awadh, **Shuja-ud-Daula**, and the Mughal emperor **Shah Alam II**. Shah Alam was at that time far from Delhi and was looking for allies to re-establish his authority. The aim of this alliance was to drive the British out of Bengal.

On paper this alliance was very powerful — its combined army is said to have been more than 40,000. But the interests of the three were different and there was a great lack of coordination among them.

## 22-23 October 1764: The Battlefield of Buxar

The two armies faced each other near Buxar (today in Bihar) on the banks of the Ganga. The Company's army was led by **Major Hector Munro**. His army of about 7,000 included about 1,800 European soldiers, and the rest were Indian sepoys.

- Despite being far larger in number, the combined army had no unified command.
- The Company's army was disciplined and its artillery was used effectively.
- After a few hours of hard fighting, the combined army fell back. The British captured more than 130 guns.

Unlike Plassey, Buxar was a real military contest in which the Company defeated three major powers of North India at once. After the battle Shah Alam soon came to terms with the British, Shuja-ud-Daula went on fighting for some time, and Mir Qasim faded into obscurity.

## 1765: The Treaty of Allahabad and the Diwani

In 1765 Robert Clive returned to India as Governor of Bengal for the second time. In August 1765 two agreements were made at Allahabad:

1. **With Shah Alam II** — The emperor handed over to the Company the **Diwani** of Bengal, Bihar and Orissa, that is, the right to collect land revenue and to administer civil justice. Most sources give the date of this farman as 12 August 1765. In return the Company promised to pay the emperor 2.6 million rupees a year and to hand over the districts of Kara and Allahabad.
2. **With Shuja-ud-Daula (16 August 1765)** — Awadh was returned to him, but he had to pay 5 million rupees as war indemnity and make a military treaty with the Company that gradually made Awadh dependent on the Company.

## Historical Significance

After the Diwani was obtained, a system of **Dual Government** was established in Bengal — revenue was in the hands of the Company, while responsibility for administration and law and order lay with the nominal Nawab. Authority lay with the Company, but accountability with no one. The burden of this fell on peasants and artisans, and the terrible Bengal famine of 1770 exposed the cruelty of this system.

Historians believe that Plassey gave the Company a foothold in Bengal, but **Buxar made it the undisputed military power of North India**. With the rich revenues of Bengal, the Company could now run its army and trade without bringing money from England — from here the journey of a trading company towards becoming an empire decisively began.
