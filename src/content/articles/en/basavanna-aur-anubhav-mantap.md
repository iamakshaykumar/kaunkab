## From Bagewadi to Kalyana

**Basavanna** (Basaveshwara or Basava) was a saint, poet and social reformer of 12th-century Karnataka. Scholars do not agree on the dates of his life — many sources say he was born around 1131 CE, while for his death different dates such as 1167 and 1196 are found. He is believed to have been born in **Basavana Bagewadi** in present-day Vijayapura district, in a Shaiva Brahmin family.

According to tradition, in his youth he went to **Kudalasangama**, where he studied and practised devotion to Shiva. Later he reached **Kalyana** (present-day Basavakalyan, Bidar district), which was then the capital of the **Kalachuri king Bijjala**. There he began as an accountant at the court and rose to a high ministerial post.

## The Anubhava Mantapa: An Assembly Open to All

At Kalyana a large group of Shiva devotees — called **Sharanas** — gathered around Basavanna. Their discussion assembly is called the **Anubhava Mantapa**, that is, the 'hall of experience'. According to tradition it was presided over by the mystic saint **Allama Prabhu**, and along with devotees such as **Akka Mahadevi** and **Channabasavanna** it also included working people such as carpenters, cobblers, washermen and boatmen, and many women.

Our descriptions of this assembly come mostly from later texts, so its exact form is a matter of debate among historians. Even so, this much is clear: it was a forum for open conversation on spiritual and social questions, where devotion and conduct were valued more than birth.

## Vachanas: Poetry in the People's Language

The Sharanas put their ideas not in Sanskrit but in spoken **Kannada**, in short, prose-like verses called **vachanas**. Basavanna's vachanas usually end with the name 'Kudalasangamadeva'. In one famous vachana he says that the rich build temples for Shiva, but what can the poor do — his body is the temple, his legs the pillars and his head the golden pinnacle. (This vachana became very famous through A. K. Ramanujan's English translation, 'Speaking of Siva'.)

Note that the vachana collections available today were compiled long after Basavanna's time, so there is sometimes doubt about the authorship of a particular vachana.

## Kayaka, Dasoha and Ishtalinga

Some main threads of Basavanna's thought are considered to be these:
- **Kayaka** — every kind of honest labour is sacred; 'Kayakave Kailasa', that is, work itself is Kailasa.
- **Dasoha** — sharing the wealth earned by labour with society and with devotees.
- **Ishtalinga** — a small Shiva linga worn around the neck, which any devotee can wear without distinction of caste or sex. It reduced dependence on temples and priests.

## The Crisis at Kalyana

According to tradition, a major dispute arose at Kalyana from the marriage of the daughter of a Brahmin family and the son of a cobbler family — both Sharanas. The stories tell that King Bijjala, treating this as an attack on the social order, gave a harsh punishment, after which violence spread in Kalyana and the Sharanas were scattered. The account of this event is based on later texts and its details are not certain. Basavanna is believed to have left Kalyana and returned to Kudalasangama, where he died.

## Legacy

The identity of the **Lingayat** (Virashaiva) community is linked with the tradition of Basavanna and the Sharanas, and today it makes up a large part of Karnataka's population. Vachana literature is counted among the most original heritages of the Kannada language. Because of his emphasis on the dignity of labour, the spiritual equality of women and men and opposition to caste distinctions, Basavanna is still remembered as a far-sighted social reformer.
