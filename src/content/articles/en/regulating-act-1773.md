## Background: A Trading Company in the Role of a Ruler

After the victories of Plassey in 1757 and Buxar in 1764, the East India Company received in 1765 the **diwani** of Bengal, Bihar and Orissa — the right to collect revenue. A trading company was now ruling millions of people, but its structure had no arrangement for this.

The Company's employees were amassing huge fortunes through private trade and gifts, while the Company itself was sunk in debt. The terrible famine of Bengal in 1770 exposed this misrule. By 1772 the Company could not pay the sum due to the British government and had to ask the Bank of England and the government for loans. In Britain the demand arose that Parliament should control the Company's affairs in India.

## Main Provisions of the Act

In 1773 the government of Prime Minister **Lord North** had this Act passed along with giving the Company a heavy government loan. Its main provisions were:

- The governor of Bengal was made the **'Governor-General of Fort William (Bengal)'**. The name of **Warren Hastings** as the first Governor-General was written in the Act itself.
- A **council of four members** was formed to assist the Governor-General, in which decisions were to be made by majority. The first members were Philip Francis, General John Clavering, George Monson and Richard Barwell.
- The **Madras and Bombay** presidencies were placed under Bengal in matters of war, peace and treaties.
- Provision was made for establishing a **Supreme Court** at Calcutta.
- **Private trade** by the Company's employees and accepting **gifts or bribes** from Indians were banned.
- The Company's directors had to give the British government information on India's revenue, civil and military affairs, and the directors' term was made four years.

## The Supreme Court at Calcutta (1774)

Under the Act, the **Supreme Court at Fort William** (Supreme Court of Judicature) was formed in 1774, replacing the old Mayor's Court of Calcutta. **Sir Elijah Impey** became its first Chief Justice, and Robert Chambers, Stephen Caesar Le Maistre and John Hyde were the other judges. It began work in October 1774.

The jurisdiction of this court was not clear — it clashed repeatedly with the council over whom it could try and under what laws. In 1775 this court sentenced **Maharaja Nandakumar** of Bengal to death by hanging on a charge of forgery. Nandakumar had made charges of bribery against Hastings, so this trial is still considered controversial.

## Shortcomings

The Act created new institutions of government, but a balance between them could not be struck:

- The Governor-General had no **veto**. Three members of the council — Francis, Clavering and Monson — often joined together and put Hastings in a minority.
- Bengal's control over Madras and Bombay was weak; the Bombay government on its own made a treaty with Raghunathrao and started the First Anglo-Maratha War.
- The powers of the Supreme Court and the council kept clashing. An amending Act of 1781 tried to settle some of these disputes.

## Pitt's India Act (1784) and Significance

To remove these shortcomings, in **1784**, in the time of Prime Minister **William Pitt the Younger**, the East India Company Act — called **Pitt's India Act** — was passed. It created a government-appointed **Board of Control** in London, which supervised the Company's political, military and revenue affairs. Trade remained with the Company's directors — this arrangement was called **'dual control'**. The Governor-General's council was reduced to three members, so that he would not easily be put in a minority.

The Regulating Act was the **first step in the control of the British Parliament** over the Company's rule in India. The post of Governor-General created by it later became the post of Governor-General of all India and, after 1858, of Viceroy. That is why discussion of India's constitutional history generally begins with this Act.
