## Background: The Company and the Tangle of Land Revenue

After receiving the **diwani** (the right to collect revenue) of Bengal, Bihar and Orissa in 1765, the biggest question before the East India Company was — how, and how much, revenue should be collected from the land? In the early years the Company had revenue collected on contract, holding auctions every few years. This made collection uncertain, both peasants and zamindars suffered, and the terrible famine of 1770 made things worse.

The Company wanted its income to be regular and certain. In London the Company's directors suggested a permanent arrangement in 1786.

## Cornwallis and the Decision of 1793

A long debate on this took place between **Lord Cornwallis**, who came as Governor-General in 1786, and the revenue expert **John Shore**. Shore first wanted to make a settlement for a short time to gain experience, while Cornwallis favoured fixing the revenue forever. As a compromise, a ten-year (decennial) settlement was made in 1790, and on **22 March 1793** it was declared **permanent**.

This arrangement was introduced in Bengal, Bihar and Orissa; later it was extended to the Banaras region and the northern parts of Madras.

## Main Features of the System

- **Zamindars** were recognised as the owners of the land and were given the responsibility of paying the revenue to the government.
- The amount of revenue was fixed forever — it could not be raised in future even if production or prices rose.
- It is generally believed that about **10/11** (nearly 89 per cent) of the rent collected went to the government and 1/11 was kept by the zamindar.
- If revenue was not paid by sunset on the fixed date, the zamindari was **auctioned** — this was called the **'Sunset Law'**.
- Zamindars were deprived of the rights to maintain private armies and to dispense justice.

## Results: Zamindars, Peasants and the Company

The Company hoped that, given ownership rights, zamindars would improve farming and become a loyal class. At the start the revenue demand was so high that many old zamindars could not pay the arrears and their zamindaris were auctioned. According to NCERT, even the lands of a large estate such as Burdwan were sold at auction. At the auctions the land was often bought by city merchants and moneylenders, who lived far from the village.

The peasants (ryots) became the weakest link in this system. Zamindars could collect arbitrary rent from them and their rights were not secure. Many levels of intermediaries also arose in between. In the long run the Company too suffered, because its revenue could not rise even when the produce and prices of land increased.

## Ryotwari and Mahalwari

After the experience of the Permanent Settlement, the British adopted other systems in the remaining regions:

- **Ryotwari system** — associated with **Thomas Munro** in the Madras Presidency (he became Governor of Madras in 1820); later also introduced in the Bombay Presidency. In it the government fixed the revenue directly with the peasant (ryot), and it was reassessed after some years.
- **Mahalwari system** — in the North-Western Provinces (today a large part of Uttar Pradesh) and Punjab. Its beginning is considered to be from the proposal of **Holt Mackenzie** in 1822. In it the whole village or 'mahal' was taken as the unit for fixing revenue.

## Historical Significance

The Permanent Settlement created a powerful zamindar class in Bengal and Bihar, whose hold on villages lasted for more than a hundred and fifty years. After independence, this system was ended in the 1950s by the zamindari abolition laws. The Permanent Settlement is an essential link in understanding the history of land reform and peasant movements in India.
