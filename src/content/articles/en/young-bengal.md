## A Young Teacher

**Henry Louis Vivian Derozio** was born on **18 April 1809** in Calcutta. His father Francis Derozio was a Christian of Indo-Portuguese origin and worked in a trading office. Derozio studied at David Drummond's Dharmatala Academy. Drummond was a Scottish teacher of free-thinking ideas, and he is believed to have influenced Derozio's rational thinking.

At the age of fourteen Derozio left school and worked for some time in an office and in an indigo factory of his relative at Bhagalpur. During this time his poems began to be published. In **May 1826**, at the age of only seventeen, he was appointed a teacher of English literature and history at the **Hindu College** of Calcutta (founded in 1817).

## Education Beyond the Classroom

Derozio was not much older than his students, and this became his strength. He did not confine himself to the textbook — he asked students to read about the European Enlightenment, the French Revolution, and thinkers such as Hume, Locke and Tom Paine, and to question everything. His stress was that nothing should be accepted merely because it is tradition.

In **1828**, with his encouragement, students formed a debating society called the **Academic Association**. In it there was open debate on subjects such as the existence of God, idol worship, caste, the condition of women, and freedom.

## Young Bengal: A Generation of Rebellion

Derozio's disciples were called the **'Derozians'** or **'Young Bengal'**. Among them were names such as **Ramgopal Ghosh**, **Ramtanu Lahiri**, **Dakshinaranjan Mukherjee**, **Krishna Mohan Banerjee**, **Peary Chand Mitra**, **Rasik Krishna Mullick** and **Radhanath Sikdar**.

These young men often went very far in their protest. Some publicly ate forbidden food, took off the sacred thread or mocked orthodox customs. This caused a sharp reaction in the traditionalist Hindu society of Calcutta. Critics called it blind imitation of the West and a cutting-off from culture, while supporters regarded it as the first great voice of intellectual freedom.

## Dismissal and Early Death

Guardians and the conservative managers of the college, angry at the changing behaviour of the students, accused Derozio of making the young 'anti-religious'. In **April 1831** he was removed from the Hindu College. He gave a written reply to the charges against him, but the decision was not changed. According to some historians, the demand for his dismissal included, along with Hindu managers such as Ramkamal Sen, Englishmen such as H. H. Wilson — opinions differ on this.

A few months later, on **26 December 1831**, Derozio died of cholera. He was only 22 years old. He was buried in the South Park Street Cemetery in Calcutta.

## Poet and an 'Indian' Identity

Derozio is usually counted as belonging to the Anglo-Indian community, but he called himself an Indian. His poetry collections *Poems* (1827) and *The Fakeer of Jungheera* (1828) show a deep attachment to India. In 'To India — My Native Land' he expresses sorrow over India's past glory and present subjection.

Many writers hold Derozio's influence responsible for Krishna Mohan Banerjee's later becoming a Christian, but some researchers disagree and say he had no role in the conversion.

## Legacy

The Young Bengal movement was neither very organised nor long-lasting, and its reach was limited to the English-educated middle class of Calcutta. Even so, many of its members later became active in journalism, education, administration and political organisations. They raised issues such as freedom of the press, women's education and oppression of peasants in public debate.

Between the reformism of Raja Rammohan Roy and the later nationalism, Derozio and his disciples appear as a link — they spread the idea that reason, questioning and free thinking are the first condition of the rebuilding of any society.
