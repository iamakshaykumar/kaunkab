## Life: History and Tradition

Most of what we know about the life of Adi Shankaracharya comes from biographical works called the "Shankara-Digvijaya", which were written many centuries after him. They therefore mix history with devotion and legend.

According to tradition he was born in a Brahmin family in the village of **Kalady**, on the banks of the Periyar (Purna) river in Kerala. His father died in his childhood. At a young age he took sannyasa and made **Govinda Bhagavatpada** his guru on the banks of the Narmada; Govinda is himself regarded as a disciple of Gaudapada.

Scholars disagree about his date. Many nineteenth- and twentieth-century scholars took it as 788–820 CE, while modern research usually places him in the first half of the eighth century (about 700–750 CE).

## The Essence of Advaita Vedanta

"Advaita" means "not two". According to Shankaracharya:
- The supreme truth, **Brahman**, is one; it is pure consciousness.
- The individual soul (**Atman**) is fundamentally not different from Brahman.
- The diversity of the world appears separate because of **maya**, or avidya (ignorance).
- The main means of liberation (moksha) is **knowledge** — the experience of the unity of the Atman and Brahman.

He did not deny practical life; he recognised two levels of truth — the practical (everyday) and the ultimate.

To explain this, the Advaita tradition has the famous example of the rope and the snake: someone sees a rope lying in the dark, mistakes it for a snake and is frightened, but in the light it turns out that it was always a rope. In the same way, when knowledge dawns, the illusion of difference vanishes. Shankaracharya regarded the knowledge portion of the Upanishads, rather than the ritual portion of the Veda, as the most important, though he accepted action as a means of purifying the mind.

## Works and Debates

Shankaracharya's commentaries on the three foundational texts of Vedanta — the **Upanishads, the Brahma Sutras and the Bhagavad Gita** (the Prasthanatrayi) — are the most famous. His commentary on the Brahma Sutras is regarded as the central text of Advaita philosophy. The "Upadeshasahasri" is also considered an authentic work of his.

Traditional stories describe his travels across the country and his debates, including the famous debate with the Mimamsa scholar **Mandana Mishra**. According to the story, after losing, Mandana Mishra became his disciple; tradition links him with Sureshvaracharya, but historians do not agree on this identification.

## The Four Monasteries and the Dashanami Tradition

According to tradition, Shankaracharya founded four monasteries in the four directions of the country:
- **Sringeri** (Karnataka) in the south,
- **Dwarka** (Gujarat) in the west,
- the Govardhan Math at **Puri** (Odisha) in the east,
- **Jyotirmath** (Uttarakhand, near Badrinath) in the north.

Regarding the historical beginnings of these monasteries, some scholars hold that their organised form developed in later centuries. The **Dashanami** tradition of ascetics (ten names such as Giri, Puri, Bharati, Saraswati and so on) is also linked to him.

## Legacy

Shankaracharya gave the philosophical side of the Vedic tradition a reasoned and systematic form. Later teachers — such as Ramanuja (Vishishtadvaita) and Madhva (Dvaita) — disagreed with his ideas and developed their own philosophies, which kept a lively debate going in Indian philosophy. According to tradition he died at Kedarnath, where today there is a memorial to his samadhi.
