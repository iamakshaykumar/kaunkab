## Saadat Khan — From Governor to Founder of a Dynasty

The founder of the nawabi dynasty of Awadh, **Mir Muhammad Amin**, known as **Saadat Khan** (Burhan-ul-Mulk), came from a Shia Sayyid family of **Nishapur** in Iran. Around 1708–09 he came to India in search of employment and rose in Mughal service. In 1720 he played a part in the conspiracy that brought down the **Sayyid brothers**, and received a high post as a reward.

On **9 September 1722** he was appointed **governor of Awadh**. He strengthened revenue administration by reducing the power of the jagirdars and gradually made Awadh an almost autonomous province, although he continued to acknowledge formal subordination to the emperor in Delhi.

At the time of Nadir Shah's invasion in 1739, Saadat Khan was captured in the battle of **Karnal**. He died in Delhi on **19 March 1739**. The sources do not agree on the cause of death — some say illness, while according to one contemporary writer he took poison after being humiliated by Nadir Shah.

## Safdarjung and Shuja-ud-Daula

Saadat Khan's nephew and son-in-law **Safdarjung** (Muhammad Muqim) became the next nawab. He was also the **wazir** of the Mughal empire and was deeply entangled in the factionalism of Delhi. He died in 1754.

In the time of his son **Shuja-ud-Daula** (1754–1775), Awadh was the decisive power in North India. In the Third Battle of **Panipat** in 1761 he sided with Ahmad Shah Abdali. In 1764, together with Mir Qasim and the emperor Shah Alam II, he fought the British at **Buxar**, but was defeated. By the **Treaty of Allahabad** of 1765 Awadh was returned to him, but the war indemnity and the military agreement with the Company set Awadh on the path of dependence on the British. His capital was **Faizabad**, where he built palaces and fortifications along the Ghaghara. He died at Faizabad on **26 January 1775**.

## Asaf-ud-Daula and Lucknow

**Asaf-ud-Daula** (1775–1797) shifted the capital to **Lucknow** as soon as he took the throne. At Faizabad his mother **Bahu Begum** had great control over the treasury and jagirs; later, in the time of Warren Hastings, the Company extracted money from these 'Begums of Awadh', which became one of the issues in the trial of Hastings in Britain.

During the famine of **1784** Asaf-ud-Daula began building the **Bara Imambara** so that thousands of people would get employment. A popular story says that ordinary people built by day and at night respectable families who had fallen into poverty worked in disguise — this should be taken as a folk tale. The nearby **Rumi Darwaza** is also from this period.

## The Culture of Lucknow

Under nawabi patronage Lucknow became a centre of Urdu poetry, music, Kathak, Awadhi cuisine and refined etiquette. After the ruin of Delhi, many poets such as Mir Taqi Mir came to Lucknow. Shia religious traditions — especially the majlis gatherings of Muharram and the imambaras — shaped the city's cultural identity.

## Growing Pressure from the Company and the End

After Asaf-ud-Daula, in the time of Saadat Ali Khan II, Awadh had to cede half its territory to the Company in 1801. In 1819 Ghazi-ud-Din Haidar, encouraged by the British, declared himself 'Badshah' and formally broke Mughal subordination. Finally, in **February 1856**, Lord Dalhousie annexed Awadh on the charge of misrule, and the last nawab, **Wajid Ali Shah**, was sent to Calcutta. This annexation is considered a major reason for Awadh's widespread participation in the Rebellion of 1857.
