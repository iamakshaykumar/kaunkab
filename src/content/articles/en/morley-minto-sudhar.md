## Background: Discontent and 'Rallying the Moderates'

After the **Partition of Bengal** in 1905, the Swadeshi movement, boycott and revolutionary activities intensified in the country. At the **Surat session** of 1907 the Congress split into the Moderates and the Extremists. Britain had a Liberal government and **John Morley** was Secretary of State for India, while in India **Lord Minto** (1905-1910) was Viceroy.

Their policy was to **keep the Moderates with them** by giving some constitutional concessions and to isolate the Extremists and revolutionaries. In this same period the government also enforced harsh repressive laws — that is, reform and repression went hand in hand.

## The Act and Its Provisions

The **Indian Councils Act 1909**, passed by the British Parliament, received royal assent on **25 May 1909** and came into force from November 1909. Its main provisions were:

- The maximum number of additional members of the central (Imperial) Legislative Council was raised from 16 to **60**; the provincial councils also grew.
- Some members began to come through **indirect elections** — through electoral bodies such as municipalities, district boards, landlords, chambers of commerce and universities.
- At the Centre an **official majority** was retained, while in the provinces a majority of non-official members was kept; even so, many of them were nominated.
- Members could discuss the budget, move resolutions and ask supplementary questions, but their resolutions were only recommendations. Debate on foreign policy and matters concerning the princely states was not allowed.

## Separate Electorates

The most far-reaching provision was **separate electorates for Muslims** — some seats were reserved for which only Muslim voters elected Muslim representatives. This demand had been made in October 1906 by the **Simla Deputation**, led by the **Aga Khan**, which met the Viceroy at Shimla, and Minto showed sympathy for it. Officials such as the Home Secretary Herbert Risley also supported it.

Views on this have differed. The Muslim League and its supporters regarded it as a necessary protection of minority interests. The Congress and nationalist historians called it a policy of **'divide and rule'**, which made religion the basis of electoral identity.

## Appointment of Indians

Along with the reforms, Indians were given places in some high posts. Morley appointed two Indians to the Council of the Secretary of State for India in London. In **1909** the well-known Calcutta lawyer **Satyendra Prasanna Sinha** became the first Indian member (Law Member) of the Viceroy's Executive Council.

## Reaction and Significance

Moderate leaders such as **Gopal Krishna Gokhale** welcomed the reforms as a step forward, although even they were uneasy about separate electorates. For the Extremists these reforms were a sham, because they contained no promise of responsible or self-government — the real reins of power remained entirely in the hands of British officials.

Historians debate how much separate electorates gave birth to communal politics and how much they were the result of tensions already existing. But it is certain that this system was carried further in the Acts of 1919 and 1935, and its influence on India's constitutional politics lasted until 1947. The **Montagu-Chelmsford Reforms** of 1919 replaced the framework of 1909.
