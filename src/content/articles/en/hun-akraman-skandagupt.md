## Who Were the Huns

The **Huns** were groups of horse-riding tribes of Central Asia who spread over Bactria and Afghanistan in the fourth and fifth centuries. In Indian texts the name 'Huna' has been used for several different groups — such as the **Kidarites**, the **Alchon** (Alkhon) and the **Hephthalites** (White Huns). Their connection with Attila's Huns, who attacked Europe, is not clear beyond the similarity of name. After occupying Gandhara and Punjab, they turned their eyes to the prosperous Gupta empire of the Ganga valley.

## Skandagupta's Throne

**Skandagupta** was a son of the emperor **Kumaragupta I** and became emperor in the Gupta year 136 (about **455 CE**). His mother's name is not found in the inscriptions, so some historians believe that he was not the son of the chief queen and had to struggle against other claimants for the throne. The Junagadh inscription says that he won the royal fortune through his valour — some also take this as a sign of a succession struggle. This question is not yet settled.

## The Bhitari Pillar Inscription

The stone pillar at **Bhitari** in Ghazipur district of Uttar Pradesh is known for Skandagupta's most famous inscription. According to it, Skandagupta re-established the "wavering fortune" of the dynasty, and in this struggle he had to spend a night sleeping on the bare earth. The inscription mentions enemies who had grown very strong in wealth and army — most scholars read them as a tribe called the **Pushyamitras**, but this reading is disputed. It goes on to say that Skandagupta fought a fierce battle with the **Huns** and defeated them.

The **Junagadh** inscription in Gujarat (Gupta year 138, about 457–458 CE) also mentions victory over the **mlecchas**, who are generally taken to be the Huns. According to the same inscription, **Chakrapalita**, son of **Parnadatta**, the governor of Saurashtra, rebuilt the embankment of the Sudarshana lake, broken by heavy rain. The invaders of this time were probably the Kidarite Huns.

## A Period of Crisis

Despite his success in stopping the Huns, Skandagupta's reign was passed under pressure. His gold coins are found in small numbers and some have a reduced gold content, which many historians link to the economic burden of the wars. His last known date is the Gupta year 148 (about **467 CE**). After this his half-brother **Purugupta** probably became emperor. After Skandagupta the Gupta empire gradually grew weaker and provincial feudatories became more independent.

## Toramana and Mihirakula

At the end of the fifth century came a fresh invasion by the **Alchon Huns**. Their king **Toramana** (c. 493–515 CE) advanced from Punjab as far as central India. The inscription on the huge **Varaha** image of Vishnu at **Eran** in Madhya Pradesh dates to the first year of Toramana's reign, showing that eastern Malwa had come under him. Another inscription at Eran (about 510 CE) mentions a battle with the Gupta king **Bhanugupta**, which many scholars consider a fight against the Huns.

Toramana's son **Mihirakula** (c. 515–542 CE) ruled from **Sakala** (today's Sialkot). An inscription of a Sun temple at Gwalior records the fifteenth year of his rule. Both Xuanzang and Kalhana describe him as a cruel ruler, but their accounts are much later.

## Fall and Impact

Mihirakula's power was broken by two defeats. According to Xuanzang, the Gupta king of Magadha, **Narasimhagupta Baladitya**, took him prisoner and released him. Then in about **528 CE** the Aulikara king of Malwa, **Yashodharman**, defeated him — the pillar inscription at Mandsaur (Sondani) claims that Mihirakula bowed his head at his feet.

The Hun invasions hastened the break-up of the Gupta empire, damaged the trade routes and cities of the north-west, and divided political power among many regional dynasties. How large the actual scale of the destruction caused by them was is still debated among historians.
