## From Trader to Wazir

Hemu, also called **Hemchandra**, was born around **1501** and spent his childhood in **Rewari** (in today's Haryana). Historians differ about his birthplace, family and caste. At first he traded in saltpetre, and then, in the time of **Islam Shah**, son of **Sher Shah Suri**, became superintendent of the Delhi market.

After Islam Shah's death in **1553**, **Adil Shah Suri** came to the throne. Adil Shah was weak at governing, so the real command passed into Hemu's hands — he became wazir (chief minister) and chief commander. It is said that he won **22 battles** against Afghan rebels for Adil Shah; this number comes from tradition and later writers.

## The Capture of Delhi and 'Vikramaditya'

In January **1556** **Humayun** died suddenly. His son **Akbar** was only thirteen and his guardian was **Bairam Khan**. At that time the Mughals held only Punjab, parts of Delhi–Agra and Kabul–Kandahar. Hemu recognised the opportunity. He advanced from the direction of Bengal and on the way took cities such as Etawah, Kalpi and **Agra** from the Mughals.

In **October 1556**, at **Tughlaqabad** near Delhi, he defeated the Mughal army defending Delhi. The date of this battle is given in the sources as **6 or 7 October**. After taking Delhi, Hemu assumed the title **'Vikramaditya'**. According to the Panipat district administration, his coronation took place at the **Purana Qila**. Historians debate whether he had declared himself fully independent of Adil Shah — some scholars, such as Satish Chandra, doubt this.

## 5 November 1556: The Field of Panipat

After the loss of Delhi there was panic in the Mughal camp, but Bairam Khan decided to fight. The two armies faced each other on the historic field of **Panipat** — the same place where thirty years earlier Babur had defeated Ibrahim Lodi. Akbar and Bairam Khan stayed about eight miles behind the field; on the field the Mughal army was led by **Ali Quli Khan** (later Khan Zaman).

Even before the battle Hemu had suffered a heavy blow: his **artillery**, whose protection was weak, was seized by Ali Quli Khan. So at Panipat Hemu had no cannon, but he had a large number of **war elephants**. The number of elephants differs in the sources — in some 500, in others as many as 1,500.

Hemu attacked fiercely with his elephants and at first both wings of the Mughals wavered. The Mughal horsemen struck from the sides and targeted the elephants and mahouts. Hemu himself was fighting seated on an elephant named **'Hawai'**. Then an **arrow struck him in the eye** and he fell unconscious in the howdah. Not seeing their commander, the army fell into a stampede and a battle that seemed to be won turned into a defeat. According to the sources about 5,000 people were killed on the field.

## The End of Hemu

The elephant carrying the unconscious Hemu was captured and brought to the Mughal camp; according to the Panipat district website he was captured by **Shah Quli Khan Mahram**. What happened next, the sources do not agree on. Akbar's court historian **Abul Fazl** writes that Akbar refused to strike a wounded and helpless man and that Bairam Khan beheaded Hemu. But according to the contemporary writer **Muhammad Arif Qandahari**, Akbar himself struck at Bairam Khan's urging and took the title 'Ghazi'. Many modern historians regard Abul Fazl's account as a courtly version fabricated later.

Hemu's head was sent to Kabul and his trunk was hung in Delhi. According to the sources his aged father was also killed later. Adil Shah Suri was killed in **April 1557** and the challenge of the Sur dynasty ended.

## Significance

The Second Battle of Panipat made the **return of Mughal rule** certain. Delhi and Agra came back into Mughal hands, and they also gained about 120 war elephants. The next four years passed under Bairam Khan's guardianship, after which Akbar took over the rule himself.

Hemu is remembered today as a capable administrator and commander who rose from an ordinary background to the throne of Delhi. Some twentieth-century writers and posters called him the "last Hindu emperor", but this is a later point of view — Hemu himself was an official of an Afghan (Sur) court and his army included a large number of Afghan soldiers and commanders.
