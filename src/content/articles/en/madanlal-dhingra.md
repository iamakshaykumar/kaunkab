## From Amritsar to London

**Madan Lal Dhingra** was born on **18 September 1883** in **Amritsar** in a well-off Punjabi Khatri family (some sources differ slightly on the year of birth). His father Dr Ditta Mal was a government civil surgeon and was considered loyal to British rule. While studying at Government College, Lahore, he was expelled from the college for taking part in a student demonstration against foreign cloth.

In **1906** he went to **University College, London**, to study mechanical engineering. There he came into contact with **India House**, founded by **Shyamji Krishna Varma** — a hostel for Indian students, which at that time was the centre of Indian revolutionary ideas abroad. Here **Vinayak Damodar Savarkar** had a deep influence on him.

## Background: Repression in Bengal and Revolution Abroad

After the Partition of Bengal in 1905, the swadeshi movement and revolutionary activities intensified in India. In 1908 Khudiram Bose was hanged, and in cases such as the Alipore Bomb Case many young men received harsh sentences. The young people connected with India House saw these events as oppression of the nation and began to talk of resistance even in London.

Angered by Dhingra's political activities, his father publicly broke ties with him.

## 1 July 1909: The Imperial Institute

On the evening of **1 July 1909**, the annual reunion ('At Home') of the **National Indian Association** was being held at the **Imperial Institute** in South Kensington. **Sir William Hutt Curzon Wyllie**, the political aide-de-camp to the Secretary of State for India, was also present; he was considered to be connected with the apparatus that kept watch on Indian students in London.

At the end of the function Dhingra fired at close range at Curzon Wyllie; he died on the spot. The Parsi physician **Dr Cawas Lalcaca**, who came to intervene, was also killed by a bullet. According to several accounts, Dhingra later said that he had not intended to kill Lalcaca. Dhingra was seized on the spot.

## Trial and Hanging

On **23 July 1909** he was tried at the **Old Bailey**, London's central criminal court. Dhingra engaged no lawyer and said that an English court had no right to try him. His written statement, in which he described his act as an act of patriotism, was later printed in newspapers. The same day he was sentenced to death.

On **17 August 1909** Dhingra was hanged at **Pentonville Prison**. His age at that time was about 25. He was not allowed a Hindu funeral and was buried in the prison itself.

## Reaction and Assessment

Indian reaction to the incident was divided. At a meeting of Indians in London on 5 July 1909 a resolution condemning Dhingra was brought, which Savarkar opposed. Dhingra's family also distanced themselves from his act.

**Mahatma Gandhi**, who was then in London as a representative of the Indians of South Africa, clearly condemned the murder and said that the real blame lay with those who had pushed the young man onto this path. His book **'Hind Swaraj'**, written the same year, is often read as a dialogue with this very idea of violent nationalism.

After this incident the British government tightened surveillance of India House and the centre soon broke up; in 1910 Savarkar too was arrested and sent to India. Even so, in India Dhingra is remembered with respect as one of the early revolutionaries to sacrifice his life on foreign soil, while the death of the innocent Lalcaca remains the sad side of this episode.
