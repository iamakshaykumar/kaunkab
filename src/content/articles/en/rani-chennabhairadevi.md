## The Kingdom of Nagire and the Saluva Dynasty

In the 16th century, **Gerusoppa**, on the banks of the **Sharavathi river** on Karnataka's western coast, was the capital of a prosperous kingdom called **Nagire** or the 'province of Gerusoppa'. This kingdom was subordinate to the **Vijayanagara Empire**, and its rulers came from a branch of the **Saluva dynasty** who followed **Jainism**. Another branch of the same dynasty ruled **Haduvalli** (near Bhatkal).

**Rani Chennabhairadevi** took over the rule of Nagire in **1552** and reigned until **1606**, for about **54 years**. Vijayanagara had given her the title **'Mahamandaleshwari'**. Her kingdom is said to have stretched from South Goa to the coastal parts of today's **Uttara Kannada** and **Dakshina Kannada** and into the Ghat region, including places such as **Bhatkal**, **Honnavar**, **Karwar**, **Ankola** and **Mirjan**.

## 'The Pepper Queen'

**Black pepper**, grown in the forests of the Western Ghats, was then among the most valuable spices in the world. From Chennabhairadevi's kingdom, pepper, cinnamon, nutmeg, ginger and sandalwood were sent in large quantities to the Arab lands and Europe through the ports of **Bhatkal** and **Honnavar**. Her grip on this trade was so strong that the **Portuguese** began calling her **'Raina da Pimenta'** — that is, **'the Pepper Queen'**.

**Mirjan Fort**, built on the bank of the **Aghanashini river** to protect the trade, is associated with her. Built of red **laterite** stone, the fort covers about four hectares, and the queen is believed to have spent considerable time here. Spices were carried from here to the coast by river.

## Conflict with the Portuguese

In the 16th century the Portuguese, with **Goa** as their base, wanted to control the sea trade of the entire western coast. They levied taxes at the ports, demanded a 'cartaz' (permit) from ships, and pressed local rulers to sell spices only to them. Even before Chennabhairadevi's reign, in **1542**, the Portuguese had defeated King **Immadi Devaraya** of Haduvalli and burned **Bhatkal**. Immadi Devaraya's wife **Chennadevi** is said to have been Chennabhairadevi's elder sister. Later Chennabhairadevi gained control of Haduvalli as well as Gerusoppa.

The queen refused to accept the Portuguese terms. According to the sources, she successfully withstood Portuguese attacks in **1559** and **1570**. According to some accounts, around 1570–71 she formed a front against the Portuguese together with rulers such as the **Adil Shahi** sultans of **Bijapur** and the **Zamorin of Calicut**. At the same time she was pragmatic — when necessary she also made trade agreements with the Portuguese. Historians see her policy as a balance between confrontation and trade.

## Religious Tolerance and Patronage

Although a Jain, Chennabhairadevi helped build and restore **Shaiva, Vaishnava and Shakta** temples. When the Portuguese pressure to convert grew in Goa, many **Saraswat Brahmin** and **Konkani** families who fled from there found refuge in her kingdom. She also patronised Jain **basadis** (temples); tradition links the **Chaturmukha Basadi** of the Gerusoppa area to her period.

She was also a patron of scholars. Under her patronage, the Jain scholar **Bhattakalanka** wrote the famous grammar of the Kannada language, the **'Karnataka Shabdanushasana'**.

## Downfall and Legacy

After the Battle of Talikota in 1565, Vijayanagara weakened, and rivalry grew among the small kingdoms of coastal Karnataka. The rulers of neighbouring **Keladi** and **Bilgi** had long had their eyes on Nagire. At the beginning of the 17th century the combined armies of the two attacked Gerusoppa and defeated the queen. Chennabhairadevi was taken prisoner and **died in captivity**. Gerusoppa became part of the Keladi kingdom.

Chennabhairadevi's story resembles that of another queen of coastal Karnataka of the same era, **Rani Abbakka** of Ullal, who also stoutly resisted the Portuguese. Both examples show that in the 16th century the earliest resistance to the European powers came from the small kingdoms of the western coast, and that women rulers played an important role in it.

For a long time Chennabhairadevi's name was confined to the folk memory of Karnataka's coastal areas, but in recent years she has gained national recognition. On **24 July 2025**, President **Droupadi Murmu** released a **commemorative postage stamp** in her honour at Rashtrapati Bhavan. Today the ruins of Gerusoppa, its Jain basadis and Mirjan Fort recall the queen who kept her hold on the spice trade for more than half a century and refused to bow before the most powerful European power at sea.
