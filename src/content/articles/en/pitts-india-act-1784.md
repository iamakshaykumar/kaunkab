## Background: The Shortcomings of the Regulating Act

The **Regulating Act** of **1773** had established the British Parliament's first direct control over the working of the East India Company, but it had many shortcomings. Governor-General **Warren Hastings** was often helpless before the majority of his own council, there were clashes of authority between the Supreme Court and the council, and the governments of Madras and Bombay made wars and treaties as they pleased. Wars with the **Marathas** and **Mysore** and the Company's growing debt raised concern in Britain.

In **1781** Parliament set up a **Select Committee** and a **Secret Committee** to investigate the Company's affairs; the chairman of the Secret Committee was **Henry Dundas**. These reports exposed the Company's mismanagement and corruption, and the demand for reform grew stronger.

## Fox's Bill and the Rise of Pitt

In **1783** **Charles James Fox**, a leader of the Fox-North coalition government, introduced an "India Bill" proposing to hand the Company's political and commercial powers to commissioners appointed by Parliament. Critics said this would give the ruling party enormous patronage (the power to distribute posts and money) linked to India. The bill passed the House of Commons, but King **George III** signalled to members of the Lords to vote against it, and in **December 1783** it was defeated in the House of Lords.

The King dismissed the government and made the **24-year-old** **William Pitt the Younger** Prime Minister. After winning a majority in the 1784 election, Pitt passed his own India bill, which received royal assent on **13 August 1784**. This came to be called **Pitt's India Act**.

## Main Provisions

The main provisions of this Act were as follows:

- **Board of Control** — A board of up to **six** "Commissioners for the Affairs of India" was set up in London, including a Secretary of State and the Chancellor of the Exchequer. This board supervised and directed all the Company's **civil, military and revenue** affairs. Orders the Company was to send out were first placed before this board.
- **Court of Directors** — The Company's directors kept the power over **trade** and the **appointment** of officials.
- **Secret Committee** — A secret committee of **three members** drawn from the directors was formed, through which the board's confidential orders, such as instructions on war and treaties, were sent directly to India.
- **Governor-General's council** — The number of council members was **reduced from four to three**, one of whom was the Commander-in-Chief. This made it easier for the Governor-General to obtain a majority.
- **Madras and Bombay** — These presidencies were clearly made subordinate to **Bengal** (Calcutta) in matters of war, diplomacy and revenue.
- The Act described a policy of conquest and expansion of dominion in India as contrary to the "wish, honour and policy" of the British nation, and Company officials were expected to give an account of their property.

## The Dual System of Government

This Act created a **dual system** (dual control) over Company rule in India: on one side the Company's **Court of Directors**, on the other the British government's **Board of Control**. In practice real political power gradually passed into the hands of the board. **Henry Dundas** became the board's most influential member and later its president; for many years he was regarded in practice as the "minister for India".

In **1786** a supplementary Act was passed, which gave the Governor-General the right to **override** his council's decision in special circumstances and allowed him to hold the post of Commander-in-Chief as well. It was under this arrangement that **Lord Cornwallis** came to India as Governor-General.

## Significance and Legacy

Pitt's India Act is regarded as an important milestone in India's constitutional history. It made clear that the Company was no longer merely a trading body but a ruling power working under the control of the British government. Critics said that dual control divided responsibility and slowed decisions, yet this framework lasted about **74 years**.

The later **Charter Acts (1793, 1813, 1833 and 1853)** kept modifying this arrangement. In **1858**, through the **Government of India Act**, Company rule ended, and the Board of Control and the Court of Directors were replaced by the **Secretary of State for India** and the India Council. In this sense Pitt's India Act laid the foundation for the direct rule of the British Crown over India.
