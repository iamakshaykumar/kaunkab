## The Writing of the Harappans

In the excavations of the cities of the **Indus Valley Civilisation** — Harappa, Mohenjo-daro, Dholavira, Lothal — thousands of objects have been found with lines of small signs engraved on them. These are called the **Indus script** (or Harappan script). The mature Harappan period, that is, about **2600 to 1900 BCE**, is its main time, although some scholars place its early forms as far back as about 3500 BCE and a few stray signs around 1300 BCE.

This script has still **not been deciphered**. For this reason we do not even know the names of the Harappan kings, their gods or their language — almost all our information about this civilisation comes from archaeology.

## Where the Inscriptions Were Found

Most inscriptions are on **seals** — usually small square seals of steatite, with a line of signs at the top and the figure of an animal below. The most common figure is that of a one-horned animal, called the "unicorn" in English. Inscriptions are also found on clay sealings, copper tablets, pottery, and objects of bone and ivory.

It is estimated that **four to five thousand** inscribed objects have been found so far. But the inscriptions are extremely short — on average about **five signs**. At **Dholavira** in Gujarat, a line of large signs was found near the northern gate of the city, called the "Dholavira signboard"; it is counted among the largest inscriptions of this script.

## Signs and Direction of Writing

Scholars do not agree on the number of signs in the script. The Indian scholar **Iravatham Mahadevan** published in 1977, through the Archaeological Survey of India, a detailed list of all known inscriptions (a **concordance**), in which **419** distinct signs were counted. **Asko Parpola** of Finland prepared a different numbering of the signs and a large collection of inscriptions. It is commonly said that the script has **more than about 400** basic signs, although in 2015 Bryan Wells put the number at about 694.

Such a large number of signs shows that it was not an alphabet (like the Roman one with 26 letters). Many scholars believe it must have been a script combining **word-signs and sound-signs** (a "logo-syllabic" script). Most inscriptions seem to have been written **from right to left** — in many inscriptions the signs look squeezed together as they reach the left side, as if space ran short — but there are some exceptions too.

## Why It Could Not Be Deciphered

Egyptian hieroglyphs were read with the help of a **bilingual inscription** like the Rosetta Stone, and Brahmi with the help of Greek-Indian coins and recurring words. No such bilingual inscription has been found for the Indus script. The inscriptions are very short, their language is unknown, and there is also no known descendant text from that period.

For this reason, none of the hundreds of claims of decipherment has been accepted by scholars. Different scholars have linked its language to **Dravidian** (Parpola and Mahadevan leaned this way), **Indo-Aryan** or some lost language — all these are still only hypotheses.

## Was It Really a Script?

In 2004 Steve Farmer, Richard Sproat and Michael Witzel argued that these signs may not have written a language at all, but were a system of religious or political symbols. Their main arguments were the extreme shortness of the inscriptions and the fact that many signs appear only once or twice. Many scholars, including Parpola, strongly opposed this. In 2009 Rajesh P. N. Rao and colleagues showed through statistical analysis that the pattern of sign order resembles that of linguistic scripts, but they too accepted that this alone proves nothing. This debate is still open today.

## Significance

The Indus script is the oldest known form of writing in South Asia. After the decline of the civilisation it almost completely disappeared, and the next solid evidence of writing in India comes much later — in the Brahmi and Kharoshthi of Ashoka's time. No direct connection between the two has been proved. If a long or bilingual inscription is ever found, perhaps the Harappans will be able to speak in their own words for the first time.
