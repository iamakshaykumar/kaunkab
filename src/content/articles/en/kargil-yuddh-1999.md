## From Lahore to Kargil

In **May 1998** both India and Pakistan carried out nuclear tests. To ease tension, in **February 1999** Prime Minister **Atal Bihari Vajpayee** travelled to Lahore by bus, and the two countries signed the **Lahore Declaration**, which promised to resolve disputes peacefully and bilaterally. But only a few months later the situation changed completely.

**Kargil** is a mountainous area of Ladakh, about 205 kilometres from Srinagar. **National Highway 1**, which links Srinagar with Leh, passes through it. Every year in the harsh winter, the forces on both sides used to vacate high posts. In the winter of 1999, soldiers of the Pakistan Army's **Northern Light Infantry** and special forces, along with some infiltrators, quietly crossed the **Line of Control** and occupied many high peaks on the Indian side. This allowed them to fire directly on the highway.

## Discovery of the Intrusion

On **3 May 1999**, local shepherds reported suspicious activity in the Kargil area. In the second week of May, the patrol of **Captain Saurabh Kalia** in the **Batalik** area was ambushed, which revealed the scale of the intrusion. Gradually it became clear that there had been infiltration at many places — the **Mushkoh Valley, Drass, Kaksar, Batalik** and on to Turtuk.

India launched **Operation Vijay** to take the positions back, involving about **30,000** soldiers. From **26 May** the Indian Air Force began air strikes under **Operation Safed Sagar**. Flying at great altitude in thin air was difficult; during this time two Indian fighter aircraft and a helicopter were shot down, and Flight Lieutenant **K. Nachiketa** became a prisoner of war. To prevent tension from rising further, the government's instruction was that Indian fighter aircraft must not cross the Line of Control under any circumstances.

## The Battle for the Peaks

The war was fought on steep rock faces at heights of 16,000 to 18,000 feet, where the enemy sitting above could easily see soldiers climbing from below. First of all it was necessary to recapture the peaks near the highway.

- **Tololing** — After several weeks of hard fighting in Drass, Tololing and Point 4590 came back into Indian hands in **mid-June**. India suffered its heaviest losses in this battle, but it turned the course of the war in India's favour.
- **Point 5140** — After the success of **Captain Vikram Batra's** unit here, his message **"Yeh Dil Maange More"** became famous across the country.
- **Tiger Hill** — This peak, about 16,500 feet high, was among the hardest fronts. The attack began on **3 July**, and units of **18 Grenadiers** and **8 Sikh**, with help from the Air Force, captured the whole peak by **8 July**.
- **Point 4875** — Fighting went on at this peak in the Mushkoh Valley from 4 July; it was here that Captain Batra, who advanced despite being wounded, was martyred.
- **Batalik** — In this area east of the Indus river too, there was hard fighting for several peaks; the last major objective here was the **Jubar** peak.

## Diplomacy and the End of the War

Pakistan at first said that it was not its regular soldiers on the peaks but "mujahideen", but later evidence made the direct role of the Pakistan Army clear. International pressure went against Pakistan. US President **Bill Clinton** refused to intervene until Pakistan withdrew its forces across the Line of Control. On **4 July 1999**, in Washington, Prime Minister **Nawaz Sharif** agreed to withdraw the troops.

Even after this, Pakistani soldiers remained at some positions, and they were removed in the last week of July. On **26 July 1999** the fighting ended completely and India took back all its posts. Since then this day has been celebrated as **Kargil Vijay Diwas**.

## The Cost and the Lessons

According to Indian official figures, **527** Indian soldiers lost their lives in this war and more than 1,300 were wounded. Estimates of Pakistan's losses vary widely — from 400 to several thousand. For this war **four Param Vir Chakras** and eleven Maha Vir Chakras were awarded.

In Pakistan there was a dispute over responsibility for the operation. Nawaz Sharif blamed Army chief **Pervez Musharraf**, while Musharraf said that the Prime Minister had been informed earlier. Only a few months later, in October 1999, Musharraf seized power.

In India the question was raised why such a large intrusion had not been detected earlier. To investigate this, the **Kargil Review Committee** was formed under the chairmanship of **K. Subrahmanyam**, and after its report many reforms were made in the intelligence system and defence management. Today the **Kargil War Memorial** at Drass reminds us of the soldiers who defended the country's border on the icy peaks.
