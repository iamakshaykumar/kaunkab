## The Meeting of Two Empires

In the sixth century BCE, when mahajanapadas like **Magadha** were rising in the Ganga valley, in the west the **Achaemenid** Empire of Iran was becoming the greatest power in the world. In the time of its founder **Cyrus** the Great, around **535 BCE**, Persian armies may have crossed the Hindu Kush and reached **Gandhara** — though the details of this are very hazy.

## Darius I and the New Provinces

Clear evidence comes from the time of **Darius I** (reigned 522–486 BCE). In his famous trilingual inscription carved on the **Behistun** rock in Iran, dated to around **518 BCE**, **Gandhara** is named among the provinces (satrapies) of the empire. In later inscriptions — such as those at Persepolis and the one on his tomb at **Naqsh-e Rustam** — **Hindush** (the Indus valley region) is also added. The word "Hindush" is the Iranian form of "Sindhu", and later names like "Hind" and "India" came out of this tradition.

According to the Greek historian **Herodotus**:

- Darius sent **Scylax of Caryanda** on a voyage down the Indus River to the sea; this voyage lasted about 30 months.
- The Indian province was the twentieth province of the empire and paid **360 talents of gold dust** every year in tax — which in his account was the largest tribute. Modern scholars consider this figure exaggerated.

## Indians Recorded in Stone

Indians appear repeatedly in Achaemenid art. Among the delegations bringing gifts on the stairs of the Apadana palace at **Persepolis** there is an **Indian group** — in dhotis, barefoot, carrying loads on poles over their shoulders. On the tomb of Xerxes I at **Naqsh-e Rustam** (c. 480 BCE), among the peoples of various nations lifting the emperor's throne are representatives of Gandhara and Hindush.

Herodotus tells us that Indian soldiers also went in Xerxes's invasion of Greece in **480 BCE**, wearing clothes of "tree wool" (cotton). A century and a half later, in **331 BCE** at the battle of Gaugamela, Indian contingents and some elephants also fought on the side of the last Achaemenid emperor Darius III.

## Influence on Script and Administration

The Achaemenid Empire ran the business of its far-off provinces in the **Aramaic** language and script. Most scholars believe that the **Kharoshthi** script of north-west India — which, like Aramaic, is written **from right to left** — developed from this contact, though evidence of the intermediate stages has not been found. The **Aramaic inscription** of the fourth century BCE found at Taxila is solid evidence of this influence. Later Ashoka also had some of his edicts in the north-west carved in Kharoshthi and Aramaic. Some scholars also see Iranian influence on the tradition of Mauryan pillars and inscriptions, but this is a matter of debate.

## The End

By the fourth century BCE the Achaemenid hold on the distant Indian provinces had weakened. **Alexander** ended the Achaemenid Empire by 330 BCE and himself came to this region in 327–325 BCE. Soon after, **Chandragupta Maurya** merged the north-west into an Indian empire. Even so, this Iranian period of about two centuries remained an important bridge linking Gandhara with West Asia.
