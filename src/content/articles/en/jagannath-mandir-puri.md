## The Temple of Nilachala

**Puri**, on the eastern coast of Odisha, is counted among the four Dhams of the Hindu tradition. The **Jagannath Temple** here stands on a small hill, called in tradition **Nilachala** or Nilagiri ('the blue mountain'). In the temple are worshipped **Jagannath** (a form of Vishnu/Krishna), his elder brother **Balabhadra** and sister **Subhadra**, along with the **Sudarshana** chakra.

An ancient legend about the origin of the temple is linked to King **Indradyumna**, who according to tradition built the first temple. This legend is a matter of faith; historically, the temple that stands today dates from the twelfth century.

## Built by the Eastern Ganga Kings

The construction of the present temple began under **Anantavarman Chodaganga** of the **Eastern Ganga dynasty**, after about **1112 CE**, and continued through the twelfth century. His descendant **Anangabhima Deva III** in the thirteenth century dedicated his kingdom to Jagannath and called himself his deputy. Since then the kings of Odisha have presented themselves as servants of Jagannath.

The temple is built in the Kalinga style of architecture. Its main tower (deula) rises about **65 metres** above the sanctum and is visible from far away along the coast. In front of the main temple stand the jagamohana, the nata-mandapa and the bhoga-mandapa, and the whole complex is enclosed by high walls. A later king of the same dynasty, Narasimhadeva I, built the Sun Temple at Konark.

## Wooden Deities and Nabakalebara

In most Hindu temples the idols are of stone or metal, but at Puri the three deities are made of **neem wood**. Their large round eyes and seemingly unfinished form give them a distinct identity. Some scholars see in this form the influence of Odisha's tribal traditions.

At intervals of some years the **Nabakalebara** ('new body') ritual is held, in which new idols are made from specially chosen neem trees. The last Nabakalebara took place in **2015**.

## The Rath Yatra

Every year on **Ashadha Shukla Dwitiya**, the **Rath Yatra** is held at Puri. The three deities, seated on huge separate wooden chariots, travel about three kilometres to the **Gundicha Temple**:
- **Nandighosha** — Jagannath's chariot, with 16 wheels
- **Taladhwaja** — Balabhadra's chariot, with 14 wheels
- **Darpadalana** — Subhadra's chariot, with 12 wheels

These chariots are built anew every year and thousands of devotees pull them with ropes. Before the procession the **Gajapati** king of Puri sweeps the path in front of the chariots with a gold-handled broom — this rite is called **Chhera Pahanra** and shows that before the Lord even the king is a servant. About a week later the deities return to the main temple in the **Bahuda Yatra**.

## Mahaprasad and the Tradition of Faith

The temple's kitchen is counted among the largest in the world, where food offerings are cooked in earthen pots over wood fires. This offering is called **Mahaprasad** and is available at the **Ananda Bazaar** in the complex, where people of different castes have sat together to eat it. Traditionally only Hindus are allowed inside the temple; others can see the deities from the road during the Rath Yatra.

In the sixteenth century **Chaitanya Mahaprabhu**, after taking sannyasa, spent most of the last years of his life in Puri, through which devotion to Jagannath spread to Bengal and then across India. Today Jagannath is at the centre of Odisha's cultural identity, and the Rath Yatra is also celebrated in many cities of the world.
