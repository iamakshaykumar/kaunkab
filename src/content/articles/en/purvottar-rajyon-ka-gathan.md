## The North-East at Independence

When India became independent in **1947**, most of the North-East lay within a single large province, **Assam**. It included the plains of the Brahmaputra and Barak valleys, the **Naga Hills**, the **Lushai Hills** (today's Mizoram), the **Khasi-Jaintia and Garo Hills** (today's Meghalaya) and, to the north, the **North-East Frontier Agency (NEFA)** (today's Arunachal Pradesh). **Manipur** and **Tripura** were separate princely states, which merged with India in **1949** and became union territories in 1956.

The tribal communities of the hill areas — Nagas, Mizos, Khasis, Garos and others — had their own languages, traditions and systems of governance. Under the **Sixth Schedule** of the Constitution they were given autonomous district councils, but many communities wanted greater self-rule to protect their identity. Decisions such as making Assamese the official language of Assam also increased discontent in the hill areas.

## Nagaland: The First New State (1963)

In the Naga areas a movement for a separate identity had been under way since before independence, led by the **Naga National Council**, and in the 1950s it turned into an armed struggle. In search of a solution, in **1957** the Naga Hills and the Tuensang area were combined into a separate administrative unit under the Centre. In **1960** a **16-point agreement** was reached between the **Naga People's Convention**, an organisation of moderate Naga leaders, and Prime Minister **Jawaharlal Nehru's** government.

On this basis Parliament passed the **State of Nagaland Act, 1962**, and **Article 371A** was added to the Constitution, giving special protection to the Nagas' religious and social practices, customary law and land ownership. On **1 December 1963** President **Sarvepalli Radhakrishnan** inaugurated Nagaland at **Kohima** as India's **16th state**. **P. Shilu Ao** became its first Chief Minister. Even after statehood, talks with some Naga groups continued for decades.

## 1972: Meghalaya, Manipur and Tripura

Leaders of the Khasi, Jaintia and Garo hills peacefully demanded a separate hill state under the banner of the **All Party Hill Leaders' Conference (APHLC)**. First a middle path was found: on **2 April 1970** **Meghalaya** became an **autonomous state** within Assam. "Meghalaya" means "abode of the clouds".

After this, Parliament passed the **North-Eastern Areas (Reorganisation) Act, 1971**, under which the map of the North-East changed on **21 January 1972**:

- **Meghalaya** became a full state; **Williamson A. Sangma** became its first Chief Minister and **Shillong** its capital.
- **Manipur** and **Tripura** went from union territories to full states.
- **Mizoram** — the Lushai Hills (Mizo Hills district) separated from Assam and became a union territory.
- **Arunachal Pradesh** — NEFA was given a new name and made a union territory.

In these same years the **North Eastern Council**, headquartered in Shillong, was also formed for the balanced development of the region.

## The Mizo Movement and the 1986 Accord

In the Mizo hills in **1959**, after the bamboo flowered, the rat population suddenly exploded and crops were destroyed. This famine is called **"Mautam"**. A feeling grew among the people that the government had not helped in time. An organisation formed for famine relief later grew into the **Mizo National Front (MNF)** under the leadership of **Laldenga**. On **1 March 1966** the MNF declared independence and began an armed uprising, and the government responded with military action. For about twenty years the region struggled with violence and unrest.

Initiatives by church leaders and civil organisations opened the way to talks. Negotiations that began under Prime Minister **Indira Gandhi** were completed during **Rajiv Gandhi's** tenure. On **30 June 1986** in New Delhi, the **Mizo Peace Accord** (Mizoram Accord) was signed by Laldenga, Union Home Secretary **R. D. Pradhan** and Mizoram's Chief Secretary **Lalkhama**. Under it the MNF laid down its arms and agreed to work within the framework of the Indian Constitution, while the Centre promised to grant Mizoram full statehood. **Article 371G** was added to the Constitution to give special protection to Mizo customs and land.

## 1987: Mizoram and Arunachal Pradesh

On **20 February 1987** **Mizoram** became a full state of India, and **Laldenga** became the first Chief Minister of the new state; in the assembly election of February 1987 the MNF won a majority. A former insurgent leader becoming Chief Minister through democratic means was historic in itself. On the same day **Arunachal Pradesh**, a union territory since 1972, was also granted full statehood under the **State of Arunachal Pradesh Act, 1986**. Its capital is **Itanagar**.

## Legacy

These decisions created the present structure of the seven north-eastern states — Assam, Nagaland, Meghalaya, Manipur, Tripura, Mizoram and Arunachal Pradesh — often called the **"Seven Sisters"**; in 2002 **Sikkim** also joined the North Eastern Council. This reorganisation of states shows that the Indian Union adopted flexibility to make room for diverse identities within the Constitution. The **Mizo Accord** is often counted among the most successful peace agreements of independent India, because Mizoram remained peaceful for a long time afterwards. Even so, border disputes, questions of identity and development challenges in some parts of the North-East remain subjects of discussion today.
