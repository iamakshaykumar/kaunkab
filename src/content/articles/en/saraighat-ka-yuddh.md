## Background: The Ahom Kingdom and the Mughals

The **Ahom** people had established a kingdom in the Brahmaputra valley in the thirteenth century, which gradually became the leading power of Assam. In the seventeenth century the frontier of the Mughal Empire had reached Bengal and Kamrup, and the region around Guwahati became the centre of conflict between the two.

In 1662–63 the Mughal governor **Mir Jumla** advanced as far as the Ahom capital Garhgaon, and the Ahoms had to accept a harsh treaty under which a large part of western Assam had to be given to the Mughals. A few years later the Ahom king **Chakradhwaj Singha** reorganised the army and, under the commander **Lachit Borphukan**, won back Guwahati in 1667.

## Ram Singh's Campaign

When Guwahati slipped out of Mughal hands, the emperor Aurangzeb sent **Raja Ram Singh I**, the Kachhwaha ruler of Amber, to Assam with a large army. With Ram Singh were Rajput, Mughal and other contingents, and also a fleet of boats for the river.

Rather than face the large Mughal army in the open field, Lachit built lines of defence using the hills, marshes and river around Guwahati. Earthen embankments and fortifications were raised, so as to reduce the advantage of the Mughal cavalry. In the battle of **Alaboi** around 1669 the Ahoms suffered heavy losses, but they held firm on the defence line of Guwahati.

Meanwhile Chakradhwaj Singha died and **Udayaditya Singha** became king. On the Ahom side, along with Lachit, the prime minister **Atan Buragohain** was also a chief architect of strategy.

## March 1671: The Decisive Fight on the Brahmaputra

Failing to make progress on land, Ram Singh decided to launch a major attack on Guwahati by the river route. The Brahmaputra is narrowest at **Saraighat** near Guwahati, and the decisive battle took place here. The exact date of the battle is not known; most sources place it in the middle of March 1671.

- At the time of the battle Lachit was seriously ill, which was lowering the morale of the Ahom soldiers.
- It is said that when the Mughal boats advanced, Lachit, in his sick condition, boarded his boat and entered the battle and filled the soldiers with fresh spirit.
- The small and nimble Ahom boats attacked the Mughal fleet from both the front and the rear.
- The Mughal naval commander **Munawwar Khan** was killed and the Mughal fleet was forced to retreat.

After the defeat Ram Singh's army returned west towards the Manas river.

## Outcome

The victory at Saraighat halted Mughal expansion in Assam at that time. Lachit Borphukan died the next year, in 1672, at Kaliabor.

The struggle did not end here. In 1679 Guwahati again passed into Mughal hands, but in 1682, in the time of the Ahom king **Gadadhar Singha**, the Ahoms won it back permanently in the battle of Itakhuli. After this the Mughals were never able to take control of Assam.

Some famous stories about Lachit, such as the tale of his punishing his maternal uncle for negligence of duty, are very popular in folk tradition, but historians point out that no evidence for them is found in contemporary records.

## Memory and Significance

Saraighat is counted among the important river battles of Indian history. It shows that deep knowledge of geography, naval skill and united leadership can halt even a large and well-resourced army.

In Assam **24 November** is celebrated as **Lachit Divas**, his birth anniversary. His memorial (Lachit Maidam) stands at Holongapar near Jorhat, and in 2022 his 400th birth anniversary was celebrated at the national level.
