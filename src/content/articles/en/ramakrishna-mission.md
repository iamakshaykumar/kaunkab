## Ramakrishna Paramahamsa: The Saint of Dakshineswar

**Ramakrishna Paramahamsa** was born on **18 February 1836** in the village of **Kamarpukur** in Bengal, in a poor Brahmin family. His childhood name was Gadadhar Chattopadhyay. At about the age of twenty he became the priest of the **Dakshineswar Kali Temple**, built near Calcutta by Rani Rashmoni. In 1859 he was married to **Sarada Devi**, who later came to be revered as his spiritual partner and as the 'Mother' of the disciples.

Ramakrishna practised many paths of spiritual discipline — bhakti, tantra and Advaita Vedanta; in 1865 Totapuri initiated him into sannyasa. His tradition says that he also tried the practices of Islam and Christianity. His simple teaching was that all religions are different paths leading to the same truth, and that God dwells in every living being.

## The Circle of Disciples and the First Math

Many educated young men of Calcutta began to come to Ramakrishna, the foremost among them being **Narendranath Datta** — later **Swami Vivekananda**. Ramakrishna, suffering from throat cancer, died on **16 August 1886** at the garden house in **Cossipore** (some accounts give the night of 15 August). A little before his death he had given saffron robes to the young disciples, and in December 1886 they took an informal vow of renunciation.

The disciples set up the first math in an old, dilapidated house at **Baranagar**. This was the beginning of the Ramakrishna Order. The math later moved to Alambazar (1892) and then to Nilambar Mukherjee's garden house (1898).

## 1 May 1897: The Founding of the Mission

After the World's Parliament of Religions at Chicago (1893) and about four years in the West, Vivekananda returned to India in early 1897. He believed that individual liberation alone was not enough; monks should serve the hungry, the sick and the uneducated, regarding this as 'service of the living being as Shiva'.

On **1 May 1897** he called a meeting of monks and householder devotees at the house of Ramakrishna's householder devotee **Balaram Bose** at **Baghbazar**, Calcutta. Here the **'Ramakrishna Mission Association'** was founded and Vivekananda was elected its general president. The motto of the organisation became — *Atmano mokshartham jagad-hitaya cha* (For one's own liberation and for the welfare of the world).

In the same year Swami Akhandananda began famine relief work at **Sargachi** in Murshidabad district, which is counted among the early examples of the Mission's service work.

## Belur Math

For a permanent home for the math, land was bought at **Belur** (Howrah), on the western bank of the Hooghly. On **9 December 1898** Vivekananda installed there the sacred relics (urn of ashes) of Ramakrishna, and from **January 1899** the math moved to Belur. This is today the headquarters of both the Ramakrishna Math and the Mission.

The **Sri Ramakrishna Temple**, built according to Vivekananda's vision, was completed later — its foundation stone was laid on 13 March 1929 and it was dedicated on **14 January 1938**. In its architecture an attempt has been made to show the spirit of 'harmony of all religions' by blending elements of Hindu, Buddhist, Islamic and Christian architecture.

## Organisation and Work

After Vivekananda's death in 1902, Ramakrishna's direct disciple **Swami Brahmananda** took over leadership of the Order, and Sarada Devi remained in the role of adviser. The Mission was registered in **1909** under the Societies Registration Act (1860). The Math and the Mission are legally separate, but the trustees of the Math form the governing body of the Mission.

Today the Mission has more than two hundred and fifty centres in India and abroad, which run schools and colleges, hospitals, rural and tribal development, and relief work in disasters such as floods and earthquakes.

## Significance and Assessment

Among the religious and social reform movements of the nineteenth century, the special mark of the Ramakrishna Mission was that it joined renunciation with social service and awakened self-confidence in the Indian spiritual tradition. Vivekananda's ideas inspired many nationalists, although the Mission itself kept away from politics. Historians also point out that the Mission's emphasis was more on service and spiritual renaissance than on reform — on questions such as the caste system it did not run a direct movement as the Brahmo Samaj or Arya Samaj did.
