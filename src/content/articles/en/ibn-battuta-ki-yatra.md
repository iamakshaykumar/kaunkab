## From Tangier to Hindustan

**Ibn Battuta** was born in 1304 in the city of Tangier in **Morocco**, North Africa. After receiving an education in Islamic law, in 1325, at about 21 years of age, he set out towards Mecca for the Hajj. This journey lasted about thirty years and took him through Arabia, Iran, East Africa, Anatolia and Central Asia to India.

According to his own account he reached the bank of the Indus river on **12 September 1333**. Passing through Multan he came to Delhi in 1334.

## At the Court of Muhammad bin Tughluq

At that time Delhi was ruled by **Muhammad bin Tughluq**, who welcomed foreign scholars generously. Seeing Ibn Battuta's education in Islamic law, the Sultan appointed him **qazi of Delhi** (judge) and gave him a large salary and jagirs. He stayed in Delhi for about six or seven years.

This time was not easy for him. Along with the Sultan's generosity, Ibn Battuta also saw his harshness and suspicion at close quarters. At one point he even lived in fear of the Sultan's wrath because of his connection with a suspected Sufi. In his account the Sultan appears on the one hand as extremely generous and on the other as a ruler who gave severe punishments.

## The Picture of India in the Rihla

Ibn Battuta was a curious traveller and he recorded those things about India that were new to his readers:
- He described **Delhi** as a vast, densely populated and prosperous city, with grain stores and markets inside its strong walls.
- **The postal system** — through the horse post (*uluq*) and foot runners (*dawa*) stationed at regular distances, messages and sometimes even fruit reached the capital quickly.
- **Coconut, betel leaf (paan)**, the trade of the markets, cotton cloth and trade routes extending far and wide.
- Slavery, incidents of sati and the danger of robbers on the roads are also in his account.

According to NCERT these accounts are an important source for understanding Sultanate-period cities, trade and communication, although, being the view of an outsider, they contain comparisons with his own background and, in places, exaggeration.

## The Embassy to China and the Further Journey

When an embassy from the Yuan emperor of China came to Delhi, the Sultan sent a return embassy and made Ibn Battuta its head. According to one date in the Rihla he left Delhi in **July 1342**, although according to the historian Ross Dunn it could also be 1341.

On the way robbers captured him and he somehow escaped. Then at **Calicut** (Kozhikode) one ship of the embassy sank in a storm and the gifts were lost. Fearing the Sultan's anger, he did not return to Delhi and went to the **Maldives**, where he remained qazi for about nine months. After this, passing through Sri Lanka, Bengal and South-East Asia, he reached China around 1345. Some scholars have also doubted the authenticity of the China part.

## The Composition of the Rihla and Legacy

In 1349 Ibn Battuta returned to Morocco. At the request of the Sultan there, Abu Inan, he dictated an account of his travels to the scholar **Ibn Juzayy**, which was completed in **1355**. This work is called the **Rihla** (journey). He died in Morocco around 1368–69.

Some details in the Rihla were written from memory and some seem to have been taken from the works of others, so historians read it with caution. Even so, it is counted among the most vivid direct sources about the court of Muhammad bin Tughluq, the city life of Delhi and the Indian Ocean trade of the fourteenth century.
