## The War of Succession

Born on **3 November 1618** at Dahod (Gujarat), Aurangzeb was the son of Shah Jahan and Mumtaz Mahal. When Shah Jahan fell seriously ill in 1657, a war for the throne broke out among his four sons — Dara Shikoh, Shuja, Aurangzeb and Murad.

At the battle of **Dharmat** in **April 1658** and then at **Samugarh** near Agra in **May 1658**, Aurangzeb defeated Dara's army. Shah Jahan was placed under house arrest in the Agra fort, where he died in 1666. Aurangzeb declared himself emperor in 1658 and took the title 'Alamgir'. In **1659** Dara Shikoh was captured and put to death; the formal reason given was opposition to religion, but many historians regard it as essentially a political step. Murad was also imprisoned and later executed, and Shuja fled towards Arakan and disappeared.

## North India: Rebellion and Resistance

Aurangzeb's first two decades were spent mainly in North India. During this time there were many rebellions — of the **Jats** in the Mathura region (from 1669), of the **Satnamis** of Narnaul (1672), and of **Afghan** tribes on the north-western frontier. In **1675** the Sikh Guru **Tegh Bahadur** was put to death in Delhi on the emperor's orders. In **1679** a long conflict with the **Rathors** began over the succession in Marwar, and it continued until the end of his reign.

## Religious Policies and the Historians' Debate

In **1679** Aurangzeb reimposed the **jizya** tax on non-Muslims, which Akbar had removed almost a hundred years earlier. Several temples — such as the Vishwanath temple at Banaras (1669) and the Keshavdev temple at Mathura (1670) — were demolished on his orders. He discouraged music at court and had the Shariah-based legal compilation 'Fatawa-i-Alamgiri' prepared.

The assessment of these policies is a matter of dispute among historians. Historians such as **Jadunath Sarkar** saw his religious bigotry as a major cause of the empire's decline. Historians such as **Satish Chandra** place greater stress on political and economic causes — the shortage of jagirs, factions among the nobles and constant warfare. **Richard Eaton** and **Audrey Truschke** argue that temple destruction was often limited to sites connected with rebellious or politically hostile rulers, and also remind us that Aurangzeb made grants to many temples and monasteries, and that during his time the proportion of Hindu (especially Maratha) officers among the Mughal nobles increased. The sources also differ greatly on the number of temples destroyed. The balanced view is that his policies combined religious conviction with political calculation, and that their effect fell differently on different communities.

## The Long Wars in the Deccan

In 1681 his rebel son **Prince Akbar** took refuge with the Maratha king **Sambhaji**. After this Aurangzeb himself came to the Deccan and spent roughly the last twenty-five years of his life there. **Bijapur** was annexed to the Mughal empire in **1686**, and **Golconda** in **1687** after a siege of eight months. In **1689** Sambhaji was captured and put to death.

Even so, the Maratha struggle did not end. Under Rajaram and after him **Maharani Tarabai**, the Marathas continued guerrilla warfare. The Mughal army won fort after fort, but the Marathas would spread again into new regions. These wars exhausted both the treasury and the army.

## Death and Legacy

Aurangzeb died on **3 March 1707** near Ahmednagar at the age of about 88. As he had wished, he was buried in a simple grave at **Khuldabad**. Popular stories say that he met his personal expenses by sewing caps and copying the Quran; this is part of his image of simplicity, but the details are not certain.

In his time the Mughal empire was geographically at its largest, with almost the entire subcontinent under it. But within a few decades of his death the power of the centre fell apart and the Marathas, Sikhs, Jats and regional nawabs rose. For this reason Aurangzeb is often seen as the symbol of both the peak of the Mughal empire and the beginning of its decline.
