## From Three Provinces to One Sultanate

After the campaigns of Bakhtiyar Khalji in the early 13th century, Bengal came under the Delhi Sultanate, but because of the distance from Delhi its governors often became independent. In the 14th century Bengal was divided into three parts — **Lakhnauti**, **Satgaon** and **Sonargaon**.

**Shamsuddin Ilyas Shah** conquered all three and by **1352** joined the whole of Bengal into one sultanate. **Firuz Shah Tughluq** of Delhi attacked twice, in 1353 and 1359, and besieged the fort of **Ekdala**, but could not conquer Bengal completely. In the end a treaty was made and Bengal remained independent for about two hundred years.

## The Ilyas Shahi Sultans and the Adina Mosque

Ilyas Shah's son **Sikandar Shah** ruled for about three decades. Between **1373 and 1375** he built the huge **Adina Mosque** at his capital **Pandua** (today's Malda district in West Bengal), which was considered the largest mosque in the subcontinent in its time. This building, with hundreds of domed bays, is a ruin today, but is a monument of national importance.

**Ghiyasuddin Azam Shah** (1390–1411) is remembered for his sense of justice and love of learning. His correspondence with the Persian poet **Hafez** is famous. In his time envoys travelled to and from the Ming court of China, and in 1406 the Chinese traveller **Ma Huan** visited Bengal.

## From Raja Ganesha to Husain Shah

In the early 15th century the influence of the zamindar **Raja Ganesha** grew, and in 1415 his son became sultan under the name **Jalaluddin Muhammad Shah**. Later the Ilyas Shahi dynasty returned and the capital moved to **Gauda**. After this there was also a few years of unstable rule by Habshi (of African origin) commanders.

In 1494 the wazir **Alauddin Husain Shah** took the throne and laid the foundation of the **Husain Shahi dynasty**, which lasted until 1538. In his time (1494–1519) the sultanate carried out campaigns toward Kamata (Assam), Tripura, the borders of Odisha and Arakan. His Hindu subjects gave him titles such as "Nripati Tilaka" and "Jagat Bhushana". In this period **Chaitanya Mahaprabhu** was spreading bhakti in Bengal, and his followers **Rupa** and **Sanatana Goswami** had earlier held high posts in Husain Shah's court.

## Patronage of Bengali Literature

The most unique contribution of the Bengal Sultanate is the court patronage of **Bengali-language literature**. In the time of Sultan **Ruknuddin Barbak Shah**, **Maladhar Basu** wrote the **Shrikrishna Vijaya**, based on the Bhagavata, between 1473 and 1480 — it is considered the oldest dated Bengali narrative poem. Maladhar Basu received the title "Gunaraj Khan"; sources differ on which sultan gave this title.

Under the patronage of Husain Shah's commander **Paragal Khan**, **Kavindra Parameshvara** made a Bengali version of the Mahabharata, and under the patronage of **Chhuti Khan**, **Shrikara Nandi** wrote another Mahabharata. **Bijay Gupta** composed the **Manasamangal** poem, which praises Husain Shah. The arrival of these epics in the people's language instead of Sanskrit gave new energy to Bengali literature.

## The Surs, the Karranis and the Mughals

In 1538 **Sher Shah Suri** seized Gauda and the Husain Shahi dynasty came to an end. After the Sur period the Afghan **Karrani** dynasty (1564–1576) ruled Bengal and in 1565 moved the capital to **Tanda**. The last sultan, **Daud Khan Karrani**, challenged Akbar's authority, but with his defeat at the Battle of **Rajmahal** on **12 July 1576**, the Bengal Sultanate ended. In the eastern delta the resistance of the "Baro Bhuiyan" zamindars still continued for a few decades.

The ruins of Gauda and Pandua built of brick and terracotta — the Adina Mosque, and Gauda's **Bara Sona Mosque** and **Chhota Sona Mosque** — still show that distinctive Bengali style of architecture, in which sloping, hut-like roofs and fine brick carving came from the local tradition.
