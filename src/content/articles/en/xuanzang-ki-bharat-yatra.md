## Why the Journey?

**Xuanzang** (the modern Chinese pronunciation is Xuanzang) was born around 602 CE in China. He became a Buddhist monk at an early age. At that time the translations of Buddhist texts available in China were incomplete and contradicted one another. So he decided to go to **India**, the land of the Buddha, to read the original texts and bring them back.

According to most sources he set out in **629 CE** (some traditions say 627 CE). At that time the Tang emperor had banned foreign travel, so he had to cross the border secretly.

## A Difficult Road

Xuanzang took the overland route through Central Asia. He passed the Gobi desert, Turfan, Kucha, Tashkent, Samarkand, Balkh and Bamiyan, and crossing the Hindu Kush reached **Gandhara**. From there he went to Kashmir, where he studied for some time, and then moved on to the Buddhist pilgrimage sites of North India. On the way he saw places such as Kapilavastu, Kushinagar, Sarnath and Bodh Gaya.

## Study at Nalanda

The most important stop of Xuanzang's journey was the **Nalanda Mahavihara**. Here he studied Yogachara philosophy, logic, grammar and Sanskrit under the aged teacher **Shilabhadra**. He gave a detailed description of the buildings of Nalanda, its thousands of monks, the strict entrance examination and the debates of scholars. Today historians rely heavily on his account to understand Nalanda.

After Nalanda he also travelled to South and West India, and later went east at the invitation of **Bhaskaravarman**, the king of Kamarupa (Assam).

## Meeting the Emperor Harshavardhana

In the last phase of his journey Xuanzang met the powerful emperor of North India, **Harshavardhana**, whose capital was **Kannauj**. According to Xuanzang, Harsha called a great religious assembly at Kannauj, in which Xuanzang put forward the case for Mahayana. After this he also went with Harsha to the assembly at **Prayag**, where Harsha gave away his accumulated wealth every five years. These assemblies are generally placed around 643 CE.

It should be remembered that Xuanzang was himself a Buddhist and Harsha's guest, so his account may lean toward praise of Harsha.

## Return and Translation

Xuanzang returned to the Chinese capital **Chang'an** (today's Xi'an) in early **645 CE**. This time the emperor Taizong welcomed him. He had brought with him hundreds of texts, images of the Buddha and relics.

At the emperor's request, in **646 CE** he had his travel account, *"Records of the Western Regions of the Great Tang Dynasty"* (Si-Yu-Ki), completed. He spent the remaining years of his life translating Sanskrit texts into Chinese. He died in **664 CE**.

## Historical Significance

Xuanzang's account gives valuable information about the kingdoms, cities, religions, education and people's way of life in 7th-century India. In the 19th century archaeologists used his directions and distances to identify many ancient Buddhist sites. His journey is a great symbol of the exchange of knowledge between India and China.
