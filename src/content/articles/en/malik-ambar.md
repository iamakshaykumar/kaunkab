## Background: From Harar to the Deccan

Malik Ambar was born around 1548 in the **Harar** region of East Africa (today's Ethiopia). His childhood name is said to have been **Chapu**. In his teens he was captured as a slave and sold several times. In Baghdad a merchant, **Mir Qasim al-Baghdadi**, bought him, had him educated, initiated him into Islam and gave him the name "Ambar".

Merchants brought him to the Deccan, where **Chengiz Khan**, the Peshwa of Ahmednagar, bought him. Chengiz Khan was himself a Habshi (of African origin). Under his patronage Ambar learned administration and the art of war.

## From Soldier to Commander

After Chengiz Khan's death Ambar became free and formed a small body of troops of his own. After serving for some time at Bijapur he returned to Ahmednagar. In 1600, when the Mughals captured the fort of Ahmednagar, the sultanate began to fall apart.

At this time Ambar took a decisive step. He declared a member of the Nizam Shahi dynasty Sultan under the name **Murtaza Nizam Shah II** and, becoming Peshwa (prime minister) himself, took the real power of government. The capital was moved first to Paranda, then to **Junnar**.

## Guerrilla War and Maratha Cooperation

The Mughal army was far larger in numbers and artillery. Ambar faced it not with direct battle but with **guerrilla warfare**, which in the Deccan was called "bargi-giri".

- Swift Maratha horsemen cut off the enemy's supplies, struck suddenly and vanished into the hills.
- Many Maratha chiefs such as **Shahaji Bhosale** (Shivaji's father) and the Jadhavs gained military experience under Ambar. Historians believe this style of fighting later became the hallmark of Maratha power.
- In **1624**, at the Battle of **Bhatvadi**, Ambar defeated the combined Mughal-Bijapur army. It is said that he broke a dam and flooded the enemy's path with water.

Ambar did not succeed every time. In the late 1610s and around 1620, in the campaigns of Prince Khurram (later Shah Jahan), he suffered defeats and lost territory, and many Maratha chiefs went over to the Mughals.

## Revenue Reforms

Malik Ambar is credited with an orderly **land revenue system** in the Deccan.

- Rent was fixed on the basis of the measurement of land and the quality of the yield.
- Instead of revenue-farming, emphasis was placed on direct and stable rent, so that farmers would be encouraged to expand cultivation.
- Many historians believe that later Mughal administrators adopted this same basis when building the revenue system in the Deccan.

## Khadki: The Founding of a New City

Around 1610 Ambar founded a new city called **Khadki** and made it his capital. For it he had a system of underground canals built to bring water from the hills. Later, in Aurangzeb's time, the city came to be called **Aurangabad**, which is now called **Chhatrapati Sambhajinagar**.

## End and Legacy

Malik Ambar died in **1626** at about the age of 78. He was buried at **Khuldabad**. His son **Fateh Khan** succeeded him, but he lacked his father's ability, and about a decade later, by 1636, the Ahmednagar Sultanate was divided between the Mughals and Bijapur.

Ambar's journey from slave to architect of a sultanate is regarded as the most remarkable chapter in the history of people of African origin in India. For the Mughals he was the hardest opponent, and for the Deccan a capable administrator.
