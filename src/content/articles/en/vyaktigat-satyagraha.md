## Background: The War and the Congress's Dilemma

When the Second World War broke out in September 1939, the Viceroy **Lord Linlithgow** declared India to be at war without consulting Indian leaders. In protest, the Congress governments in the provinces resigned in October–November 1939.

The Congress's dilemma was that it was against fascism and Nazism but did not want to cooperate in the war as an enslaved country. Its condition was that a promise of independence after the war and an immediate responsible national government be given. When pressure on Britain grew after the fall of France in June 1940, the Congress repeated this demand.

## The August Offer (8 August 1940)

In reply Linlithgow announced the **'August Offer'** from Shimla on **8 August 1940**. It said that:

- more Indians would be taken into the Viceroy's Executive Council;
- a War Advisory Council would be formed;
- after the war a representative body of Indians would frame the constitution;
- no system of government would be imposed without the consent of the minorities.

The Congress Working Committee rejected it at Wardha on **21 August 1940**, calling it vague and inadequate. Critics said that the condition about minorities in practice gave the Muslim League something like a veto over any settlement. The League too ultimately did not accept the offer.

## Why an Individual, Not a Mass, Satyagraha

Within the Congress many were in favour of a large mass movement, but Gandhi did not agree. His argument was that it was not right to win freedom by putting Britain, caught in a war, in difficulty, and that the atmosphere in the country was not ready for a large movement; there was also the danger of violence breaking out in a large movement.

So he chose a middle path: a **limited, individual satyagraha**. Satyagrahis chosen by Gandhi, after giving prior notice, would make an anti-war speech at a public place — that it was wrong to help Britain's war with money or men and that all wars should be opposed through non-violence. The aim was to stress **freedom of speech** and to show that India was not in the war of its own will. On 27 September 1940 Gandhi met Linlithgow and put his case, but no agreement was reached.

## Vinoba Bhave: The First Satyagrahi

On **17 October 1940**, at **Paunar** near Wardha, **Vinoba Bhave** began the satyagraha by making an anti-war speech. Gandhi had chosen him because of his devotion to non-violence and his years of constructive work; at that time he was not very famous in the country. Within a few days he was arrested.

The second satyagrahi was **Jawaharlal Nehru**, but even before he could begin the satyagraha he was arrested in Uttar Pradesh on **31 October 1940** and sentenced to four years on the basis of old speeches (some sources give the length of the sentence as four and a half years). The third satyagrahi was **Brahmadatt**.

## Expansion and End

In the first phase enthusiasm was limited, so Gandhi suspended it for some time in December 1940. From January 1941 a second phase began, which included everyone from Congress leaders, office-bearers and legislators to ordinary workers. It is estimated that about **20 to 25 thousand** people were arrested under the Defence of India Act.

By the end of 1941 the situation of the war had changed — Germany attacked the Soviet Union and in December 1941 Japan also entered the war. In this atmosphere, in December 1941 the government released Nehru and the other satyagrahis, and the movement gradually died down.

## Significance

The Individual Satyagraha brought no immediate political gain, and many historians regard it as a movement deliberately kept on a 'slow flame'. Even so, it kept the national movement alive during the war, challenged the British claim that India was willingly with them in the war, and maintained the discipline of the Congress. After the failure of the Cripps Mission in March 1942, this same discontent turned into the widespread **Quit India Movement** of August 1942.
