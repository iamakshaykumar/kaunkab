## Background: From the Sayyids to the Lodis

After Timur's invasion of 1398 the Delhi Sultanate had shrunk greatly. The **Sayyid dynasty**, in power from 1414, was confined to a small area around Delhi. In the same period, **Bahlul Lodi** of the Afghan (Pashtun) Lodi tribe emerged as a powerful governor in the Sirhind region of Punjab.

The last Sayyid sultan **Alam Shah** preferred to leave Delhi and live at Badaun, and on **19 April 1451** Bahlul Lodi became the Sultan of Delhi. This was the first Afghan dynasty in Delhi.

## Bahlul Lodi (1451–1489)

Bahlul's rule rested on the cooperation of the Afghan chiefs. According to tradition he gave his chiefs the respect due to equals and would sit with them on the carpet — a style quite different from the royal distance kept by sultans like Balban.

A large part of his reign was spent fighting the **Sharqi sultans of Jaunpur** in the east, who were Delhi's greatest rivals. After a long struggle, Bahlul defeated the Sharqi sultan Husain Shah and merged Jaunpur into his kingdom. He died in 1489.

## Sikandar Lodi (1489–1517) and Agra

Bahlul's son **Sikandar Lodi** (original name Nizam Khan) is considered the ablest ruler of the Lodi dynasty. He extended the kingdom as far as Bihar and increased the Sultan's control over the Afghan chiefs.

To keep watch over the regions of Rajasthan and central India, he refounded **Agra** on the bank of the Yamuna (**1504**) and made it his capital. Later this same Agra became the main capital of the Mughal empire.

Sikandar patronised trade and learning, introduced a standard yard (Gaz-i-Sikandari) for measuring agricultural land, and himself wrote Persian poetry under the name 'Gulrukhi'. On the other hand, he is considered bigoted in religious matters; the chronicles of that period mention the destruction of some temples. Historians view his rule with both these aspects.

## Ibrahim Lodi (1517–1526)

After Sikandar, his son **Ibrahim Lodi** became Sultan. He tried to impose the Sultan's authority on the chiefs, which went against Afghan tradition. As a result many powerful chiefs became angry with him; his brother Jalal Khan also rebelled. He also had a conflict with **Rana Sanga** of Mewar.

Discontented people such as the governor of Punjab, **Daulat Khan Lodi**, and Ibrahim's uncle **Alam Khan** invited **Babur**, the ruler of Kabul, to come to India.

## End and Legacy

On **21 April 1526**, in the **First Battle of Panipat**, Babur defeated Ibrahim Lodi's large army with artillery and a new war strategy. Ibrahim was killed on the battlefield; his grave is still at Panipat. With this the Lodi dynasty and the Delhi Sultanate, which had lasted since 1206, came to an end.

Historians see the Lodi period as a tension between the Sultan's absolute authority and the Afghan chiefs' tribal tradition of equality — this very tension ultimately became the dynasty's weakness. The Lodi rulers promoted a distinctive style of domed tombs, examples of which can still be seen in Delhi's Lodi Garden. Afghan power rose again only a few years later in the form of **Sher Shah Suri**, who drove the Mughal emperor Humayun out of India.
