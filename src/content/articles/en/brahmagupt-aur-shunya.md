## Who Was Brahmagupta?

**Brahmagupta** is believed to have been born in **598 CE** at **Bhillamala** (today's Bhinmal, Rajasthan). He himself wrote that his work was composed during the reign of the Chapa dynasty king **Vyaghramukha**. Later he is linked with **Ujjain**, which was then a major centre of Indian astronomy. His death is placed at about **668 CE**.

He wrote two major works:
- **Brahmasphutasiddhanta (628 CE)** — the name means "the corrected/well-established doctrine of Brahma". It has 24 chapters and about 1008 verses.
- **Khandakhadyaka (665 CE)** — a practical handbook for almanacs and astronomical calculation.

## Zero: From an Empty Place to a Number

The decimal place-value system was already developing in India. The idea of an "empty place" was implicit in the work of Aryabhata (499 CE), but the rules of mathematics for zero as an **independent number** are found clearly for the first time in Brahmagupta's work. That is why 628 CE is regarded as a milestone in the history of zero.

In the eighteenth chapter of the Brahmasphutasiddhanta, rules such as these are given (in today's language):
- Adding zero to a number, or subtracting zero from it, does not change the number.
- The sum of a positive number and an equally large negative number is **zero**.
- Multiplying any number by zero gives **zero**.

## The Mathematics of Negative Numbers

Brahmagupta called positive numbers **"dhana"** (fortune) and negative numbers **"rna"** (debt). His rules are like those taught in schools today:
- The sum of two positives is positive, the sum of two negatives is negative.
- The sum of a positive and a negative is their difference.
- Negative × positive = negative; **negative × negative = positive**; positive × positive = positive.

In one place his rule does not match modern mathematics: he stated **0 ÷ 0 = 0**, whereas today division by zero is considered undefined. For dividing a number by zero he gave no firm rule. Even so, thinking about such a difficult question as division by zero was in itself a great step at that time.

## Contributions to Algebra and Geometry

- He clearly described a method of solving **quadratic equations**.
- He gave a formula for finding the area of a **cyclic quadrilateral** (a quadrilateral whose four corners lie on a circle), which is today called "Brahmagupta's formula".
- He gave a method for solving difficult equations such as **Nx² + 1 = y²** (which Europe later called "Pell's equation"), which Bhaskara II later carried further.
- He also spoke of the earth's attraction of objects towards itself.

## Legacy: From Baghdad to Europe

In the eighth century Brahmagupta's texts reached the court of the Abbasid caliph **Al-Mansur**, where the astronomer **Muhammad ibn Ibrahim al-Fazari** made an Arabic translation of them. This translation became famous under the name **"Sindhind"**. Through Arab scholars, the Indian numeral system and the idea of zero later reached Europe.

In India too, respect for him continued. Bhaskara II called him **"Ganakachakrachudamani"**. In 1817 the English scholar Henry Colebrooke published an English translation of his algebra, which drew the attention of modern scholars to his achievements.
