## Background: From the Simon Commission to Civil Disobedience

The **Simon Commission**, set up in 1927, had not a single Indian member, so it was boycotted across the country. In October 1929 the Viceroy **Lord Irwin** announced that a **Round Table Conference** of the British government and Indian representatives would be held in London to discuss India's constitutional future. The name 'Round Table' came from the idea that all parties would sit as equals.

But at its Lahore session in December 1929 Congress declared Purna Swaraj as its goal, and in March 1930 the Civil Disobedience Movement began with the Dandi March. Thousands of people, including Gandhi, were in jail.

## The First Conference (November 1930 – January 1931)

The first conference began in London on **12 November 1930** and lasted until 19 January 1931. It was presided over by the British Prime Minister **Ramsay MacDonald**. It had representatives of the princely rulers, the Muslim League, the Hindu Mahasabha, the Liberals, the Depressed Classes and other groups — **Aga Khan**, **Muhammad Ali Jinnah**, **Tej Bahadur Sapru** and **Dr. Ambedkar** among them. But **Congress boycotted it**. The conference agreed on the idea of an All-India Federation, but everyone felt that without Congress no solution would last.

## The Gandhi–Irwin Pact (5 March 1931)

At the end of January 1931 Gandhi and the leaders of the Congress Working Committee were released. After long talks an agreement was reached between Gandhi and Lord Irwin on **5 March 1931**, also called the 'Delhi Pact'. Under it the government agreed to release non-violent political prisoners, withdraw repressive ordinances, permit peaceful picketing of liquor and foreign cloth shops, and allow people living near the seacoast to make salt for their own use. In return Congress suspended civil disobedience and agreed to take part in the second conference.

The pact was also criticised — the demand for an inquiry into police excesses was not accepted, and the hanging of **Bhagat Singh, Rajguru and Sukhdev** was not averted; they were hanged on 23 March 1931. A few days later, at the end of March 1931, the Karachi session of Congress, held under the presidency of Sardar Patel, approved the pact.

## The Second Conference (September – December 1931)

Gandhi left for London on 29 August 1931 aboard the SS Rajputana. The second conference ran from **7 September 1931** to 1 December 1931, in which Gandhi was the **sole official representative of Congress**; Sarojini Naidu was also with him. Gandhi claimed that Congress represented all of India, but the other delegates did not accept this.

The conference stalled on **representation of minorities**. Muslim delegates wanted separate electorates, and Dr. Ambedkar demanded separate electorates for the Depressed Classes too, which Gandhi strongly opposed. When no agreement was reached, Prime Minister MacDonald said he would give the decision himself — this became the **Communal Award** of August 1932, in protest against which Gandhi's fast led to the Poona Pact. Gandhi returned empty-handed; in January 1932 he was arrested again and civil disobedience restarted.

## The Third Conference and the Outcome

The third conference ran from 17 November to 24 December 1932. Only 46 delegates attended; Congress again did not take part, and Britain's Labour Party also stayed away. Its importance remained limited.

On the basis of the discussions of all three conferences the British government prepared proposals, which a joint committee of Parliament examined, and eventually the **Government of India Act 1935** was made. This law laid the foundation of provincial autonomy and a federal structure, many features of which later appeared in the Indian Constitution as well. The Round Table Conferences also made it clear that the question of India's freedom could no longer be separated from the complex question of the representation of communities.
