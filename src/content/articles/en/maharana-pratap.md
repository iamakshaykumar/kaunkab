## Birth and the Road to the Throne

Pratap was born in May 1540 to **Rana Udai Singh II** of Mewar and Queen **Jaiwanta Bai**. According to tradition he was born in the fort of **Kumbhalgarh**. In his childhood, in 1568, Akbar captured Chittor, and the royal court of Mewar shrank back to **Gogunda** in the Aravalli hills and the newly founded Udaipur.

Udai Singh died in 1572. He had chosen his younger son **Jagmal** as heir, but the main chiefs of Mewar supported the elder son Pratap. On **28 February 1572**, the day of Holi, Pratap was crowned at Gogunda. The angry Jagmal later went into Mughal service.

## Akbar's Envoys and the Refusal

Most Rajput states had by then made treaties with the Mughals. Akbar wanted Mewar too to accept his overlordship. Between 1572 and 1573 he sent four envoys — **Jalal Khan Qurchi**, **Kunwar Man Singh** of Amber, **Raja Bhagwant Das** and **Raja Todar Mal**. Pratap behaved courteously, but never accepted the condition of appearing in person at the Mughal court.

Stories such as an insult at a feast with Man Singh are famous in Rajasthani tradition, but modern historians regard them as exaggerated. After the talks failed, the Battle of **Haldighati** was fought on 18 June 1576, which is described in detail in a separate article.

## After Haldighati: A Struggle from the Mountains

At Haldighati the Mughal army had the upper hand on the field, but could not capture Pratap. The years after were the hardest. Akbar himself came towards Mewar at the end of 1576, and then, in campaigns led by **Shahbaz Khan**, Kumbhalgarh (1578), Gogunda and many posts in central Mewar passed into Mughal hands.

Instead of open battle, Pratap adopted **guerrilla tactics**. Bhil archers were a great support to him on the mountain paths and in the forests. The supplies of Mughal outposts were cut off, and they were attacked whenever there was a chance. In this period the Oswal Jain merchant **Bhamashah** gave a large sum of money and helped to raise the army again; the figures commonly given on the basis of James Tod (twelve years' expenses of 25,000 soldiers) are traditional and cannot be taken as exact.

## Recovery of Lost Land and Chavand

After about 1579 the Mughal pressure began to ease, because Akbar had to turn his attention to the revolts in Bengal and Bihar and then to the north-west. Pratap took advantage of this. Rajasthani tradition regards the clash at **Dewair in 1582** as the turning point, where after victory over a Mughal outpost many posts were won back one by one. The campaign led by **Jagannath Kachhwaha** in 1584 also achieved no lasting success.

By his last years Pratap had brought most of Mewar back under his control, except **Chittor and Mandalgarh**. **Chavand** in southern Mewar became his centre. Here he built a temple of Goddess Chamunda and gave patronage to poets, craftsmen and painters.

## The Last Days

On **19 January 1597**, at the age of about 56, Maharana Pratap died at Chavand. It is said that injuries received during a hunt were the cause. His son **Amar Singh I** succeeded him, continued the struggle for many years and finally, in 1615 under Jahangir, made an honourable treaty with the Mughals.

According to some accounts, Akbar too was moved on hearing of Pratap's death, and the Charan poet **Dursa Adha** composed verses in praise of Pratap, but such episodes should be seen only as tradition.

## Legacy

Maharana Pratap's kingdom was not large, and he could not take back Chittor. Even so, refusing to submit to a vast empire for several decades makes him extraordinary. From the folk tales of Rajasthan to the memory of modern India, he became a symbol of **self-respect and freedom**. Historians also remind us that the struggle of that time was more about political autonomy than religion — Pratap's army also included the Afghan commander Hakim Khan Sur, and on the Mughal side the Rajput commander Man Singh.
