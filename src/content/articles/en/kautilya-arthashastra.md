## Who Was Kautilya?

In Indian tradition **Kautilya**, **Chanakya** and **Vishnugupta** are regarded as names of one and the same person — the Brahmin teacher who, around 321 BCE, helped remove the Nanda dynasty and place **Chandragupta Maurya** on the throne of Magadha. His stories are found in Buddhist and Jain texts, the Puranas and Vishakhadatta's play *Mudrarakshasa*. According to these accounts he was connected with **Taxila**.

But these stories are later and also contain an element of legend. In no contemporary inscription of the Mauryan period, nor in Greek accounts (such as Megasthenes), is Chanakya's name found.

## The Nature of the Text

The Arthashastra is written in Sanskrit prose (with some verses). It names its author as **Kautilya**, and at the end the name **Vishnugupta** also appears.
- It has **15 adhikaranas** (books), **150 chapters** and **180 prakaranas** (topics).
- The second adhikarana is the largest, describing the duties of the **adhyakshas** (heads of departments) for the treasury, mines, agriculture, trade, weights and measures, the army and so on.
- The third and fourth adhikaranas describe law, courts and the control of crime.

## Main Ideas

- **Saptanga theory** — the seven constituents of the state: swami (the king), amatya (minister), janapada (land and people), durga (fort), kosha (treasury), danda (army) and mitra (ally).
- **Mandala theory** — framing foreign policy by treating the neighbouring state as a natural enemy and the state beyond it as a potential friend.
- **Shadgunya** — sandhi (peace treaty), vigraha (war), asana (waiting), yana (march), samshraya (seeking shelter) and dvaidhibhava (dual policy) — the six measures of foreign policy.
- **Espionage system** — an elaborate network of spies to gather news from inside and outside the state.

The Arthashastra is often called a treatise of harsh and practical politics, but it also reminds the king of his responsibility for the welfare of his subjects.

## The Debate on Its Date

Tradition regards it as a work of the fourth century BCE. The scholar **R. P. Kangle** also linked its core to the Mauryan period. But modern research — such as Thomas Trautmann's stylistic analysis and Patrick Olivelle's study — shows that the available text was formed in several stages. According to Olivelle, its main version was prepared around the **first to second century CE** and revised later. Therefore, to believe that the whole text of today was written word for word by Chanakya is disputed.

## The Discovery of 1905

For centuries the name of the Arthashastra was found in other texts, but the original text was thought lost. In **1905** a scholar of Tanjore gave a palm-leaf manuscript to the Oriental Library at Mysore (today's Oriental Research Institute), where the librarian **R. Shamasastry** identified it as the Arthashastra. From 1905 he began to publish an English translation in instalments, in 1909 he published the Sanskrit text, and in 1915 the complete English translation appeared.

## Significance

This discovery gave a new direction to the study of ancient Indian political thought. The Arthashastra shows how systematic the thinking on administration, taxation, trade and diplomacy was in ancient India. Even today students of political science and economics study it.
