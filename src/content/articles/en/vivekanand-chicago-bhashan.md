## From Narendranath to Vivekananda

Swami Vivekananda was born on 12 January 1863 in Calcutta. His original name was **Narendranath Datta**. In his college days he was a serious student of Western philosophy and science and questioned the existence of God. In search of answers to these questions he met the saint of Dakshineswar, **Ramakrishna Paramahamsa**, who later became his guru.

After Ramakrishna's death in 1886 Narendranath took sannyasa and for several years travelled across India as a parivrajaka (wandering monk). On this journey he saw the poverty and backwardness of the country at very close quarters. He adopted the name 'Vivekananda' shortly before going to America; this name was suggested by Raja Ajit Singh of Khetri.

## The Journey to America

In 1893, in the American city of Chicago, the 'World's Columbian Exposition' was being held to mark 400 years since Columbus's voyage to America. Along with it the **World's Parliament of Religions** was to be held — the first large formal gathering of representatives of the world's religions.

With the help of young disciples in Madras and supporters such as the Raja of Khetri, Vivekananda set sail from Bombay by ship on 31 May 1893 and, passing through Japan and Canada, reached Chicago at the end of July. There he found that to speak at the Parliament a letter of introduction from a recognised institution was necessary, which he did not have. In Boston he met Professor **John Henry Wright** of Harvard, who, impressed by his scholarship, recommended him to the organisers.

## 11 September 1893: "Sisters and Brothers of America"

On 11 September 1893 the Parliament was inaugurated in the building known today as the **Art Institute of Chicago**. Representatives of many countries and religions sat on the platform, and thousands of listeners sat before them.

When his turn came, the thirty-year-old Vivekananda, dressed in saffron robes, began his speech with these words — **"Sisters and Brothers of America"**. At these words the crowd of about seven thousand stood and applauded for a long time — about two minutes.

This short speech became immortal because of its warmth and its message. Its main ideas were:
- He said he was proud to belong to a religion that had taught the world both **tolerance and universal acceptance**.
- He recalled that India had given refuge to people persecuted in various countries — such as the Jews and the Parsis.
- Giving the example of an ancient hymn, he explained that just as rivers rising from different sources all finally merge into the same sea, so different paths reach the same goal.
- He expressed the hope that this convention would prove to be the death-knell of sectarianism, bigotry and fanaticism.

## After the Parliament

The Parliament continued until 27 September 1893, and during this time Vivekananda spoke several times, among which the 'Paper on Hinduism' of 19 September is especially famous. American newspapers counted him among the most popular speakers of the Parliament.

After this, for about four years he continued to lecture on Vedanta and yoga in America and England and founded the Vedanta Society in New York. When he returned to India in 1897 he received a historic welcome from Colombo to Almora.

## Legacy

On 1 May 1897 he founded the **Ramakrishna Mission** in Calcutta, which still works today in the fields of education, health and disaster relief. On 4 July 1902, at the age of only 39, he died at Belur Math.

The importance of the Chicago speech was not only religious. In the colonial period, when the West regarded India as backward, an Indian monk presented India's spiritual tradition with confidence on the world stage. This awakened self-respect among Indians, which later inspired many leaders of the national movement.
