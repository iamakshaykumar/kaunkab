## From a Gupta General to a Dynasty

In the latter half of the 5th century, when the Gupta empire was growing weak, **Bhattarka**, a military officer of the Guptas in Saurashtra (today's Kathiawar, Gujarat), established his own independent rule around **475 CE**. His dynasty came to be called the **Maitraka**, and its capital was **Valabhi**.

The early Maitraka rulers held only the title of **"Senapati"** (general) and acknowledged Gupta overlordship in name only. The third ruler, Dronasimha, took the title "Maharaja", and later rulers such as **Dharasena II** adopted higher titles. The Maitraka kings were mostly Shaivas, but they made gifts to Buddhist, Jain and Brahmin institutions alike.

## Valabhi, the Port City

Valabhi was a port city near the Gulf of Khambhat, engaged in international trade. The prosperity that came from trade gave the wealthy citizens and kings here the means to spend on education and religion. Many **copper plates** of the Maitrakas have been found, recording gifts of villages and land to monasteries, temples and Brahmins. Many of these grants were made for the buildings of Buddhist monasteries, the food and clothing of monks, and the upkeep of **books**.

## A Centre of Buddhist Learning

In the monasteries of Valabhi, the influence of Hinayana (Nikaya) schools of Buddhism such as the **Sammitiya** was strong. Two famous teachers here were **Gunamati** and **Sthiramati**, whose names are also linked with Nalanda. The Chinese traveller **Xuanzang**, who came in the middle of the 7th century, mentioned about **a hundred monasteries** and **more than six thousand monks** at Valabhi.

Besides Buddhism, it is said that practical subjects such as statecraft, law, economics and administration, as well as Brahmanical texts, were also taught here. However, this information has been pieced together from scattered sources, and unlike Nalanda, systematic excavation of the monasteries of Valabhi has not yet brought out much detail.

## Yijing's Testimony

The Chinese monk **Yijing**, who came to India in the last quarter of the 7th century, wrote that in India, **Nalanda** and **Valabhi** were the two places that could be compared with the most famous centres of learning in China. According to him, capable students stayed here for two or three years to study, then proved their ability by debating with scholars and gained recognition at royal courts.

## Jainism and the Valabhi Council

Valabhi also holds a special place in Jain history. According to Jain tradition, a council (the **Valabhi Vachana**) was held here under the presidency of Acharya **Devardhigani Kshamashramana**, in which the Shvetambara Jain Agamas were for the first time systematically **written down**. Tradition places it 980 or 993 years after Mahavira's nirvana, that is, around 453 or 466 CE, but scholars disagree about its exact date.

## End and Significance

In the 7th century the Maitrakas were for some time under the influence of **Harshavardhana** — the Maitraka king **Dhruvasena II** was married to Harsha's daughter — but later became independent again. In the 8th century, repeated Arab naval attacks weakened the kingdom, and around **776 CE** Maitraka rule came to an end (some scholars place this as late as 783 CE). The direct cause of Valabhi's fall is not clear in any contemporary source; its connection with the Arab invasions rests mainly on later traditions.

Valabhi is a reminder that higher education in ancient India was not limited to Nalanda — on the western coast too, a great centre of learning flourished on the strength of trade and royal patronage.
