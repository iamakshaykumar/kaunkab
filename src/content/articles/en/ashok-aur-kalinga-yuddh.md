## Background

**Ashoka** was the son of the Mauryan emperor Bindusara and the grandson of Chandragupta Maurya. After becoming king he continued to expand the empire. **Kalinga** (present-day Odisha) on the eastern coast was a prosperous and independent kingdom that had not yet come under the Mauryas.

## The War

In the eighth year of his reign, about 261 BCE, Ashoka invaded Kalinga. According to Ashoka's own **Thirteenth Rock Edict**, about one hundred thousand people were killed in this war and one and a half hundred thousand were taken away as captives. Far more people were affected by the hardships that followed the war.

These numbers may be exaggerated, but the most remarkable thing is that a victorious king himself had his **remorse** over his victory inscribed — this has happened very rarely in history.

## The Path of Dhamma

After Kalinga, Ashoka gave up the policy of conquest by war and made the spread of **dhamma** (dharma) his goal. Ashoka's dhamma was not a new religion but a set of rules for moral life:

- Respect for parents and elders
- Compassion towards all living beings
- Tolerance towards all sects
- Good treatment of slaves and servants

Ashoka himself was drawn towards Buddhism. According to tradition, he sent his son **Mahendra** and daughter **Sanghamitta** to Sri Lanka to spread Buddhism.

## The Inscriptions

Ashoka had his messages engraved on rocks and pillars, which are found today in India, Nepal, Pakistan and Afghanistan. Most of the inscriptions are in the **Prakrit** language and **Brahmi** script. They are among the oldest legible written documents of India.

## Significance

Independent India made the **lion capital** of the Ashoka Pillar at Sarnath its national emblem and gave a place to the **Ashoka Chakra** in the tricolour. Ashoka is still remembered today as a ruler who chose compassion over power.
