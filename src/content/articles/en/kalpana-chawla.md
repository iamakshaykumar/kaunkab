## "Montu" of Karnal

**Kalpana Chawla** was born in the city of **Karnal** in Haryana. Her official date of birth is **17 March 1962**. At home everyone affectionately called her **"Montu"**. From childhood she was fascinated by the sky and aeroplanes; she would watch the small aircraft flying over the Karnal flying club for hours.

She studied at Tagore Bal Niketan School in Karnal and then earned a degree in **aeronautical engineering** from **Punjab Engineering College** in **Chandigarh** in 1982. Very few girls studied this subject at the time, but Kalpana chose her own path.

## America and the Road to NASA

She went to the **United States** for further studies. She earned an M.S. in aerospace engineering from the **University of Texas at Arlington** in 1984 and a Ph.D. from the **University of Colorado Boulder** in 1988. In America she met flying instructor **Jean-Pierre Harrison**, whom she married. Kalpana was herself a skilled pilot and held licences to fly several kinds of aircraft.

In 1988 she began working at **NASA's Ames Research Center**, where she did computer-based research on the flow of air around aircraft. In 1991 she became an American citizen. In **1994** NASA chose her from among thousands of applicants for astronaut training, and in 1995 she joined the astronaut corps.

## First Flight: STS-87 (1997)

On **19 November 1997** the space shuttle **Columbia** lifted off on mission **STS-87**. Kalpana was a **mission specialist** on it and the prime operator of the robotic arm. With this she became the **first woman of Indian origin to go into space**.

On this mission the shuttle made **252 orbits** of the Earth in **more than 15 days** and covered about 6.5 million miles. Using the robotic arm, Kalpana released the **Spartan** satellite, which was to study the outer atmosphere of the Sun. The satellite did not work properly and later had to be captured by a spacewalk. NASA's investigation made clear that the fault lay in software and procedures, and Kalpana was not held responsible. The mission ended on **5 December 1997**. Her success was widely celebrated in India.

## Second Flight: STS-107 (2003)

Kalpana's second flight was postponed several times, and finally on **16 January 2003** **Columbia** set off again on mission **STS-107**. This was a 16-day science mission in which the seven crew members, working in day and night shifts, carried out about **80 experiments**. Kalpana was the **flight engineer**. The other crew members were commander **Rick Husband**, pilot **William McCool**, **Michael Anderson**, **David Brown**, **Laurel Clark** and Israel's first astronaut, **Ilan Ramon**.

About 82 seconds after launch, a **piece of foam insulation** broke off the shuttle's external fuel tank and struck the leading edge of the left wing. At the time it was not considered a serious danger.

## 1 February 2003: The Columbia Disaster

Having completed its mission, Columbia was returning to Earth on **1 February 2003**. During re-entry into the atmosphere, hot gases at thousands of degrees entered through the damaged part of that same wing. About 16 minutes before it was due to land in Florida, the shuttle broke apart over **Texas**, and **all seven astronauts were killed**. Kalpana was 40 years old at the time.

The **Columbia Accident Investigation Board**, which investigated the disaster, named the damage from the foam strike as the direct cause and also criticised NASA's working culture and safety procedures. After this, shuttle flights remained suspended for about two and a half years. Over her two missions Kalpana had spent a total of about **30 days and 14 hours** in space.

## Memory and Inspiration

Kalpana Chawla was posthumously awarded the American **Congressional Space Medal of Honor**, the NASA Space Flight Medal and the NASA Distinguished Service Medal. India named its first weather satellite **"Kalpana-1"**. A NASA supercomputer, an asteroid, many hostels, scholarships and a cargo spacecraft were also named after her. The government medical college in Karnal, Haryana, also bears her name.

Kalpana Chawla's story became an inspiration, especially for the girls of India — a girl from a small town who dared to dream and worked hard to fulfil those dreams. A well-known saying of hers is that there is always a path from dreams to success; you only need the vision to find it, the courage to walk it and the perseverance to stay on it.
