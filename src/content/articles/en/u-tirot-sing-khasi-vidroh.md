## The Khasi Hills and Nongkhlaw

In the **Khasi Hills** of today's **Meghalaya**, in the early nineteenth century there were many small Khasi states, whose rulers were called **syiem**. A syiem was not an absolute king — he ruled together with a council (**durbar**) of representatives of different clans. **U Tirot Sing** (born about **1802**) was the syiem of one such state, **Khadsawphra**, whose centre was **Nongkhlaw**. (In the Khasi language "U" is a respectful term placed before men's names.)

## The Question of the Road

In **1826**, after the First Anglo-Burmese War, the Brahmaputra Valley (Assam) came into British hands. Now the East India Company wanted a road to be built through the Khasi Hills directly connecting **Guwahati** in Assam with **Sylhet** in Bengal. Behind this plan was **David Scott**, the Governor-General's agent on the north-east frontier.

Tirot Sing permitted this road with the consent of his durbar. In return he hoped that his claim to some **duars** (the passes descending from the hills to the plains and the fertile land around them) adjoining the Assam plains would be recognised. For about a year and a half the work went well and British bungalows were even built at Nongkhlaw.

## Conflict and the Attack of 1829

Disputes soon arose. When Tirot Sing went to assert his right over the duars, **Balram Singh**, the Raja of Rani, opposed it, and the Company's soldiers blocked the path of Tirot Sing's armed party. Khasi leaders felt that under the pretext of the road the British were establishing permanent control of their territory. The Khasi durbar ordered the British to leave Nongkhlaw, but this was ignored.

On **4 April 1829** Khasi warriors attacked the British post at Nongkhlaw. In it two British officers — **Lieutenant Bedingfield** and **Lieutenant Burlton** — were killed (according to one contemporary account, Burlton died the next day, 5 April, while fleeing). David Scott had left for Cherrapunji (Sohra) a little earlier, and so escaped.

## Four Years of Guerrilla War

After this attack the Company sent troops and the **Anglo-Khasi War** began. The Khasi warriors mainly had **swords, shields and bows and arrows**, while the British had guns and trained soldiers. So Tirot Sing and his companions adopted **guerrilla warfare** instead of face-to-face battle — using the dense forests, deep valleys and hill paths to make sudden attacks and then vanish. The struggle lasted about **four years**.

During the fighting Tirot Sing was wounded and hid in **caves** near Nongkhlaw; these caves are still part of local memory. Finally the British lured a local man with money to find out his hiding place, and on **9 January 1833** he had to surrender.

## Last Days in Dhaka

After a trial Tirot Sing was sent to **Dhaka**. According to the research of the historian David R. Syiemlieh, he received an allowance there like a native ruler and, though in detention, could travel around the city by palanquin. He died in Dhaka itself on **17 July 1835**; he is believed to have suffered from a stomach ailment. Accounts differ somewhat on the exact conditions of his detention.

## Memory

The result of the war was British dominance over the Khasi Hills, but Tirot Sing remained one of Meghalaya's most respected heroes. **17 July** is a state holiday in Meghalaya as **"U Tirot Sing Day"**, a prestigious state arts and literature award is given in his name, and there is a statue of him at Nongkhlaw. His struggle is a reminder that resistance to British rule was taking place long before 1857, even in the remote hill regions of the country.
