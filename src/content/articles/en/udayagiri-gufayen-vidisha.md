## The Hill of Sunrise

A few kilometres west of the city of **Vidisha** in Madhya Pradesh, on the bank of the **Bes** River and near the **Betwa** River, is a lone sandstone hill called **Udayagiri** — that is, "the hill of sunrise". The hill is about **2.5 kilometres** long and is about 11 kilometres from **Sanchi** and about 60 kilometres from Bhopal. The roughly **20 caves** cut into its rock are a unique source for understanding the art, religion and politics of the Gupta period together. Inscriptions here also give an old name of the hill, **Vishnupadagiri** (the hill with Vishnu's feet).

Most of the caves are small — many are just a sanctum with a carved doorway in front. Of these, nineteen are dedicated to Hindu deities — Vishnu, Shiva and the Goddess — and one to a Jain Tirthankara. Today they are monuments protected by the **Archaeological Survey of India (ASI)**.

## Inscriptions: The Oldest Dated Cave Temples

The greatest importance of Udayagiri is that some of its caves bear **dated inscriptions**, which allow them to be placed in a definite period.

- Near **Cave 6** is a Sanskrit inscription of **Gupta year 82** (about **401 CE**), which mentions the donation of this cave temple during the reign of **Chandragupta II** (Vikramaditya).
- On the back part of **Cave 8** is an undated five-line inscription. In it Chandragupta's minister **Virasena** says that he had this cave made in devotion to Shiva and that he came here with the king during his campaign of conquest.
- **Cave 20** — the only **Jain cave** here, dedicated to the Tirthankara **Parshvanatha** — has an inscription of **425 CE**, believed to be from the time of **Kumaragupta I**.

These inscriptions make it clear that the main Hindu caves here were made in the last decades of the fourth century and the early fifth century. For this reason they are counted among the oldest dated Hindu cave temples in India.

## The Varaha Image in Cave 5

The most famous work at Udayagiri is **Cave 5**. In fact it is less a cave than a shallow, wide niche cut into the rock, in which a huge image of Vishnu's **Varaha avatar** is carved. The Varaha, with a boar's face and a man's body, is shown lifting and rescuing **Goddess Earth (Bhudevi)** from the ocean of the great deluge. Near his feet are serpent deities, and on the back wall stand rows of gods, sages and other figures.

Many historians see this scene as not only religious but also a **political message** — as Varaha saved the Earth, so the Gupta emperor "saved" his kingdom from enemies. Some scholars link it to Chandragupta II's victory over the Shaka satraps of western India. But this is an interpretation, not a proven fact; the king's own name is not written on the image.

## Other Major Caves

- **Cave 4** has an **ekamukha linga** — a Shiva linga on which one face of Shiva is carved. Above its doorway is also a figure playing a veena-like instrument, counted among the early depictions of this instrument in India.
- **Cave 6** has Durga in her **Mahishasuramardini** form, Ganesha and the Matrikas — that is, the Shaiva, Vaishnava and Shakta traditions, all in one place.
- **Cave 13** has an image about 3.6 metres long of Vishnu reclining on Shesha (**Anantashayi** or Sheshashayi Vishnu). A kneeling figure below is generally taken to be Chandragupta II, though this too is only a guess.
- **Cave 19** is the largest cave, with four heavy pillars and a scene of the **Samudra Manthan** (churning of the ocean). It also has a later inscription of about 1037 CE, showing that pilgrims kept coming here centuries later.

## Discovery and Significance

In the nineteenth century **Alexander Cunningham** studied these caves in detail in the 1870s and described them in the ASI reports. Later the number of caves was raised to 20.

The geography of Udayagiri is also special. It is a little north of today's **Tropic of Cancer**, while fifteen hundred years ago the Tropic of Cancer was closer to it. Some scholars believe that this place may also have been a centre of astronomy and calendar calculation. Many scholars also identify the 'Vishnupada' hill, on which the inscription of Delhi's **iron pillar** speaks of setting up a standard-pillar, with Udayagiri; but this identification is not universally accepted.

On the whole, Udayagiri shows how temple art was developing in the Gupta period — with small caves, carved doorways and huge narrative images, in which the message of religion and royal power can be read together.
