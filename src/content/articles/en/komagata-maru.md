## Background: The "Continuous Journey" Rule

In the early twentieth century many people from Punjab, especially Sikh farmers and ex-soldiers, were going to Canada and the United States in search of work. India and Canada were both parts of the British Empire, yet the Canadian government wanted to stop Indians from coming. For this an order was introduced on **8 January 1908**, known as the **"continuous journey" rule**. According to it, only a person who had come by a direct, non-stop journey from his country of birth could land in Canada. At that time no direct ship ran from India to Canada at all, so in practice this rule was a way of keeping Indians out. A condition that Asian immigrants must carry 200 dollars had also been imposed.

## Gurdit Singh and the "Guru Nanak Jahaz"

**Baba Gurdit Singh** (of Sarhali), who did business in Singapore and Malaya, decided to challenge this rule. In **March 1914** he chartered the Japanese steamship **Komagata Maru** at Hong Kong and renamed it the **"Guru Nanak Jahaz"**. The ship left Hong Kong on **4 April 1914**, and more passengers boarded at the ports of Shanghai and Japan on the way. When it left Yokohama on **3 May 1914**, the ship carried **376 passengers** — **340 Sikhs, 24 Muslims and 12 Hindus**, almost all from Punjab.

## Two Months of Siege at Vancouver

On **23 May 1914** the ship reached Burrard Inlet at Vancouver, but it was not allowed to dock. The passengers were held on the ship for about **two months**. For some time even the supply of food and water was stopped. The Indians settled in Vancouver collected funds and fought a case in court through a lawyer, but on **6 July 1914** the Court of Appeal of British Columbia gave its decision against the passengers. Only about **20 passengers**, who were already residents of Canada, were allowed to land (the number is given in sources as between 20 and 24).

To force the ship out, the police and then the naval ship **HMCS Rainbow** were brought. Finally, on **23 July 1914**, the Komagata Maru left Vancouver under naval escort. Meanwhile **the First World War** had begun in Europe.

## The Budge Budge Firing (29 September 1914)

British officials considered these passengers dangerous, because in North America the **Ghadar Party** was campaigning against British rule. The ship was not allowed to go up to the city of Calcutta, and on **29 September 1914** it was stopped at **Budge Budge** on the Hooghly river, some distance from Calcutta. The officials wanted to send the passengers directly to Punjab by a special train. Many passengers, led by Gurdit Singh, wanted to go to Calcutta first. When the police tried to arrest some leaders there was a clash and the soldiers opened fire.

Sources do not agree on the number of dead. According to Parks Canada and the Canadian Encyclopedia, about **20 passengers** were killed; official records give the total dead (including some policemen) as up to 26, while some eyewitnesses claimed far more deaths. **More than 200** passengers were jailed without trial or placed under house arrest in their villages. Gurdit Singh escaped and remained in hiding for years; later, on **Gandhi's** advice, he surrendered and was sentenced to five years' imprisonment.

## Impact and Memory

The Komagata Maru incident showed that the British Empire's promise of "equal subjects" was hollow for Indians. It increased anger against the British among overseas Indians and in Punjab, and gave new energy to the **Ghadar movement**.

The Government of India built a martyrs' memorial at Budge Budge in **1952**, inaugurated by **Jawaharlal Nehru**; the martyrs are remembered here every year on 29 September. On the centenary of the incident in **2014**, India issued commemorative coins and Canada Post issued a postage stamp. In Canada, in **2008** the Legislative Assembly of British Columbia and Prime Minister Stephen Harper expressed regret, and on **18 May 2016** Prime Minister **Justin Trudeau** made a formal apology in Parliament.
