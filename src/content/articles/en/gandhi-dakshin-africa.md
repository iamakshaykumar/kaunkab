## A Court Case, an Insult

The young barrister **Mohandas Gandhi**, who had failed to build a law practice in India, left for South Africa in April 1893 for a court case of the firm of the Gujarati merchant **Dada Abdulla**. He reached **Durban** in Natal in May 1893. A few days later, on the night of **7 June 1893**, while travelling to Pretoria, he was thrown out of the compartment at **Pietermaritzburg** station merely because of his colour, even though he held a valid first-class ticket. Spending the cold night in the waiting room, he decided that he would not run from injustice but face it. Gandhi himself regarded this incident as the turning point of his life.

## The Natal Indian Congress and 'Indian Opinion'

After the case was settled Gandhi was about to return, but he stayed on to oppose a bill that would take away the voting rights of Indians in Natal. On **22 August 1894** the **Natal Indian Congress** was formed, with Gandhi as its honorary secretary. Through petitions, letters and newspapers he put forward the case of Indian merchants and indentured labourers. In 1903 the weekly **'Indian Opinion'** was started, printed in several languages including English and Gujarati.

In this period Gandhi hoped to win rights by showing loyalty to the British Empire. In the Boer War (1899–1902) he raised an Indian ambulance corps, and during the Zulu (Bambatha) rebellion of 1906 he also took a stretcher-bearer corps to carry the wounded.

## Phoenix and Tolstoy Farm

Influenced by John Ruskin's book 'Unto This Last', Gandhi founded the **Phoenix Settlement** near Durban in 1904, where everyone did manual labour together and printed 'Indian Opinion'. In 1910 his friend **Hermann Kallenbach**, an architect of German origin, gave land near Johannesburg, which was named **Tolstoy Farm**. Here the families of satyagrahis lived together simply, farmed and taught the children. These experiments in community living later became the foundation of the Sabarmati and Sevagram ashrams in India.

## 1906: The First Satyagraha

In 1906 the Transvaal government proposed an ordinance requiring compulsory registration, including fingerprints, of Asians, which Indians called the 'Black Act'. At a meeting held at the Empire Theatre in Johannesburg on **11 September 1906**, people took an oath not to obey it and to go to jail for it. This was the start of the non-violent resistance that was later named **'satyagraha'**. Gandhi and hundreds of Indians went to jail many times; after the 1908 agreement with General **Jan Smuts** and its breakdown, registration certificates were burnt publicly.

## The Great Campaign of 1913 and the Return

The movement reached its peak in 1913. Women also went to jail in protest against the **annual tax of 3 pounds** imposed on Indians freed from indenture and a court ruling that declared non-Christian marriages invalid — Kasturba was among them. In November 1913 Gandhi led striking workers from the coal mines of Newcastle on a **march on foot** towards the Transvaal. As pressure grew, talks followed, and in June 1914 the **Indian Relief Act** was passed, which abolished the 3-pound tax and recognised marriages performed by Indian rites. On **18 July 1914** Gandhi left Cape Town for India by way of England, and reached Bombay on 9 January 1915.

## Legacy and Debate

South Africa made Gandhi a leader, an organiser and a thinker; 'Hind Swaraj' (1909) was also written in this period. But historians also remind us that his struggle then was mainly limited to the rights of Indians, and that some of his remarks about Africans in the early years reflect the racial prejudice of that era. Even so, leaders such as Nelson Mandela regarded Gandhi's non-violent resistance as an inspiration, and a memorial plaque in his memory stands today at Pietermaritzburg station.
