## One Pillar, Many Eras

Inside the fort of Prayagraj (Allahabad) stands a highly polished sandstone pillar about 10.7 metres tall. The Mauryan emperor **Ashoka**'s six pillar edicts were engraved on it first. It also bears Ashoka's "Schism" edict (addressed to the officials of Kaushambi) and the "Queen's Edict", which mentions the gift of Queen **Karuvaki**.

Many scholars believe the pillar was originally at nearby **Kaushambi** and was brought here later; some think it was here from the start. It is also said that Akbar had it brought into the fort, but there is no solid proof of this.

## Harishena's Prashasti

About six hundred years after Ashoka, a long inscription in praise of the Gupta emperor **Samudragupta** was engraved on this same pillar. It is called the **Prayag Prashasti** or the Allahabad Pillar inscription. It was composed by **Harishena**, Samudragupta's court poet and senior official, who also describes himself as "Sandhivigrahika" (minister of war and peace).

- The language is **Sanskrit** and the script is **Gupta-period Brahmi**.
- It is written in both verse and prose, so it is regarded as an early example of the Sanskrit "champu" style.
- According to the inscription, his father **Chandragupta I** embraced Samudragupta in a full assembly, chose him as successor, and told him to "protect the earth".

Some scholars have argued that the prashasti was engraved after Samudragupta's death, but most consider it to date from his lifetime.

## The Geography of Conquests

The most important part of the prashasti is the account of Samudragupta's campaigns, which shows a different policy towards different regions:
- **Aryavarta (northern India)** — Kings such as Rudradeva, Matila, Nagadatta, Chandravarman, Ganapatinaga, Nagasena, Achyuta and Balavarman were "uprooted", and their kingdoms were directly merged into the Gupta Empire.
- **Dakshinapatha** — Twelve kings (such as Mahendra of Kosala and Vishnugopa, the Pallava king of Kanchi) were defeated and captured, then released and their kingdoms restored.
- **Atavika states** — The kings of the forest regions were subjugated.
- **Frontier states** — Samatata, Davaka, Kamarupa, Nepal and Kartripura, and tribal republics (ganas) such as the Malavas, Yaudheyas, Arjunayanas, Madrakas and Abhiras, submitted by paying tribute and obeying orders.
- **Distant rulers** — Rulers such as the "Daivaputra-Shahi-Shahanushahi", the Shaka-Murundas, and the rulers of islands like Simhala (Sri Lanka) offered gifts and service. Scholars disagree on the exact identification of these names (for example, whether they were Kushana or Sasanian rulers).

The prashasti says Samudragupta fought a hundred battles. Since this is a document of royal praise, exaggeration is natural; it is hard to treat "gifts" from distant rulers as true submission.

## The Poet-King

The prashasti describes Samudragupta not only as a warrior but also as a poet and lover of music, and calls him "Kaviraja". On some of his gold coins he is shown playing the veena, and some coins were issued to commemorate his Ashvamedha sacrifice. The twentieth-century historian Vincent Smith called him the "Napoleon of India" because of his conquests, though many historians today do not find such comparisons useful.

## Later History and Study

Before Jahangir came to the throne (1605), when he was Prince Salim, a Mughal genealogy in Persian was engraved on this pillar, which damaged some of Ashoka's inscriptions. It also carries a short inscription about Birbal's pilgrimage. Around 1834 **James Prinsep** studied the inscriptions of this pillar, then lying fallen in the fort, which proved very helpful in deciphering the Brahmi script. Later, **John Faithfull Fleet** published an authoritative text and translation of the prashasti in 1888. Today the pillar lies in the fort area under army control, so special permission is needed to see it.

## Significance

The Prayag Prashasti is a political map of 4th-century India: it shows which kingdoms and republics existed at the time and how the Guptas established dominance over them in different ways. The fact that Ashoka's message of dhamma and Samudragupta's tale of conquest stand on one and the same pillar also offers an interesting comparison of two different ideals in Indian history.
