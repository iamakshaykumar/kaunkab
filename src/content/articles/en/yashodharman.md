## The Age of the Huns

At the end of the 5th century the **Huns** (whom historians today often call the 'Alchon Huns'), who had come from Central Asia, had spread over north-western India. Skandagupta had once checked them, but after that the Gupta empire kept growing weaker. The Hun ruler **Toramana** reached Central India, and his son **Mihirakula** became the most dreaded name in North India at the beginning of the 6th century. According to the Chinese traveller Xuanzang his capital was **Shakala** (today's Sialkot), and Buddhist tradition describes him as a destroyer of monasteries and stupas.

## The Aulikara Dynasty of Dashapura

**Dashapura** (today's Mandsaur) was an old city in Malwa. It was ruled by a local dynasty called the **Aulikaras**, which for a long time was a feudatory of the Guptas. According to the Risthal inscription, **Prakashadharma** of this same dynasty had defeated the Hun ruler Toramana. After this **Yashodharman** came forward, who is also called **Vishnuvardhana** in inscriptions. Some scholars regard Yashodharman and Vishnuvardhana as two different persons, but most regard them as one and the same king. The exact years of his rule are not known.

## The Victory at Sondhni (c. 528 CE)

Yashodharman's greatest achievement was the **victory over Mihirakula**, which is generally placed **around 528 CE**. The pillar inscription standing at **Sondhni** near Mandsaur claims that even Mihirakula had to bow his head at his feet. This victory greatly reduced the power of the Huns, and Mihirakula's influence shrank toward Kashmir and Gandhara.

It is worth noting that Xuanzang tells a different story: according to him, the king of Magadha, **Baladitya** (generally taken to be the Gupta emperor Narasimhagupta), took Mihirakula prisoner and then released him. Historians reconcile these two accounts in different ways — some believe that several powers fought against the Huns on different fronts.

## The Mandsaur Inscriptions and the Victory Pillars

Almost all information about Yashodharman comes from three inscriptions:
- **The two victory pillars of Sondhni** — tall monolithic sandstone pillars, on which almost the same text is engraved. Their poetry was composed by the poet **Vasula**.
- **The Mandsaur inscription** — dated **Malava Samvat 589**, that is, **532 CE**. It mentions the building of a well and praises the victories of Yashodharman-Vishnuvardhana.

These inscriptions were published in the 19th century by J. F. Fleet in his famous collection (1888). Art historians regard the pillars of Sondhni and the sculptures found there as a link between Gupta art and later medieval art.

## Claims and Reality

The pillar inscription makes very large claims — that Yashodharman conquered even those regions which neither the Guptas nor the Huns could enjoy, and that his kingdom stretched from the Lauhitya (Brahmaputra) to the western sea and from the Himalayas to Mount Mahendra. Historians regard this as the **exaggeration of a praise-poem**. The 'northern and eastern' kings said to have been defeated are not named in the inscriptions, and no independent evidence of these conquests is found.

## After Yashodharman

Yashodharman's empire broke up as quickly as it had been built. No solid account of his successors is found, and within a few decades the influence of the Kalachuris and other powers came over Malwa. In North India the Maukharis, the Later Guptas and then the Vardhana dynasty rose next.

Even so, Yashodharman's importance is lasting. He dealt a decisive blow to the growing power of the Huns and showed that after the Guptas grew weak, regional dynasties too could play a large role. His inscriptions are also important in the history of Indian time-reckoning because of their use of the 'Malava Samvat'.
