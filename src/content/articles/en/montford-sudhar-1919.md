## Background: War and Promises

In **World War I** India gave Britain a huge number of soldiers and a lot of money. At the same time the **Home Rule Movement** was going on in the country, and through the **Lucknow Pact** of 1916 the Congress and the Muslim League had come onto one platform. The British government was under pressure to give Indians some share in governance.

In this atmosphere the Secretary of State for India, **Edwin Montagu**, announced on **20 August 1917** in the British Parliament (House of Commons) that the aim of British policy was the increasing association of Indians in every branch of the administration and the **gradual development of self-governing institutions**, so as to establish step by step **"responsible government"** in India within the British Empire. This is also called the **"August Declaration"**.

## The Montagu-Chelmsford Report (1918)

In the winter of 1917-18 Montagu came to India and, with Viceroy **Lord Chelmsford** (Viceroy 1916-1921), met leaders across the country. Their joint report was published in the summer of **1918**. On this basis the British Parliament passed the **Government of India Act 1919**, which received royal assent on **23 December 1919**. The first elections under the new system were held in **1921**.

## What Was Dyarchy?

The most famous provision of this Act was **dyarchy** in the provinces. The subjects of provincial government were divided into two parts:

- **Transferred subjects** — such as education, public health, agriculture and local self-government. These were handed over to Indian ministers, who were responsible to the elected legislative council.
- **Reserved subjects** — such as law and order, police, finance and land revenue. These remained with the governor and his executive council, who were responsible not to the legislature but to the British government.

That is, the important departments and the key to the money stayed in British hands, while Indian ministers received departments with limited resources. The governor could even ignore the ministers' advice. For this reason critics said that the ministers had responsibility but no real power.

## Changes at the Centre

There was no dyarchy at the Centre, but the legislature was divided into **two houses**:

- **Central Legislative Assembly** (lower house) — 145 members, of whom 104 were elected and 41 nominated.
- **Council of State** (upper house) — 60 members, of whom 34 were elected.

It was decided that **three of the eight members** of the Viceroy's Executive Council would be **Indians**. Even so, the Viceroy retained the veto and special powers. The franchise was based on property and tax, so only about **5 million** people could vote. The **separate electorates** given to Muslims in the Morley-Minto Reforms of 1909 were extended further to Sikhs, Europeans, Anglo-Indians and Indian Christians. The Act made provision for a **Public Service Commission** in India for the first time and provided for a review of the reforms after ten years. As part of these reforms a **Chamber of Princes** of 108 members was also created for the rulers of the princely states, but it had no real power and remained only an advisory body.

## Reaction and Significance

Nationalists considered the reforms **inadequate**. The **Rowlatt Act** that came in 1919 itself and the **Jallianwala Bagh massacre** of April 1919 made the atmosphere more bitter. In 1920 the Congress adopted the path of Gandhiji's **Non-Cooperation Movement** and boycotted the elections of 1920-21. On the other hand, many Liberal (Moderate) leaders such as Surendranath Banerjee decided to try out the reforms and separated from the Congress.

Historians evaluate these reforms in different ways. Some regard them as a show of sharing power, while according to others they gave Indians for the first time the experience of ministerial office and electoral politics. As per the fixed review, the **Simon Commission** was formed in 1927, and later the **Government of India Act 1935** ended dyarchy in the provinces.
