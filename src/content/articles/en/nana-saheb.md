## The Peshwa's Adopted Son

**Nana Saheb**'s childhood name was **Dhondu Pant**. He is believed to have been born on **19 May 1824**. After defeat in the Third Anglo-Maratha War (1818), the last Peshwa, **Baji Rao II**, was removed from Poona and sent to **Bithur** near Kanpur, where he received a pension of about **8 lakh rupees** a year from the Company. The childless Baji Rao adopted Dhondu Pant in **1827**. **Tatya Tope**, and in her childhood Manu (later **Rani Lakshmibai**), also lived at this court in Bithur.

## The Pension Dispute and Azimullah Khan

Baji Rao II died in **1851**. The government of Governor-General **Lord Dalhousie** argued that the pension had been only for Baji Rao's own lifetime, so the adopted son had no right to it. Nana Saheb kept the Bithur estate, but not the pension or the title of Peshwa. He sent his dewan **Azimullah Khan** to **London** with the case, but in **1853–54** that too ended in disappointment. This humiliation is considered a major reason for his stand in 1857, although until the revolt he kept up friendly contact with British officers.

## The Kanpur Rising and the Siege of Wheeler

When news of Meerut and Delhi spread in **May 1857**, the Collector of Kanpur actually asked Nana Saheb for help in guarding the treasury. But on **5 June 1857** the sepoys at Kanpur rose in revolt. After the treasury and magazine were seized, Nana Saheb accepted leadership of the rebels. It is said that on Azimullah Khan's advice he chose to hold Kanpur instead of going to Delhi.

From **6 June** General **Hugh Wheeler**'s makeshift entrenchment was besieged; it held about **900** people: soldiers, officers, merchants, women and children. For about three weeks scorching heat, lack of water, disease and constant shelling steadily worsened conditions inside. On **25 June** an offer of safe passage came from Nana Saheb's side, and Wheeler accepted it.

## Satichaura Ghat and Bibighar

On the morning of **27 June 1857** the British reached **Satichaura Ghat** on the Ganga to leave by boat for Allahabad. Firing suddenly broke out there and most of the men were killed; about 120 surviving women and children were taken prisoner. Historians do not agree on who started the firing or whether it was planned in advance; there is no conclusive evidence that Nana Saheb planned it.

On **1 July** Nana Saheb was formally proclaimed **Peshwa** at Bithur. Meanwhile General **Henry Havelock**'s army was advancing from Allahabad towards Kanpur. After the defeat of the force of Nana Saheb's brother **Bala Saheb**, on **15 July 1857** about 200 women and children held captive in a house called the **Bibighar** were killed and their bodies thrown into a well. Accounts also differ on who gave this order: some blame Nana Saheb's advisers, while British writers held Nana Saheb directly responsible.

On **16 July** Havelock recaptured Kanpur. Then, on the orders of Brigadier **James Neill**, very brutal reprisals followed: people were hanged without proper trial and there was heavy violence in nearby villages. "Remember Cawnpore" became a battle cry of British soldiers.

## Life Underground and a Mysterious End

After losing Kanpur, Nana Saheb left Bithur. In **November 1857** Tatya Tope attacked Kanpur again and even had some success for a few days, but in **December 1857** Sir Colin Campbell defeated him. That same month the British burnt the Bithur palace. The British put a reward of **one lakh rupees** on Nana Saheb. In 1858 he stayed with the rebels of Awadh and Rohilkhand, and by **1859** he had moved into the **Terai of Nepal** along with other leaders such as Begum Hazrat Mahal. The last confirmed letter in his name dates from **May 1859**.

There are many stories about his death: some accounts describe death from fever in the Terai in **September 1859**, some speak of 1860, and folk tales describe him living for years afterwards as a sadhu in Gujarat or at Naimisharanya. None of these has been firmly confirmed.

## Legacy

Nana Saheb was one of the 1857 leaders whose personal humiliation and political grievance gave leadership to the sepoys' revolt. In British writing he was long portrayed as a villain because of Bibighar, while the Indian nationalist tradition regarded him as a leading hero of the freedom struggle. Modern historians, moving away from both images, see the violence of Kanpur, on both sides, as part of the horror of war. Today the **Nana Saheb Memorial** at Bithur and **Nana Rao Park** in Kanpur keep his memory alive.
