## A Path Apart from Delhi

At the end of the fourteenth century the Tughluq Sultanate was growing weak, and Timur's attack in 1398 loosened Delhi's grip further. In this period the governor of Malwa, **Dilawar Khan Ghuri**, began in practice to rule independently, and in **1401** he took a royal title and laid the foundation of the **Malwa Sultanate**. The first capital was **Dhar**.

## Hoshang Shah and Mandu

Dilawar Khan's son **Hoshang Shah** (c. 1406–1435) made **Mandu** the capital. This plateau, at a height of about 633 metres on the Vindhya mountains, is surrounded by deep valleys on all sides, and the valley of the **Narmada** can be seen far away to the south — that is, nature itself had made it a fortress. A fort wall about 37 kilometres long and many gateways made it more secure still. The sultans named it **Shadiabad** — "the city of joy".

Hoshang Shah also suffered defeat and imprisonment in his conflict with the Sultan of Gujarat, but he returned and sat on the throne again. His white marble **tomb** is among the most beautiful buildings of Mandu.

## The Era of the Khalji Sultans

In **1436** the wazir **Mahmud Khalji** removed the Ghuri dynasty and founded the Khalji dynasty, which lasted until 1531. Mahmud Khalji (1436–1469) was an ambitious warrior and led many campaigns towards Gujarat, Mewar, Delhi and the Deccan. The huge **Jami Masjid** of Mandu, whose plan is said to have been inspired by the Great Mosque of Damascus, was completed in this period. In 1439 an illustrated copy of the Jain text "Kalpasutra" was also made at Mandu — which shows that the art traditions of several communities were running side by side here.

The reign of his son **Ghiyasuddin Khalji** (about 31 years from 1469) was relatively peaceful. The **Ni'matnama** manuscript of his time is famous. Tradition links the **Jahaz Mahal** with his harem — this long, narrow building, built between two lakes (Munja Talab and Kapur Talab), looks like a floating ship when the lakes are full. The nearby **Hindola Mahal** is called the "swinging palace" because of its slanting walls.

## Decline: Gujarat, the Surs and the Mughals

In the sixteenth century the sultanate was weakened by internal quarrels. The influence of the Rajput chief **Medini Rai** grew over **Mahmud Khalji II**, and in 1519, in the Battle of **Gagron**, **Rana Sanga** of Mewar defeated him and held him captive for some time. In the end, on **25 May 1531**, **Bahadur Shah** of Gujarat conquered Mandu and the Khalji dynasty came to an end.

After this Malwa was for a time under Humayun, and then from 1542 under **Sher Shah Suri**, who made **Shujaat Khan** governor. In 1555 Shujaat Khan's son **Baz Bahadur** declared himself an independent ruler.

## Baz Bahadur and Rupmati

On **29 March 1561**, in the Battle of **Sarangpur**, Akbar's commanders **Adham Khan** and Pir Muhammad Khan defeated Baz Bahadur. After another defeat in 1562 he fled, and Malwa became a Mughal province with Ujjain as its centre. Later (1570) Baz Bahadur accepted service under Akbar.

Baz Bahadur's name is linked in folk tales with the singer **Rupmati**. According to the tale, Rupmati used to look at the Narmada every day from a high pavilion, and on hearing the news of Mandu's fall she took poison rather than fall into Adham Khan's hands. This love story comes from later poems and folk songs; many of its details are not historically proven. Today at Mandu the **Rupmati Pavilion**, the **Baz Bahadur Palace** and the **Rewa Kund** recall this tale.

## Legacy

The ruins of Mandu are today a major tourist site in Dhar district of Madhya Pradesh, where the Mandu Festival is held every winter. The simplicity of Afghan-Ghuri architecture, the strong stone walls and the palaces built beside the lakes bear witness to the culture of the Malwa Sultanate, which flourished for more than a century and a half.
