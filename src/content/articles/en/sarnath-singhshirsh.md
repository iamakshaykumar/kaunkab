## The Place of the Buddha's First Sermon

**Sarnath** near Varanasi (ancient Rishipattana Mrigadava) is the place where, after attaining enlightenment, the **Buddha** gave his first sermon to his five companions, called the "Dharmachakra-Pravartana" (setting in motion the wheel of dharma). The Mauryan emperor **Ashoka**, who turned to Buddhism after the Kalinga war, had a tall stone pillar erected here around **250 BCE** to mark this sacred place. The sculpture placed on its top is the **Lion Capital**.

## The Design: Lions, Animals and Wheel

About **2.1 metres** tall, this Lion Capital is carved from a **single block** of sandstone, and has such a shining polish that it looks like metal. This polish is regarded as a hallmark of Mauryan art; the stone is generally believed to come from the quarries of Chunar (Mirzapur). It has three parts:

- **At the top**: **four Asiatic lions** seated back to back, facing the four directions.
- **In the middle**: a round base (the **abacus**), on which four **wheels** and, between them, four animals (**elephant, bull, horse and lion**) are in relief.
- **At the bottom**: a bell-shaped form like an **inverted lotus**.

Originally above the lions there was also a large stone **Dharmachakra**, which now survives only in fragments. The lions are regarded as symbols both of the "lion's roar" of the Buddha's teaching and of royal authority, but scholars hold different views about the meaning of the whole sculpture and about Persian or Greek influence on it.

## Discovery

With time the pillar broke and fell, and the Lion Capital was buried in the ground. It was found again in **1904–05** in the excavation of the engineer-archaeologist **F. O. Oertel** for the Archaeological Survey of India. Today it is displayed in the **Sarnath Archaeological Museum**, while the broken lower part of the pillar stands at its original place. An inscription of Ashoka is also engraved on this pillar, warning monks who split the Buddhist Sangha.

## How It Became the National Emblem

At independence India needed symbols that belonged not to any one religion or community but showed the country's long history and moral ideals. The **wheel** of the Lion Capital was placed at the centre of the national flag, and the whole sculpture was chosen as the State Emblem. On the day India became a republic, **26 January 1950**, the government formally adopted it as the **State Emblem**.

In the State Emblem:

- **Three of the four lions** are visible.
- In the middle is the **wheel with 24 spokes**, with the **bull** to its right and the **horse** to its left.
- Below, **"Satyameva Jayate"** is written in Devanagari, taken from the **Mundaka Upanishad**.

To prevent misuse of this emblem, the **State Emblem of India (Prohibition of Improper Use) Act, 2005** was enacted.

## Sarnath Today

The Lion Capital appears today on every official paper of the Government of India, on coins, banknotes and passports. On **25 July 2026** the UNESCO World Heritage Committee inscribed the "Ancient Buddhist Site of Sarnath" on the **World Heritage List**; it became India's 45th World Heritage Site. Thus a Mauryan sculpture about 2,250 years old remains the identity of modern India even today.
