## Background: The Shah Mir Dynasty and a Difficult Inheritance

In the 14th century the sultanate of the **Shah Mir dynasty** was established in Kashmir. Zain-ul-Abidin was the son of Sultan **Sikandar** (reigned c. 1389–1413) of this dynasty. Under Sikandar's rule, especially under the influence of his minister Suha Bhatta, many temples were demolished and the jizya was imposed on non-Muslims. For this reason Sikandar was called **"But-shikan"** (idol-breaker) in later history. Many Kashmiri Pandits had left the valley.

In his youth Zain-ul-Abidin was sent with a diplomatic party to the court of **Timur** at **Samarkand** and stayed there for several years. This experience of Central Asian crafts and court culture later showed in his rule.

## The Struggle for the Throne

After Sikandar his elder son **Ali Shah** became sultan and Zain-ul-Abidin remained wazir. Around **1418**, when Ali Shah set out on a pilgrimage, the rule was handed over to Zain-ul-Abidin. When Ali Shah returned, Zain-ul-Abidin handed power back to him without opposition — historians disagree about why this happened.

After this a civil war broke out between the brothers. With the help of the Khokhar chief **Jasrat** of Punjab, Zain-ul-Abidin defeated Ali Shah near **Uri** and became sultan formally in **1420**. (Abul Fazl gives the date of the coronation as 1422.) The sources also say different things about the end of Ali Shah.

## The Policy of Tolerance

Zain-ul-Abidin reversed his father's policy to a large extent:

- The **jizya** on non-Muslims was abolished.
- Hindus who had left the valley were encouraged to return, the old grants of the Brahmins were restored and the rebuilding of temples was permitted.
- A ban was also placed on cow slaughter.

His contemporary Sanskrit historians **Jonaraja** and **Shrivara** praise him greatly. It should be remembered that both were associated with his court, so their accounts may contain some exaggeration.

## Learning and Translation

The sultan himself is regarded as knowledgeable in Persian, Kashmiri and Sanskrit. Under his patronage the **Mahabharata** and portions of Kalhana's **Rajatarangini** were translated into Persian; this translation of the Rajatarangini is known as "Bahr-ul-Asmar". On the other hand, Shrivara began a Sanskrit adaptation of the Persian poet Jami's "Yusuf and Zulaikha". In this way his court became a bridge between the Sanskrit and Persian traditions.

## Crafts, Building and Prosperity

On returning from Samarkand he brought with him artisans such as papermakers, bookbinders and carpet weavers. In the royal workshops crafts such as **papermaking**, **carpet weaving** and **papier-mache** developed, which are still the identity of Kashmir today.

He had canals and bridges built. The **Zaina Kadal** bridge of Srinagar and the **Zaina Lank** island in Wular Lake are linked with his name. His capital was **Naushahr**. Most of his wooden buildings have been destroyed, but in Srinagar the brick-built tomb of his mother (also called the "Tomb of Budshah") still stands today.

## Last Years and Legacy

In the last years of his life the quarrels among his sons greatly saddened the sultan. The sources also mention his illness and mental unrest. He died on **12 May 1470**, and his son **Haji Khan** took the throne under the name "Haidar Shah".

His rule of about fifty years is regarded as the golden age of Kashmir's history. Because of his religious tolerance, love of learning and patronage of crafts, the people of Kashmir still affectionately call him **"Budshah"** — that is, "the great king".
