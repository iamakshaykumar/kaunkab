## The Place of Enlightenment

According to Buddhist tradition, about two and a half thousand years ago Prince **Siddhartha Gautama** meditated under a peepal tree on the bank of the Niranjana river near Gaya, attained enlightenment (bodhi) and became the **Buddha**. That tree is called the "Bodhi tree" and the place Bodh Gaya. Among the four holiest sites of the Buddhists — Lumbini, Bodh Gaya, Sarnath and Kushinagar — Bodh Gaya is regarded as the most important.

The tree that stands today as the Bodhi tree is not the original; according to tradition it is a descendant of the same lineage.

## Ashoka's Temple and the Vajrasana

In the 3rd century BCE, around 250 BCE, the Mauryan emperor **Ashoka** had a temple and monastery built here. That original structure no longer survives, but the polished stone seat placed under the Bodhi tree — the **Vajrasana** — is believed to be from the same period. It marks the place where the Buddha sat.

An idea of what Ashoka's temple looked like comes from a carving at the **Bharhut** stupa, which shows an open shrine built around the Bodhi tree. In the Shunga period (about 150 BCE) a sandstone railing was installed here, on which scenes from Jataka tales and everyday life were carved. Later, in the Gupta period, a granite railing was added.

## The Gupta-Period Temple

The grand temple seen today is built mainly of **baked bricks** and is considered to date from about the **5th–6th century CE**, that is, the Gupta period or just after it; some scholars consider some parts of it even earlier. Its main structure:
- A pyramid-shaped spire about **55 metres** high, with small spires at its four corners.
- Inside the sanctum, an image of the Buddha in the earth-touching (bhumisparsha) posture.
- It is counted among the oldest surviving brick temples in India.

In the fifth century the Chinese traveller **Faxian** came to Bodh Gaya and described its monasteries. In the seventh century **Xuanzang** also saw this temple and wrote a description of it.

## Neglect and Restoration

After Buddhism weakened in India, care of the temple declined. The Buddhist kings of **Myanmar (Burma)** sent parties several times to repair it — in the thirteenth century and again in the nineteenth. In the 1870s a party of the Burmese king began repairs, but British officials did not consider their work correct from a historical point of view.

In 1880 the Government of Bengal gave **Joseph David Beglar** the responsibility for repairs, and **Alexander Cunningham** also supervised the work. This restoration lasted until about **1884**. During this the small stupas around the temple were also rebuilt. The repair did save the temple, but some of its alterations were later criticised.

## The Question of Ownership and Bodh Gaya Today

For several centuries the temple remained under the control of the **mahant** of a Shaiva monastery. In 1891 **Anagarika Dharmapala** of Sri Lanka founded the **Maha Bodhi Society** in Colombo and led a movement to hand over the temple's management to Buddhists. After a long dispute the **Bodh Gaya Temple Act** was passed in 1949, under which management went to a committee formed by the Bihar government, with both Buddhist and Hindu members.

In 2002 the **"Mahabodhi Temple Complex at Bodh Gaya"** was included in the UNESCO World Heritage list. On 7 July 2013 several low-intensity bomb blasts took place in the complex, but the main temple was not damaged. Today pilgrims come here from all over the world — from Sri Lanka, Myanmar, Thailand, Tibet, Japan and other countries.

## Significance

The Mahabodhi Temple shows several centuries of Indian architecture in a single place — from Ashoka's Vajrasana to the Shunga railing and the Gupta-period brick spire. As the birthplace of Buddhism, it connects the whole of Asia with India.
