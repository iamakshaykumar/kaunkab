## Background: the rise of a jagirdar's son

**Farid Khan**, son of an ordinary Afghan jagirdar of Sasaram in Bihar, reached the highest peak of power through his ability, administrative insight and indomitable courage. He received the title "Sher Khan" for killing a tiger with a single stroke of his sword.

After Babur's death, while Humayun was busy trying to manage the Mughal empire, Sher Khan consolidated his power in Bihar and Bengal. By winning the **Battle of Chausa** in 1539 and the **Battle of Bilgram (Kannauj)** in 1540, he forced Humayun to flee from India and founded the **second Afghan empire (the Sur Empire)** in Delhi.

## Revolutionary administrative reforms

Historians remember Sher Shah as a great administrator and reformer more than as a conqueror:

1. **Decentralised administrative structure:** The empire was divided into 'sarkars' (districts) and the sarkars into 'parganas'. In each pargana a shiqdar (law and order), a munsif (revenue and justice) and a patwari were posted.
2. **Land revenue (the zabti system):** Sher Shah for the first time had land measured and divided it into good, medium and poor grades according to its fertility. Peasants were given a 'patta' and made to write a 'qabuliyat' so that exploitation by middlemen would stop.
3. **Currency reform (the beginning of the rupiya):** Sher Shah discontinued old worn-out coins and put a standardised currency into circulation. He introduced the pure silver coin of 178 grains, the **'rupiya'**, and the copper coin, the **'dam'**; the name rupiya remains the name of our currency to this day.

## A network of communications, roads and sarais

To make trade and military movement easier, Sher Shah had four great highways built:
- **Sadak-i-Azam (Grand Trunk Road):** It began at Sonargaon in Bengal and ran through Kolkata, Prayagraj, Delhi and Lahore to Peshawar.
- At every two kos (about 6 km) **sarais (rest houses)** were built, where free food, water and lodging were provided for Hindu and Muslim travellers.
- Shady trees were planted on both sides of the roads and a system of dak chowkis (postal stations) was started.

## Untimely end and historical legacy

In 1545, during the siege of the **Kalinjar fort** in Bundelkhand, a ukka (a firearm or cannonball) burst, Sher Shah was badly burned and he died.

Although his rule lasted only five years, the administrative model he established was later adopted and refined by the Mughal emperor Akbar.
