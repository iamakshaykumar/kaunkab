## Background: The Licence Raj

After independence India adopted a planned, government-led economic model. The public sector dominated large industries, imports were tightly controlled, and private companies needed a government licence to set up a new factory, expand production or import goods. Critics called this system the **'Licence-Permit Raj'**. From 1950 to the 1970s the growth rate averaged about 3.5%. In the 1980s some relaxations were made and growth picked up, but it relied to a large extent on government spending and foreign loans.

## Why Did the Crisis Come?

By the late 1980s the government's fiscal deficit had grown very large, and foreign debt rose from about 20.6 billion dollars in 1980–81 to about 64 billion dollars in 1989–90. In 1990–91 several shocks struck at once:

- The **Gulf War** raised crude oil prices and reduced the money sent home by Indians working in the Gulf countries.
- Turmoil in the **Soviet Union**, a major trading partner of India, hurt exports.
- There was **political instability** in the country — two governments fell in two years, and in February 1991 the Chandra Shekhar government could not present a full budget. International rating agencies downgraded India's credit rating.

By June 1991 the foreign exchange reserves had fallen so low that they were barely enough to pay for about three weeks of essential imports. India was on the verge of defaulting on its foreign payments.

## Gold Pledged and the IMF

In May 1991 the Chandra Shekhar government secretly sent a part of the gold abroad to raise money, and later more gold was pledged; in all about **67 tonnes** of gold was used (the details differ slightly across sources). At the same time an emergency loan was taken from the **International Monetary Fund (IMF)**. The news that gold had been sent out of the country caused deep public anger.

## The Rao–Manmohan Reforms (July 1991)

After the elections that followed Rajiv Gandhi's assassination, **P. V. Narasimha Rao** became Prime Minister in June 1991. He made **Manmohan Singh**, an economist from outside politics and a former Governor of the Reserve Bank, his finance minister. Within a few weeks, several major steps were taken:

- **Devaluation of the rupee:** On 1 and 3 July 1991 the rupee was devalued in two instalments, by a total of about 18–19%.
- **New Industrial Policy (24 July 1991):** Except for a few select industries, the requirement of industrial licences was abolished, the list of industries reserved for the public sector was shortened, the restrictions on large companies under the MRTP Act were removed, and automatic approval was given for foreign investment of up to 51% in many sectors.
- **Budget (24 July 1991):** Manmohan Singh's budget announced reducing the fiscal deficit, cutting import duties, ending export subsidies and selling some shares in government companies.
- **Financial sector:** In August 1991 the **Narasimham Committee** was formed for banking reforms. In later years the stock market regulator SEBI was given statutory powers and the rupee gradually moved towards a market-based exchange rate.

## Debate and Criticism

The reforms were sharply debated from the start. Critics said they were carried out under pressure from the conditions of the IMF and the World Bank and weakened economic sovereignty; the government said the reforms were the country's own need. Many economists believe that the foundations of liberalisation had already been laid in the 1980s. In later decades, questions were also raised about growing economic inequality, the relatively slow progress of agriculture and the pace of job creation.

## Significance

After 1991 India's economic growth was faster and steadier than in earlier decades, the foreign exchange reserves were rebuilt, and information technology, telecommunications and the services sector expanded rapidly. Extreme poverty also fell notably, though economists disagree on how much the reforms contributed to this. 1991 is generally regarded as the biggest turning point in the economic policy of independent India, which ended the era of the 'Licence Raj'.
