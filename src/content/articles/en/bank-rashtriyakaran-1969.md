## Banks after Independence

At Independence most of India's big banks were in **private hands**, and many were controlled by large industrial houses. Critics said that the banks took deposits from the public but lent mainly to the industries their owners were linked to. Agriculture, small industry and villages were almost shut out of bank credit; farmers still depended on moneylenders. In the 1950s and 1960s several small banks also failed, trapping depositors' money.

The government had taken some steps earlier. In **1955** the Imperial Bank of India was turned into the government-owned **State Bank of India**. In 1967–68 Finance Minister **Morarji Desai** adopted a policy of **"social control"** over banks, which involved changes to bank boards and an emphasis on more lending to agriculture and small industry. But one section of the Congress believed this was not enough and that the big banks should be taken directly into government hands.

## Politics and the Night of 19 July

In 1969 the conflict inside the Congress between Prime Minister **Indira Gandhi** and the group of old leaders known as the "Syndicate" was at its peak. Indira Gandhi made bank nationalisation the centrepiece of her economic policy. Morarji Desai was not in favour of it. On **16 July 1969** Indira Gandhi took the finance ministry back from him, and Desai left the post of Deputy Prime Minister.

Three days later, on the night of **19 July 1969**, a presidential ordinance — the **Banking Companies (Acquisition and Transfer of Undertakings) Ordinance, 1969** — brought **14 large commercial banks** under government ownership. The banks chosen were those with deposits of **more than 50 crore rupees**. They were:

- Central Bank of India, Bank of India, Punjab National Bank, Bank of Baroda
- United Commercial Bank, Canara Bank, Dena Bank, United Bank of India
- Syndicate Bank, Allahabad Bank, Indian Bank, Bank of Maharashtra
- Indian Overseas Bank and Union Bank of India

These 14 banks held about **85 per cent** of the country's total bank deposits. Within two weeks Parliament passed a law to replace the ordinance, which received the President's assent on **9 August 1969**.

## The Court Challenge

The nationalisation was challenged in the Supreme Court by **Rustom Cavasjee Cooper** (R. C. Cooper), a shareholder in one of the banks. On **10 February 1970** an **11-judge** bench of the Supreme Court ruled by a 10–1 majority that some key provisions of the law were unconstitutional — mainly because the **compensation** to be paid to shareholders had not been fixed in a proper way and some provisions were discriminatory. The court did not hold the idea of nationalisation itself to be wrong.

The government immediately issued a fresh ordinance and then, in **1970**, passed a new law with revised provisions. In this way the banks remained in government ownership. Later, in **1980**, **six more banks** were nationalised, after which about **91 per cent** of the country's banking business came under government control.

## The End of the Privy Purses

In the same period Indira Gandhi's government also campaigned to abolish the **privy purses**. When more than 500 princely states joined India at Independence, the agreements with their rulers had promised them an annual personal allowance — the **privy purse** — and certain privileges. Some big royal houses received one million rupees or more a year, while most received much less. The government argued that such hereditary allowances did not fit the principle of equal rights in a democracy and were a burden on the treasury. The former rulers said these allowances were part of the promise made to them at the time of merger.

In **1970** the constitutional amendment to abolish the privy purses passed the Lok Sabha, but in the **Rajya Sabha** it fell short of the required two-thirds majority by **just one vote** and was defeated. After this, on **6 September 1970**, a presidential order withdrew the recognition of all the rulers. But in **December 1970** the Supreme Court struck down this order in the **Madhavrao Scindia** case.

After these two setbacks in the courts, Indira Gandhi dissolved the Lok Sabha early and held elections in **1971**, winning a huge majority with the slogan "Garibi Hatao" (Remove Poverty). The new Parliament passed the **26th Constitutional Amendment** in **1971**, which ended both the privy purses and the official recognition of the former rulers for good.

## Impact and Legacy

After nationalisation the government asked the banks to open branches in areas that had never had a bank. Over the following decades the number of bank branches in **villages and small towns** grew many times over. Areas such as agriculture, small industry and self-employment were treated as the **"priority sector"**, and banks were required to give them a fixed share of their lending. According to supporters, this brought ordinary people's savings into banks, gave credit support to the **Green Revolution** and reduced dependence on moneylenders.

Critics have argued that government control increased political interference, low efficiency and bad loans (NPAs) in the banks. After the economic reforms of **1991**, private banks were again allowed and public sector banks were also reformed. Even so, the decision of 1969 is counted among the biggest turning points in the economic policy of independent India, and the abolition of the privy purses ended the last privileges of the era of the princely states.
