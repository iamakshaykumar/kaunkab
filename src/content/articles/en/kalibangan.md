## Two Mounds on the Ghaggar

In the northern corner of Rajasthan, in **Hanumangarh district**, **Kalibangan** lies on the southern bank of the **Ghaggar river** (which many scholars link with the Vedic Saraswati). In the local dialect "Kalibangan" is commonly said to mean **"black bangles"** — because of the fragments of baked clay bangles scattered on the surface of the mounds.

There are two main mounds here. The western mound (about 9 metres high) was the **citadel**, and the eastern mound (about 12 metres high) was the **lower town**. This is the same dual plan seen at Harappa and Mohenjo-daro.

## Discovery and Excavation

In the early twentieth century the Italian Indologist **Luigi Pio Tessitori** took these ruins to be ancient (pre-Mauryan), but could not identify their correct period. In the 1950s **Amalananda Ghosh** identified it as a Harappan site. After this the Archaeological Survey of India, under **B. B. Lal** and **B. K. Thapar**, excavated here in nine seasons between **1960 and 1969**. The full report was published much later, in 2003.

## The Settlement Before Harappa

The greatest importance of Kalibangan is that two different phases were found here one above the other. The lower layer (**Kalibangan-I**) is **pre-Harappan** and is linked to the Sothi-Siswal culture. Its date is roughly taken from the later part of the fourth millennium BCE to about 2600 BCE, though different scholars give different estimates.

The settlement of this phase was enclosed by a mud-brick wall. Its pottery has been divided into six types (A to F), and this classification became the basis for identifying pottery across the whole region. It is believed that after an **earthquake** around **2600 BCE** this settlement was abandoned.

## The World's Oldest Ploughed Field

Outside the pre-Harappan settlement archaeologists found a **ploughed field**, whose date is taken to be about **2800 BCE**. It is called the oldest known ploughed field in the world. In it the plough furrows are laid out in two directions like a grid — close together in one direction and far apart in the other. From this it is inferred that two crops were sown together in one field. This discovery shows that ploughing had begun in this region even before the rise of the Harappan cities.

## The Harappan Town and Fire Altars

After the earthquake the **Harappan people** settled here (**Kalibangan-II**). The lower town had a grid of streets — the main streets about 7.2 metres wide and the lanes about 1.8 metres. Harappan seals, a cylindrical seal, a baked clay bull and a large number of bangles were found here.

A special identity of Kalibangan is its **fire altars**. They have been found in a row on a platform of the citadel, in houses of the lower town and in a separate public space. From their construction and location they are believed to be connected with some ritual — though their exact use is not certain. About 34 graves were also found here, showing three kinds of burial practice.

## Decline and Significance

In the early part of the second millennium BCE (generally by about 1750 BCE) this town was deserted. The archaeologist Robert Raikes linked this with the **drying up of the Ghaggar river**, but the debate over the causes of decline continues today.

Kalibangan shows that the Harappan civilisation did not emerge suddenly but developed gradually from already existing rural farming settlements. In 1983 the Archaeological Survey of India also opened a museum here.
