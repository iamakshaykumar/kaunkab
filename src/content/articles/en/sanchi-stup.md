## Ashoka's Foundation

Sanchi stands on a small hill in Raisen district of Madhya Pradesh, about 46 kilometres from Bhopal. **Vidisha**, a prosperous trading city of ancient times, is about 10 kilometres away, and the Buddhist centre at Sanchi flourished through the donations of this city's merchants and citizens.

In the 3rd century BCE the Mauryan emperor **Ashoka** had a hemispherical brick stupa built here, which is believed to hold relics connected with the Buddha. Near the stupa Ashoka also set up a highly polished stone pillar, on which is engraved a warning against splitting the Sangha (the so-called "Schism Edict"). Its four-lion capital is today in the Sanchi museum. The Buddhist tradition of Sri Lanka says Ashoka's queen Devi was from Vidisha, but this story rests on tradition.

## Expansion in the Shunga Period

In the 2nd century BCE, during the **Shunga** period, Ashoka's brick stupa was covered with stone and made almost twice as large. A **harmika** (square railing) and an **umbrella** (chhatra) were placed on top, and below it a raised platform for circumambulation and a stone **vedika** (railing) all around were built. The pillars of this railing carry the names of donors, including monks and nuns, merchants and ordinary householders.

## Gateways of the Satavahana Period

Around the 1st century BCE four magnificent **gateways** (toranas) were built around the stupa. An inscription on the southern gateway mentions the gift of Ananda, the foreman of the artisans of **King Siri Satakarni**, because of which these gateways are linked to the **Satavahana** period.
- The gateways have finely carved **Jataka tales**, events from the Buddha's life and scenes connected with Ashoka.
- An inscription says that this carving was done by the **ivory workers of Vidisha**, which is why the stone too shows the fine workmanship of ivory.
- The ornamentation of yakshis, elephants, lions and floral designs shows the early tradition of Indian sculpture.

In the later centuries too, donations and construction continued at Sanchi; inscriptions and temples of the Gupta period have also been found here.

## Neglect and Rediscovery

After Buddhism weakened in India, the monuments of Sanchi were neglected and began to fall into ruin. In 1818 the British officer **General Henry Taylor** wrote the first known account of the site in English. In 1851 **Alexander Cunningham** and **Frederick Maisey** carried out the first formal survey and excavation here. From a nearby stupa the relics of the Buddha's chief disciples **Sariputta** and **Mahamoggallana** were found and taken to England; later they were returned and placed in the Chetiyagiri Vihara built at Sanchi in 1952.

Meanwhile clumsy excavations and treasure-hunting also damaged the stupa. In the 1880s some gateways were re-erected, and between **1912 and 1919**, under the supervision of **John Marshall**, the monuments were extensively restored. The Begums of Bhopal, **Shah Jahan Begum** and **Sultan Jahan Begum**, gave funds for its preservation.

## Significance

The monuments of Sanchi show that Buddhism was supported not only by kings but also by merchants, craftsmen and ordinary people together. With monuments dating from about the 3rd century BCE to the 12th century CE, this site is a unique source for understanding the development of Indian art and architecture. In 1989 the **"Buddhist Monuments at Sanchi"** were included in the UNESCO World Heritage List.
