## Between History and Tradition

**Lal Ded** ('Ded' in Kashmiri means a respected woman, like a grandmother) is Kashmir's most beloved saint-poetess. She is also called **Lalleshwari**, **Lalla Yogini** and **Lalla Arifa**. Almost all information about her life comes from folk tradition — she is not mentioned in the main Kashmiri chronicles of her time, and the oldest written account of her is in a Persian work of 1587 (Tazkirat-ul-Arifin).

Most scholars place her birth between about **1301 and 1320**; the date of her death is uncertain, and different sources give the 1370s or 1390s. According to tradition she was born in a Brahmin family at **Pandrethan** (old name Puranadhishthana) near Srinagar, or at Sempore.

## From Household Life to Renunciation

According to the traditional story, she was married at about the age of twelve and was given the name Padmavati in her in-laws' house. The stories say she had to endure harsh treatment there. A famous folk tale says that her mother-in-law would put a stone on her plate and cover it with a little rice so that the plate looked full from outside — but Lal quietly bore it. These stories are not historical evidence but a tradition showing her patience.

At about the age of 24–26 she left home and became a disciple of the Shaiva guru **Siddha Srikantha** (Sed Boyu). After this she lived on alms, wandering through the Valley and singing her experiences in short verses.

## Vakh: Philosophy in Four Lines

Lal Ded's compositions are called **vakhs** (from Sanskrit 'vak') — short verses, usually of four lines. About 250–285 vakhs are current in her name, although some of them may have been added later. They are considered **the oldest surviving poetry in the Kashmiri language**; before her, the learned language of Kashmir was Sanskrit.

The philosophical basis of the vakhs is **Kashmir Shaivism** — the idea that Shiva and the individual soul are not separate, and that true knowledge comes from searching within. Lal questioned ritualism, animal sacrifice before idols and showy austerity, and stressed self-discipline, breath practice and recognising the 'Shiva' within. Her language is simple, full of rural imagery and goes straight to the heart.

## Kashmir in a Changing Time

Lal Ded's life was spent in a time of great change in Kashmir. Around 1339 the **Shah Mir sultanate** was established there, and in the following decades the arrival of Sufi saints from Central Asia and Iran increased. At this time her vakhs reached people around both temples and khanqahs.

Kashmiri tradition links her with **Nund Rishi** (Sheikh Nooruddin Noorani), who later shaped the Rishi Sufi tradition. A folk tale says that the newborn Nund would not drink his mother's milk, and then Lal came and persuaded him to take milk. Whether or not the story is historical, it shows that in Kashmir's memory the Shaiva yogini and the Sufi rishi are part of the same spiritual stream.

## Legacy

Lal Ded's verses lived on for centuries in oral tradition — in homes, in fields and in proverbs. In 1920 **George Grierson** and **Lionel Barnett** printed the first major collection of them, 'Lalla-Vakyani', based on an oral text, with an English translation. Later Jayalal Kaul, Ranjit Hoskote and other translators brought her to new readers.

Even today lines of her vakhs are used like proverbs in Kashmiri speech. The Lal Ded Memorial School in Srinagar and plays and music made on her keep her memory alive. As a woman who left social bonds and spoke the truth in her own voice, Lal Ded is counted among the earliest important voices of Indian bhakti and mystical literature.
