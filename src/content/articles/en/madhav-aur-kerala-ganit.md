## Madhava of Sangamagrama

**Madhava** is believed to have been born around **1340 CE** at **Sangamagrama** in Kerala; this village is usually identified with the area around **Irinjalakuda** in Thrissur district, though there is no complete agreement on this. He died around **1425**. He was both a mathematician and an astronomer — at that time the two subjects went together, because accurate calculation was needed to find the correct positions of the planets.

Most of Madhava's works are **lost**. A few texts such as *Venvaroha* (a method for finding the Moon's position) and *Sphutachandrapti* are believed to be his. His greatest discoveries are known to us from later texts, in which the authors clearly attribute them to Madhava.

## Infinite Series: The Greatest Achievement

In older Indian mathematics, tables of the sine (jya) were made by methods with a limited number of steps. Madhava went a step further and used **infinite series** — that is, sums in which the terms go on endlessly, and the more terms you add, the more accurate the answer becomes. He is credited with these:
- **The series for π** — π/4 = 1 − 1/3 + 1/5 − 1/7 + … , which is today called the **Madhava–Leibniz series**.
- The series for the **sine and cosine**, which in Europe were later linked with Newton.
- The series for the **inverse tangent (arctangent)**, which in the West became known by Gregory's name.

The π series above moves very slowly towards the correct answer. Madhava made it faster by adding **correction terms**. A value of π found in his name is correct to **11 decimal places**.

## The Guru-Disciple Tradition: The Kerala School

For about two hundred years after Madhava, this knowledge was passed on through the guru-disciple tradition in a few scholarly families of Kerala. This is called the **Kerala school of astronomy and mathematics**. Its main names are:
- **Parameshvara** — believed to be a disciple of Madhava; he observed eclipses and planets for years and improved calculations.
- **Nilakantha Somayaji** — his **Tantrasangraha** (c. 1500 CE) states these series but does not give proofs. He also wrote a commentary on the Aryabhatiya and gave a revised model of the planets.
- **Jyeshthadeva** — the special feature of his **Yuktibhasha** (c. 1530 CE) is that it was written in **Malayalam** instead of Sanskrit and gives **proofs** (demonstrations with reasoning) of the series.
- After this, scholars like **Achyuta Pisharati** and **Narayana Bhattathiri** were also linked to this tradition.

## Was This Calculus?

In Europe such series came in the seventeenth century in the work of Gregory, Newton and Leibniz — about two hundred and fifty years after Madhava. For this reason many writers call the Kerala school a forerunner of calculus. But historians are cautious: the Kerala mathematicians used certain particular series and ideas like limits, while the calculus of Newton and Leibniz developed as a general method. The comparison of the two is still debated among scholars.

## Did It Reach Europe or Not?

Some scholars guess that these ideas may have reached Europe in the sixteenth century through **Jesuit missionaries** who came to Kerala, or through trade routes. But so far no **direct evidence** — such as the translation of a manuscript — has been found. The available evidence suggests that before the nineteenth century these series were almost unknown outside Kerala.

## Rediscovery

In 1835 **C. M. Whish**, an officer of the East India Company, published an article on these texts, but it was neglected for a long time. In the twentieth century, research by C. T. Rajagopal, K. V. Sarma and other scholars gave Madhava and his tradition recognition again. Today Madhava is counted among the most original mathematicians of India after Aryabhata and Bhaskaracharya.
