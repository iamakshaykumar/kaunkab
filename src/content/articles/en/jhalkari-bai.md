## A Daughter of Bhojla Village

**Jhalkari Bai** is said to have been born on **22 November 1830** in **Bhojla** village near Jhansi. Her father, **Sadova Singh**, was a farmer, and her mother's name is given as **Jamuna Devi**. She belonged to the **Kori** community, which was given a low status in society at the time.

There was no school in the village, so she received no formal education, but she learned horse riding and the use of weapons as a child. Stories from Bundelkhand say that in the forest she killed a tiger with an axe and, while grazing cattle, faced a leopard with a stick. These are tales of folk memory and cannot be historically confirmed, but they show how brave people considered her.

## In the Rani's Army

Jhalkari Bai married **Puran Singh**, who is described as a soldier in the artillery of Rani Lakshmibai's army. According to folk tradition, once when Jhalkari Bai went to the fort with other women to greet the Rani, the queen was astonished to see her face: the two bore a striking **resemblance**. The Rani took her into her **women's army**, which later writing called the **'Durga Dal'**.

There she trained in firing guns, using cannon and swordsmanship. Gradually she became one of the Rani's trusted companions and advisers. This was a time when in **1854**, under the **Doctrine of Lapse**, the British had annexed the state of Jhansi to Company rule, and after the revolt of **1857** the Rani had taken back the government of Jhansi.

## The Siege of Jhansi (1858)

In **March 1858** the army of **Sir Hugh Rose** besieged Jhansi. Heavy bombardment went on for many days. On **3 April 1858** the British army entered the town and fierce fighting broke out. That same night Rani Lakshmibai left the fort with her adopted son and went towards **Kalpi**.

The most famous story about Jhalkari Bai belongs to this moment. According to folk tradition and later literature, to hide the Rani's escape and win her time, Jhalkari Bai put on the **Rani's attire** and rode on horseback towards the British camp. She declared herself to be Rani Lakshmibai and demanded to meet Hugh Rose. The British officers remained confused for some time; it is said that the truth came out only about a day later, by which time the Rani had got far away.

## Differing Accounts of Her End

Sources do not agree about Jhalkari Bai's end. According to some accounts she died fighting in this battle on **4 April 1858**, and her husband Puran Singh was also killed defending the fort. According to other accounts the British released her once her identity was revealed, and she lived much longer, by one view until **1881**. Since her name hardly appears in the British official records of the time, historians treat her story with caution, distinguishing folk memory from written sources.

## Literature and Folk Memory

Literature played a big part in making Jhalkari Bai's story popular. **Vrindavan Lal Verma**'s famous novel **'Jhansi Ki Rani'** (1951) gives her an important place. In the same year Ramchandra Heran's Bundeli novel 'Maati' appeared, and in **1964** Bhawani Shankar Visharad wrote her first biography. In the folk songs of Bundelkhand she is still sung of as the Rani's brave 'look-alike' companion.

Historians point out that the British documents of that period focused mainly on kings, queens and major commanders, so the names of ordinary soldiers from the villages, especially women, often went unrecorded. In such cases folk songs, stories and oral tradition became the main carriers of their memory. In the last decades of the twentieth century Dalit writers and social organisations brought these stories to the fore again, and movements for a regional Bundelkhand identity also adopted Jhalkari Bai as their symbol.

## Legacy

From the late twentieth century, Jhalkari Bai emerged as a symbol of pride and identity for **Dalit and backward communities**. The Kori community observes her death anniversary as **'Shahid Diwas'** (Martyrs' Day). On **22 July 2001** the Indian Postal Department issued a **postage stamp** in her honour. In **2017** President **Ram Nath Kovind** unveiled her statue in Bhopal, and her statues stand in several cities including Gwalior and Agra. Her character also appeared in the 2019 film 'Manikarnika'. Jhalkari Bai's story reminds us that the struggle of 1857 belonged not only to kings and queens but also to ordinary men and women from common villages.
