## The Sequel to the Silappatikaram

In Tamil literature five works are called the **Five Great Epics** (Aimperumkappiyam). The two most famous among them are the **Silappatikaram** and the **Manimekalai**. Many writers call them the "twin epics", because the story of the Manimekalai carries on from where the story of the Silappatikaram ends.

The Silappatikaram tells the story of the merchant **Kovalan**, his wife **Kannagi** and the dancer **Madhavi**. Kovalan and Madhavi have a daughter — **Manimekalai**. After Kovalan's death Madhavi, turning away from the world, takes refuge in Buddhism, and her daughter Manimekalai too later becomes a Buddhist nun. This Manimekalai is the heroine of this epic.

## The Author and the Date

The author of this epic is believed to be **Seethalai Sathanar** (Kulavanikan Seethalai Sathanar). From the word "Kulavanikan" it is guessed that he may have been a grain merchant, but this is later tradition and very little is known about his life.

There is considerable disagreement among scholars about its **date**. Estimates range from the second century to the sixth century (and, according to some, even later). According to the scholar Paula Richman, the sixth-century view of Kandaswamy and **Kamil Zvelebil** is the most solid. It is therefore generally taken to be of about the sixth century, but this should not be understood as a final verdict.

The poem has **30 cantos** (kadai) and about **4,861 lines**, written in the Tamil **akaval** metre.

## The Story: From Puhar to Kanchi

The story begins in the Chola port city of **Puhar** at the time of the **Indra festival**. The Chola prince **Udayakumaran** becomes infatuated with the young Manimekalai and pursues her. To save Manimekalai, the sea goddess **Manimekhala** flies her away while she sleeps to an island called **Manipallavam**.

On this island Manimekalai finds a jewelled seat bearing the Buddha's footprints, and she remembers her **past lives** — in which Udayakumaran was her husband. Here, from a pond, she receives a miraculous **alms bowl** called **Amudasurabhi** (Amritasurabhi), which never becomes empty. On returning, Manimekalai feeds the hungry with this bowl. According to the tale, the bowl stays full even in the twelve-year famine in the Pandya country.

To escape the prince, Manimekalai uses a spell to take the form of a vidyadhara woman named **Kayachandikai**. But the real Kayachandikai's husband, by mistake, kills Udayakumaran. After this Manimekalai is imprisoned; the queen wants to harm her, but in the end she is released. Freed, she turns the **prison into a house of service**.

Next she goes to the Chera capital **Vanchi**, where she listens to the views of teachers of different philosophies, and then reaches **Kanchipuram**. There she meets her mother and her teacher **Aravana Adigal**, who teaches her the Buddhist doctrine. In the last canto Manimekalai performs severe austerities so that, freed from the cycle of birth and death, she may attain **nirvana**.

## Buddhist Philosophy and Controversy

The Manimekalai is not just a story but also a **religious-philosophical poem**. In its last cantos the doctrines of Buddhism — such as the twelve links of **dependent origination** (pratityasamutpada) — are explained. It is regarded as the **only Tamil Buddhist literary work** that survives today; Buddhist literature in Tamil must once have been very large, but almost all the rest is lost. It is therefore a priceless source for the history of Buddhism in South India.

Scholars also debate whether it reflects the **Mahayana** tradition of Buddhism or the **Hinayana** (Theravada). Scholars such as Kamil Zvelebil have called it a work of Buddhist propaganda that criticises other creeds, especially Jainism, and have considered it somewhat weaker than the Silappatikaram from a literary point of view. On the other hand, many readers regard its compassion, its message of feeding the hungry, and its story of a woman's self-determination as its real strength.

## In the Modern Period

For centuries the Manimekalai survived only in hand-written copies on palm leaves. **U. V. Swaminatha Iyer**, the great seeker of Tamil manuscripts, collated its copies and published a critical edition in **1921**. Today it is an important part of the Tamil literature curriculum, and films and plays have also been made on its story.
