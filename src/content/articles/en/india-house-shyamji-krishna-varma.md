## From Kutch to Oxford

**Shyamji Krishna Varma** was born in **October 1857** at **Mandvi** in the princely state of Kutch (some sources say 1 October, some 4 October). He was an extraordinary scholar of Sanskrit. In **1879** he went to England as an assistant to **Monier Monier-Williams**, the Sanskrit professor at Oxford, and studied at **Balliol College**. He is described as the **first Indian to take an M.A. from Oxford**. In **1884** he became a barrister.

Returning to India he practised law and worked as dewan (chief minister) in states such as **Ratlam**, **Udaipur** and **Junagadh**. But after several humiliating experiences at the hands of British officials, in **1897** he left India and moved permanently to England, settling in **Highgate**, London.

## 1905: Three Institutions, One Aim

Shyamji was an admirer of the philosopher **Herbert Spencer**, who said that resistance to aggression is "not merely justifiable but imperative". With this thinking, in **1905** he took three steps one after another:

- **February 1905** — Founding of the **Indian Home Rule Society**, which people such as **Bhikaiji Cama**, **S. R. Rana** and **Lala Lajpat Rai** also joined.
- **1905** — Editing and publication of the monthly journal **'The Indian Sociologist'**, which the British government banned in India as "seditious" (some sources give its beginning as 1904).
- **1 July 1905** — Inauguration of **India House** at **65 Cromwell Avenue, Highgate**.

## India House: From Hostel to Centre of Revolution

India House was inaugurated by the British socialist leader **Henry Hyndman**. Present at the ceremony were **Dadabhai Naoroji**, the suffrage leader **Charlotte Despard** and **Bhikaiji Cama**. At first about **thirty** students could live in it.

On paper it was a hostel for Indian students who had come to study in England, but the real aim was to fill them with nationalist ideas. Shyamji gave scholarships in memory of the **heroes of the 1857 uprising**, on the condition that the recipient would not, on returning to India, accept any salaried post or honour from the British government. S. R. Rana gave three scholarships in memory of **Maharana Pratap**.

## Savarkar, Dhingra and British Pressure

In **1906**, on Shyamji's scholarship, **Vinayak Damodar Savarkar** came to London and soon became the leading figure of India House. Young men such as **V. V. S. Aiyar**, **P. M. Bapat**, **Hemchandra Kanungo** and **M. P. T. Acharya** joined here; it is believed that Bapat and Kanungo tried to gather information on bomb-making here. Savarkar's book *The Indian War of Independence* was published in **1909**.

Because of growing police surveillance and fear of prosecution, Shyamji moved to **Paris** in **1907** and never returned to Britain. In his absence India House became even more radical. On **1 July 1909** its member **Madan Lal Dhingra** assassinated **Curzon Wyllie** in London. After this the British government increased its severity, Savarkar was arrested in **March 1910**, and the group of India House scattered across France, Germany and America. It is worth noting that Shyamji himself was not in London at the time of these acts of violence; his role was more that of providing ideas, money and a platform.

## Exile and Memory

In **1914** Shyamji moved to **Geneva**, where his activities remained limited during the World War because of Swiss restrictions. He died in Geneva on **30 March 1930**. In **2003** his ashes were brought to India and in **2010** the **'Kranti Teerth'** memorial was built at Mandvi. In **1989** the Government of India issued a postage stamp on him.

India House lasted only five or six years, but it showed that the fight for freedom could be fought from within London, the capital of the empire itself. Many of those scattered from here went on to remain involved in Indian revolutionary activity in Europe and America.
