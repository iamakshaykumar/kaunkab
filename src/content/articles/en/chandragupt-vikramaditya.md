## Succession and the Story of Ramagupta

Chandragupta II was the son of the Gupta emperor **Samudragupta** and queen **Dattadevi**. His reign is placed from about **380 CE** to **415 CE**, although some historians put its beginning at about 375 CE.

A dramatic story connected with his accession is found in the Sanskrit play **'Devichandraguptam'**, of which only a few fragments survive. According to the story, after Samudragupta his elder brother **Ramagupta** became king, and after being defeated by a Shaka king he agreed to hand over his queen **Dhruvadevi** to him. Chandragupta then went into the enemy camp disguised as the queen and killed the Shaka king. Later he removed Ramagupta and married Dhruvadevi. That Dhruvadevi was Chandragupta's queen is confirmed by inscriptions, but scholars deeply disagree on how much of the rest of the story is historical.

## Marriage Alliances and the Vakataka Alliance

Along with war, Chandragupta also strengthened his position through marriage alliances. One of his queens, **Kuberanaga**, was a princess of the Naga dynasty. Her daughter **Prabhavatigupta** was married to the **Vakataka** king **Rudrasena II** of the Deccan. After Rudrasena's death in about 390 CE, Prabhavatigupta ruled for about twenty years as regent for her young sons. During this time the Vakataka kingdom remained a close ally of the Guptas, so that in the campaign in western India Chandragupta had no worry about his southern border.

## Victory over the Shaka Satraps

Chandragupta's greatest military success was the victory over the **Shaka satraps** of western India, who had ruled Malwa, Gujarat and Saurashtra for about three centuries. On the evidence of coins, this victory is believed to have taken place between about **397 and 409 CE**, because after this the satraps' coins stop. This victory gave the Gupta empire the ports of the western coast and rich trade routes.

After the conquest Chandragupta issued **silver coins** in the style of the satraps, but had the Gupta emblem, the **Garuda**, stamped on them. He was the first Gupta emperor to issue silver coins. His gold coins bear the name 'Chandra' and the title **'Vikramaditya'**. Tradition regards Ujjain as his second capital, but there is no firm inscriptional evidence for this.

## The Inscriptions of Udayagiri and Sanchi

The rock-cut caves in the **Udayagiri** hills near Vidisha are a major heritage of his time. Outside Cave 6 there is an inscription of Gupta year 82 (about **401 CE**), which mentions the building of a Vaishnava cave temple by a feudatory of Chandragupta. Another inscription says that his minister **Virasena** came here with the king during a campaign of world conquest. The huge sculpture of the **Varaha** incarnation in Cave 5 is counted among the finest works of Gupta-period sculpture.

At **Sanchi**, an inscription of Gupta year 93 (about 412–413 CE) speaks of a donation to a Buddhist monastery by his military officer **Amrakardava**. This is the last known dated inscription of his reign, and shows that Buddhists too received patronage in the kingdom of a Vaishnava emperor. In this period the Chinese monk **Faxian** (399–412 CE) travelled in India and described its peaceful, prosperous life, although he did not write the king's name.

## The Iron Pillar of Mehrauli

The **iron pillar** standing in the **Qutub complex** in Delhi, about 7.2 metres tall and weighing more than six tonnes, carries a eulogy of a king named 'Chandra'. According to the inscription, this king defeated enemies in **Vanga** (Bengal), conquered the **Vahlikas** across the Indus river, and set up a standard of Vishnu on the **Vishnupada** hill. Most historians take this 'Chandra' to be Chandragupta II, but this identification is not completely certain. Where the pillar originally stood — Udayagiri, Mathura or elsewhere — and when it was brought to Delhi are also disputed.

## Legacy

In about 415 CE Chandragupta II was succeeded as emperor by his son **Kumaragupta I**. In Chandragupta's time the Gupta empire stretched from Bengal to Gujarat, and along with political stability art, architecture and literature were encouraged. Many scholars place the great poet **Kalidasa** in his time, but there is no direct proof of this. The image of the 'King Vikramaditya' of later folk tales is also believed to bear the stamp of his memory.
