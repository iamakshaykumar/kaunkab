## 'The Ocean of Learning'

Ishwar Chandra Bandyopadhyay was born on **26 September 1820** in the village of **Birsingha** in Bengal's Medinipur district, in an ordinary Brahmin family. For his studies he went to the **Sanskrit College** in Calcutta, where he made a deep study of grammar, literature, smriti and nyaya. Because of his extraordinary scholarship he received the title **'Vidyasagar'** (ocean of learning). Later he served as a teacher and administrator at Fort William College and the Sanskrit College.

## The Condition of Widows

In 19th-century Bengal, especially among the upper castes, child marriage was common. Many girls became widows in childhood and were not allowed to marry again for the rest of their lives. They were deprived of coloured clothes, good food and social mixing. Sati had been banned in 1829 through the efforts of Rammohan Roy, but the hardships of living widows remained.

## Arguments from the Scriptures

Vidyasagar knew that society would not change through English arguments alone. So he turned to the scriptures himself. In 1855 he wrote booklets in Bengali on widow marriage, in which, mainly on the basis of a verse of the **Parashara Smriti**, he argued that the remarriage of a woman in certain circumstances is sanctioned by the scriptures. These booklets set off a vigorous debate in Bengal, and opponents also mocked him sharply.

## The Fight for the Law

Vidyasagar and his supporters sent petitions to the Legislative Council demanding that widow marriage be made legal. The conservative side, led by **Radhakanta Deb** and the **Dharma Sabha**, sent a petition against it, which carried about four times as many signatures as the supporters' petition.

Despite this, the bill went forward. Its draft was prepared in the time of **Lord Dalhousie** and it was passed in the time of **Lord Canning**. The **Hindu Widows' Remarriage Act (Act XV of 1856)** was passed on 16 July and came into force on **26 July 1856**. According to it, the remarriage of a widow was valid and children born of it were regarded as legitimate. One condition was also that a widow who remarried had to give up the limited right she held in the property of her deceased husband — this provision was criticised later.

## The First Marriage and a Difficult Road

After the law was made, on **7 December 1856** in Calcutta, under Vidyasagar's supervision, the first legal widow remarriage took place — the marriage of the Sanskrit scholar **Srishchandra Vidyaratna** with the child-widow **Kalimati Devi**. After this Vidyasagar arranged many more widow marriages, often bearing their expenses himself, which plunged him into heavy debt. In 1870 he also had his son Narayanchandra married to a widow.

Even so, change at the social level was slow. The law opened the way, but in the following decades the number of widow marriages remained small, because such families had to face social boycott.

## Legacy

Vidyasagar also raised his voice against polygamy and child marriage, got schools opened for girls' education, and gave Bengali prose a simple, modern form. He died in Calcutta on **29 July 1891**. The Act of 1856 shows how the reformers of the 19th century made a path for social change by finding arguments from within tradition itself. (In independent India, after the Hindu Marriage Act, 1955, this old law became unnecessary and was formally repealed in 1983.)
