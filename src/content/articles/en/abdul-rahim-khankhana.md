## Bairam Khan's Son at Akbar's Court

**Abdul Rahim** was born on **17 December 1556** in **Lahore**. His father **Bairam Khan** was then guardian of the teenage emperor **Akbar** and the most powerful man in the Mughal empire. In 1560 Akbar took power into his own hands and Bairam Khan set out on a pilgrimage to Mecca, but in **1561** he was murdered by an Afghan at **Patan** in Gujarat. Rahim was then about five years old.

The family somehow reached Akbar's court by way of Ahmedabad. Akbar raised the boy under his own care like a prince and called him **"Mirza Khan"**. Here Rahim learned **Turkish, Persian, Arabic, Sanskrit** and **Hindi (Braj and Awadhi)**. Later he was married to **Mah Banu**.

## Khan-i-Khanan — Mughal Commander

Rahim spent about fifty years of his life in the Mughal army and administration.

- **Gujarat (1584)** — When the former sultan of Gujarat, **Muzaffar Shah III**, rebelled, Rahim defeated him at **Sarkhej** near Ahmedabad in January 1584. The **Fateh Bagh** was laid out there to mark the victory, and Akbar gave him the title **"Khan-i-Khanan"** (Khan of Khans, i.e. commander-in-chief) — the same title his father had once held.
- **Sindh (1590–91)** — After a campaign against **Jani Beg**, ruler of Thatta, Sindh came into the Mughal empire.
- **Deccan (from the 1590s)** — With Prince **Daniyal** he led the campaigns against Ahmadnagar. In this period **Chand Bibi**, the defender of Ahmadnagar, died, and in **1600** the fort of Ahmadnagar fell to the Mughals. In later years Rahim served for long periods as governor of the Deccan at **Burhanpur**.

Akbar counted him among his most trusted nobles; popular tradition includes him among Akbar's **"Navratnas"** (nine jewels). In **1589–90** he also translated Babur's memoir, the **Baburnama**, from Chagatai Turkish into **Persian**.

## Jahangir's Reign — Ups and Downs

After Akbar's death in **1605**, Rahim's position under Jahangir kept changing. At times he was sent back to the Deccan, at times he faced suspicion at court. In the 1620s, during the rebellion of Prince **Khurram (Shah Jahan)**, Rahim was accused of siding with the rebel prince, and in this turmoil he also lost some of his sons. In the end he was pardoned, and his title was restored in the last part of his life. He died in **Agra** on **1 October 1627** (some sources say 1626). He was buried in Delhi in the very tomb he had built for his wife.

## Rahim's Dohas

Despite being a soldier and administrator, Rahim is remembered today above all as a **poet**. He wrote **dohas** (couplets) in **Braj** and **Awadhi** that teach lessons about life in plain language, and they still appear in school books and everyday speech. The word "Rahiman" often appears in them. Two famous examples:

> Rahiman dhaaga prem ka, mat toro chatkaay.
> Toote pe phir na jure, jure gaanth pari jaay.

> Rahiman dekhi baden ko, laghu na deejiye daari.
> Jahaan kaam aavai sui, kaha karai tarvaari.

The first means that the thread of love should not be snapped with a jerk, because once broken it does not join again, and even if it joins, a knot remains. The second says: do not throw away a small thing on seeing a big one — where a needle is needed, what can a sword do?

Besides the dohas, his works include **Barvai Nayika Bhed** (in the Awadhi barvai metre), **Madanashtak** on devotion to Krishna, **Nagar Shobha**, and the Sanskrit astrology treatises **Khetakautukam** and **Dwatrimshadyogavali**. He also patronised painters and calligraphers; an illustrated copy of the **Razmnama**, the Persian version of the Mahabharata, was prepared for him.

## Generosity and Legacy

Rahim was also famous for his generosity. It is said that he kept his eyes lowered while giving alms. In a well-known story a poet (in many versions **Tulsidas**) asked: "Seekhe kahaan Nawabju, aisi deni den; jyon-jyon kar oonchau karau, tyon-tyon neeche nain" — where did you learn such a way of giving, that the higher your hand rises, the lower your eyes fall? Rahim's reply is said to be: "Denhaar kou aur hai, bhejat jo din rain; log bharam ham par karain, taasaun neeche nain" — the giver is someone else (God), who sends day and night; people mistakenly think it is me, so my eyes are lowered. This story is not historically confirmed, but it captures Rahim's image well.

**Rahim's Tomb** in Delhi's **Nizamuddin** was built in 1598. After long neglect it was restored by the **Aga Khan Trust for Culture** and reopened to the public in **2020**.

Rahim symbolises the composite culture of the Mughal age, in which the son of a Turkoman noble translated into Persian, wrote on astrology in Sanskrit and composed verses of Krishna devotion in Braj and Awadhi. Four hundred years later his dohas are still on ordinary people's lips — a greater legacy than most generals ever achieve.
