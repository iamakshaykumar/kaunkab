## Background: Post-War Discontent

During the Second World War the Royal Indian Navy (RIN) grew rapidly, and thousands of young Indians were recruited as sailors (ratings). After the war they faced poor food, bad living arrangements, lower pay than British sailors, and racial treatment from British officers. At the same time, the trials of the **Indian National Army (INA)** held at the Red Fort in 1945–46 had stirred national feeling across the country, and within the armed forces too.

## The Spark: HMIS Talwar

**HMIS Talwar**, located in Bombay, was the navy's signal training establishment. In early February 1946, its radio operator **Balai Chandra (B. C.) Dutt** was arrested on charges of writing anti-British slogans and possessing objectionable material. After this, the commanding officer was accused of using racial abuse towards the sailors.

On **18 February 1946**, the sailors of Talwar refused to work and to eat in protest against the bad food and the insults. This hunger strike soon became an open mutiny.

## Spread of the Mutiny

The very next day, 19 February, the sailors elected the **Naval Central Strike Committee**, whose president was **M. S. Khan**, with **Madan Singh** also a prominent leader. The strike spread from the ships and shore establishments of Bombay harbour to Karachi, Calcutta, Madras, Visakhapatnam and other centres. Figures for the scale of the mutiny differ among sources — roughly 10,000 to 20,000 sailors and 50 to 78 ships and about 20 shore establishments are cited.

The committee put forward a list of demands, which included better food, pay equal to British sailors, appointment of Indian officers, and the release of INA prisoners and all political prisoners. That is, service grievances and national politics were both mixed together in it.

## Clashes in Bombay and Karachi

In Bombay the sailors drove around the city in trucks and received support from ordinary people, students and workers. At the call of the Communist Party a general strike took place on **22 February**, the mills stayed closed and there were clashes with British soldiers on the streets. In these riots and firing, hundreds of people were killed in Bombay — the estimate cited on Wikipedia goes up to 236.

In Karachi the sailors seized **HMIS Hindustan** on 21 February, but after shelling by the British army they had to surrender the next day. The British commander Admiral Godfrey warned that if necessary the entire navy would be destroyed.

## The Stand of the Political Leadership and the Surrender

The reaction of national leaders was mixed. The top leadership of the Congress and the Muslim League did not support the mutiny; Gandhiji criticised it because of violence and indiscipline. Only the Communist Party supported it fully, and in the Congress some leaders such as **Aruna Asaf Ali** stood with the sailors.

**Sardar Patel** and **Muhammad Ali Jinnah** appealed to the sailors to lay down arms and assured them that no reprisals would be taken against them. On **23 February 1946** the strike committee in Bombay surrendered. The committee's last message said that they were surrendering to India, not to Britain — this sentiment is recorded in many history books. But later many sailors were punished and 476 were dismissed from service.

## Historical Importance

The naval mutiny lasted only a few days, yet its effect was deep. Coming right after the INA trials, it gave the British government a clear signal that the loyalty of the Indian armed forces was no longer certain — and without them, ruling India was not possible. Soon after this the Cabinet Mission came to India. Historians also discuss why the leadership did not embrace it: they felt that a breakdown of discipline in the forces so close to independence would be dangerous for the future. A memorial to this mutiny has been built at Colaba in Mumbai.
