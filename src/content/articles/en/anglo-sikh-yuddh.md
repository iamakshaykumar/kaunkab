## Background: After Ranjit Singh

After the death of **Maharaja Ranjit Singh** in 1839, the Lahore Durbar became entangled in conspiracies and murders. Within a few years several rulers and ministers were killed. In 1843 the five-year-old **Duleep Singh** became Maharaja and his mother **Maharani Jind Kaur** became regent. Meanwhile the number and political influence of the Khalsa army grew rapidly — from about 29,000 in 1839 to more than 80,000 by 1845. On the other side the British built up their forces at Ferozepur near the Sutlej. Sikh and Indian historians regard this as an aggressive preparation, while British accounts describe it as defensive.

## The First War (1845–1846)

On **11 December 1845** the Sikh army crossed the Sutlej. The Sikhs said they were moving within their own territory, but the British treated it as a declaration of war. After this, four major battles were fought:

- **Mudki** (18 December 1845)
- **Ferozeshah** (21–22 December 1845) — a very costly battle for the British
- **Aliwal** (28 January 1846) — Sir Harry Smith's victory
- **Sobraon** (10 February 1846) — the decisive battle, in which the retreating Sikh soldiers were trapped in the Sutlej when the bridge of boats broke, and thousands were killed

## Questions About Lal Singh and Tej Singh

Two chiefs of the Sikh army, the wazir **Lal Singh** and the commander **Tej Singh**, remained surrounded by controversy. According to Sikh historians both deliberately weakened their own army, because the powerful Khalsa army had become a danger to the court as well. The available records show that Lal Singh was in contact with British officers. Accounts differ about the bridge breaking at Sobraon — some Sikh narratives blame Tej Singh's doing, while British accounts say it broke under the weight of fleeing soldiers.

## The Treaties of Lahore and Bhairowal

By the **Treaty of Lahore** of **9 March 1846** the Sikhs gave up the Jalandhar Doab between the Beas and the Sutlej and accepted an indemnity of one and a half crore rupees. Since they could not pay the whole sum, they also had to give up Kashmir and the hill territories. A few days later, by the **Treaty of Amritsar**, the British handed Kashmir over to **Gulab Singh** of Jammu for 7.5 million rupees. By the **Treaty of Bhairowal** of December 1846 a British Resident and a council of regency were installed at Lahore; in practice power passed to the Company, and Jind Kaur was set aside with a pension.

## The Second War (1848–1849) and the Annexation

In April 1848 two British officers, Vans Agnew and Anderson, who had come to take charge from Diwan **Mulraj** at **Multan**, were killed. This local revolt soon spread when the army of **Chattar Singh Attariwala** in Hazara and then of his son **Sher Singh** also joined the rebellion. In November 1848 a fierce battle was fought at **Ramnagar** and then on **13 January 1849** at **Chillianwala**, in which the British suffered heavy losses and the result was almost inconclusive. In January 1849 Multan fell, and on **21 February 1849** in the battle of **Gujrat** (Punjab) the Sikh army was decisively defeated. In March 1849 the Sikh chiefs laid down their arms near Rawalpindi.

The Governor-General **Lord Dalhousie** decided on the annexation of Punjab. On **29 March 1849** the ten-year-old Maharaja Duleep Singh was made to accept the terms of annexation at Lahore (some accounts place the formal proclamation of annexation in early April). The state's property was confiscated and the **Koh-i-Noor** diamond passed into British hands.

## Results

Duleep Singh was given a pension and sent first to Fatehgarh and in 1854 to England; he died in exile in Paris in 1893. With the annexation of Punjab, almost the entire territory of India came under the Company. Ironically, a few years later, in the revolt of 1857, the British made extensive use of Sikh soldiers recruited from Punjab.
