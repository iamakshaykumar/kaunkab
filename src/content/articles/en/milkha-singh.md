## The Wound of Partition

**Milkha Singh** was born in the village of **Govindpura** in undivided Punjab, which is today in Pakistan. Official records give his date of birth as **20 November 1929**, though other dates are also found in some records. He was one of several children of a simple farming family.

The **Partition** of **1947** robbed him of his childhood. His parents and several brothers and sisters were killed in communal violence. The teenage Milkha somehow reached India and lived in refugee camps in **Delhi**. Those days were full of hunger, uncertainty and struggle. He later said that this pain gave him the determination to make something of his life.

## The Army and the Start of Running

After several attempts he joined the **Indian Army** in **1951**. It was in the army that he was introduced to running. After a good performance in a cross-country race he was selected for special training. Milkha worked hard day and night; it is said that he trained so much that he would often collapse from exhaustion.

He first stepped onto the international stage at the **1956 Melbourne Olympics**, but went out in the early rounds. There he watched world-class 400-metre runners and learned from them. This experience became the foundation of his later success.

## 1958 — The Golden Year

**1958** was the most successful year of Milkha Singh's career. At the **Tokyo Asian Games** he won gold medals in both the **200 metres** and the **400 metres**.

The same year, at the **British Empire and Commonwealth Games** in **Cardiff**, Britain, he won the **440 yards** in **46.6 seconds**, beating **Malcolm Spence** of South Africa. This was **independent India's first gold medal at the Commonwealth Games**. He was welcomed home as a hero, and in **1959** he was honoured with the **Padma Shri**.

## Rome 1960 — A Story of Tenths of a Second

At the **1960 Rome Olympics** Milkha Singh was considered a strong medal contender. In the **400-metre final** he started fast and stayed ahead for a good distance. But midway through the race he eased his pace slightly and looked back — something he later called the biggest mistake of his life. In the final stretch other runners overtook him and he finished **fourth**, a tiny fraction of a second behind the bronze medal. In this race **Otis Davis** of the United States and **Carl Kaufmann** of Germany took the first two places in a time equalling the world record, and the bronze went to South Africa's **Malcolm Spence** — the same runner Milkha had beaten in Cardiff two years earlier.

Even so, his time was **45.73 seconds**, which remained India's national record for **nearly 40 years**. In the same year an international athletics meet was held in **Lahore**, Pakistan, where he defeated the well-known Pakistani sprinter **Abdul Khaliq**. According to popular accounts, Pakistan's President **General Ayub Khan**, after watching him race, said that Milkha did not run but flew — and from then on he came to be called the **"Flying Sikh"**. He received this name on the very land where his family had been destroyed.

At the **1962 Jakarta Asian Games** he won two more golds, in the **400 metres** and the **4×400-metre relay**. He also took part in the 1964 Tokyo Olympics.

## The Second Phase of Life and Legacy

After retiring from running, Milkha Singh served as an officer in the sports department of the **Punjab** government and worked to bring young people into sport. He married **Nirmal Kaur**, a former captain of the Indian women's volleyball team. Their son **Jeev Milkha Singh** became an international golfer. In 2001 he declined the **Arjuna Award**, saying it was being given far too late and should go to young athletes. He donated his medals to the nation.

In **2013** his autobiography **'The Race of My Life'** was published, and the film **'Bhaag Milkha Bhaag'** was based on it, introducing his story to a new generation.

He died in **Chandigarh** on **18 June 2021** from complications of **COVID-19** — just five days after the death of his wife Nirmal Kaur. Milkha Singh's story is not only about medals but about the courage that turned an orphaned refugee boy into the country's most famous runner.
