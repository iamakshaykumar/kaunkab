## Village Self-Government: An Old Idea

The idea of the village panchayat is very old in India. During the freedom movement, **Mahatma Gandhi** dreamt of **'Gram Swaraj'**, in which every village would manage its own affairs. When the Constitution was framed, panchayats were mentioned only in the **Directive Principles** (Article 40) — that is, states were advised to set up panchayats, but it was not binding.

A committee under **Balwant Rai Mehta** was set up to review the **Community Development Programme** begun in 1952. Its **1957** report recommended **three-tier** Panchayati Raj — village, block and district. On this basis, on **2 October 1959**, Prime Minister **Jawaharlal Nehru** formally launched Panchayati Raj at **Nagaur in Rajasthan**. Several states, including Andhra Pradesh, adopted it.

## Weaknesses and Attempts at Reform

After the initial enthusiasm, panchayats grew weak. In many states elections were not held for years, panchayats had neither money nor powers, and state governments could dissolve them whenever they wished. Several committees were set up for reform — the **Ashok Mehta Committee** (1977–78) suggested a two-tier structure, and the **L. M. Singhvi Committee** (1986) recommended giving panchayats **constitutional status**.

Prime Minister **Rajiv Gandhi's** government introduced the **64th Constitutional Amendment Bill** in 1989. It passed the Lok Sabha but fell in the Rajya Sabha for want of the required majority. Later, the government of **P. V. Narasimha Rao** introduced revised bills in 1991.

## The 73rd Amendment: Constitutional Status for Panchayats

Parliament passed both amendments in **December 1992**; the Lok Sabha on **22 December** and the Rajya Sabha on **23 December**. After ratification by the states, the **73rd Amendment came into force on 24 April 1993**. It added **Part IX** (Articles 243 to 243-O) and the **Eleventh Schedule** to the Constitution, which lists **29 subjects** such as agriculture, drinking water, rural roads and primary education. Its main provisions were:

- **Gram Sabha** — the assembly of all registered voters of the village, which oversees the work of the panchayat.
- **Three-tier structure** — village panchayat, intermediate (block) panchayat and district panchayat; states with a population below 20 lakh may do without the intermediate level.
- **Five-year term** — if dissolved, elections must be held within six months.
- **Reservation** — seats for the Scheduled Castes and Tribes in proportion to their population, and **at least one-third for women** in total seats and in chairperson posts.
- **State Election Commission** — an independent body to conduct panchayat elections.
- **State Finance Commission** — a review of the financial position of panchayats every five years.

## The 74th Amendment: Municipalities

Alongside it, the **74th Amendment came into force on 1 June 1993**. It added **Part IX-A** (Articles 243-P to 243-ZG) and the **Twelfth Schedule**, which lists **18 subjects** such as urban planning, water supply, sanitation and fire services. It laid down three kinds of urban bodies — the **nagar panchayat** for areas in transition from village to town, the **municipal council** for smaller towns and the **municipal corporation** for large cities. It also provided for **ward committees** in large cities, a **District Planning Committee** in every district and a **Metropolitan Planning Committee** in metropolitan areas. Here too, regular elections and reservation for the Scheduled Castes and Tribes and for women were arranged on the same lines as for panchayats.

## Further Steps

In 1996 the **PESA Act** (Panchayats (Extension to Scheduled Areas) Act) was passed, giving gram sabhas in the tribal areas of the Fifth Schedule special rights over land, forest produce and local resources. Nagaland, Meghalaya, Mizoram and the Sixth Schedule areas were kept out of this system because of their traditional councils. Since **2010**, 24 April has been observed every year as **National Panchayati Raj Day**.

## Legacy and Significance

These two amendments carried Indian democracy beyond Delhi and the state capitals to the village and the neighbourhood. Today lakhs of representatives are elected to more than two and a half lakh gram panchayats and thousands of urban bodies, a large number of them women. Challenges remain, however — in many places panchayats depend on state governments for funds, not all 29 subjects have been fully transferred, and in some places male relatives work in place of elected women representatives, a problem known as **'pradhan pati'** (the headwoman's husband). Despite this, the 73rd and 74th Amendments are regarded as India's biggest constitutional initiative for **decentralisation** and grassroots democracy.
