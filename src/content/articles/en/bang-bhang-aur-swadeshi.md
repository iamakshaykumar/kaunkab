## Background: The Argument of 'Administrative Convenience'

At the start of the 20th century, the Bengal Presidency was the largest province of British India. It included present-day West Bengal, Bangladesh, Bihar and Odisha, and its population ran into crores. The government of Viceroy **Lord Curzon** argued that it was difficult to administer such a large province, so it was necessary to divide it.

But nationalist leaders saw through the real intention hidden behind this argument. Bengal was then the most active centre of Indian nationalism. The partition would divide the Bengali-speaking people into two provinces and would also draw a line on the basis of religion — in the new province of **Eastern Bengal and Assam** (capital Dhaka) Muslims were in the majority, while in the rest of Bengal Hindus were. This was seen as a policy of "divide and rule".

## 16 October 1905: A Day of Mourning

The partition was announced in July 1905 and came into force on **16 October 1905**. Bengal observed this day as a day of national mourning:

- People fasted, no hearths were lit in homes, and "Vande Mataram" echoed through the streets of Calcutta.
- At **Rabindranath Tagore's** call, people tied rakhis on one another as a symbolic vow of Bengal's unity.
- After bathing in the Ganga, processions were taken out, and in meetings people took oaths to boycott foreign goods.

Earlier, on **7 August 1905**, the boycott resolution had been formally passed at a big meeting in the Town Hall of Calcutta.

## Swadeshi and Boycott: The Weapons of the Movement

The protest did not remain limited to petitions and speeches. The movement adopted two main weapons — **boycott** (giving up foreign, especially British, goods such as cloth and salt) and **swadeshi** (using goods made in the country).

- In many places bonfires of foreign cloth were lit and pickets were set up at shops.
- Indigenous industries were encouraged — textile mills, soap and match factories, and swadeshi banks and insurance companies were opened.
- As an alternative to government schools and colleges, the foundation of **national education** was laid; in 1906 the National Council of Education was formed.
- Through "samitis" the message was carried to the villages, and folk songs, plays and journals awakened public consciousness.

The movement spread outside Bengal too. **Bal Gangadhar Tilak** in Maharashtra, **Lala Lajpat Rai** in Punjab, and **Bipin Chandra Pal** and **Aurobindo Ghose** in Bengal became its leading voices. The trio of "Lal-Bal-Pal" became famous in this period.

## Moderates versus Extremists

The Swadeshi movement also deepened differences within the Congress. The **Moderate** (liberal) leaders wanted to keep the boycott limited and stress constitutional methods, while the **Extremist** leaders wanted to make it a wide mass struggle toward complete swaraj. These differences came out in the split of the Congress at the **Surat session** of 1907.

The government carried out harsh repression — bans on meetings, prosecutions of newspapers and arrests of leaders. In 1908 Tilak was sentenced to six years on a charge of sedition and sent to Mandalay (Burma). In this period some young people also turned toward the revolutionary path.

## 1911: The Partition Annulled

After continuous protest the British government had to bend. On **12 December 1911**, at the **Delhi Durbar**, Emperor George V announced the annulment of the Partition of Bengal. Along with it came the announcement of shifting India's capital from Calcutta to Delhi. Bihar and Odisha were separated from Bengal to form a new province.

## Historical Significance

The movement against the partition of Bengal was a decisive turning point in the Indian national movement. For the first time it drew students, women, merchants and ordinary people into politics in large numbers. Ideas such as swadeshi, boycott and national education later became the foundation of the Non-Cooperation and Civil Disobedience movements of the Gandhian era. At the same time, the seeds of communal politics were also sown in this period — the Muslim League was founded in Dhaka in 1906 — and its influence was visible for decades to come.
