## Nimar and Bhil Society

The **Nimar** region of central India stretches across the Narmada valley between the **Satpura** and **Vindhya** hills. In the nineteenth century part of it was directly under British administration (the Central Provinces) and part was in the **Holkar State** of Indore. A large number of people of the **Bhil** community lived here, depending on farming, forest produce and animal husbandry.

After 1857 the British increased their control over the forests. **Forest laws** restricted the traditional rights of tribal people to wood, grazing and hunting in the forest. Along with revenue collection, the grip of **moneylenders** and **malguzars** (village revenue farmers) also tightened; seizing peasants' land by lending at high interest was common. A large section of Bhil society felt deep discontent against this.

## Early Life

Tantya Bhil was born in a village of **Pandhana** tehsil in **Khandwa district**. Sources differ on his date of birth (some give **1840**, others **1842**) and the name of the village is also written in different ways. It is said that in his youth his ancestral land was lost in a dispute with a malguzar and a moneylender, and this injustice made him a rebel.

Around **1874** he was arrested for the first time on a charge of "bad livelihood" and sentenced to a year. In **1878** he was arrested again, but escaped from **Khandwa jail** just three days later. With this began the chapter of his life that made him a people's hero.

## A Challenge from the Forests

From 1878 to 1889, about **eleven years**, Tantya Bhil and his companions were active in the forests of Nimar, Khandesh and the Holkar State. They targeted British government treasuries, rich moneylenders and landlords. According to folk tales he distributed the seized money and grain among the poor, the hungry and those sunk in debt, and helped with the marriages of poor girls. That is why the people kept protecting and hiding him.

For British officials he was a "notorious dacoit", and the police and the army of the Holkar State ran long campaigns to catch him. Thanks to his deep knowledge of forests and hills, his speed and his skill at disguise, he escaped again and again. It was said of him that he was seen in several places at once, a sign of both his popularity and his mystery.

Two views of him are found in historical writing. Colonial documents treat him as a criminal, while Indian folk memory and later historians see him as a symbol of **tribal resistance** against exploitation.

## Capture and Execution

In the end Tantya Bhil was caught through treachery. According to popular accounts, **Ganpat**, the husband of his adopted sister, betrayed him. He was called to a parley with the promise of a pardon and captured in an ambush. He was first held at **Indore**, then taken under heavy guard to **Jabalpur**.

He was tried at Jabalpur, and on **19 October 1889** the sessions court sentenced him to death. According to the most widely accepted date, he was hanged on **4 December 1889**. Some writers say there is no full certainty about the exact date of the execution. News of his capture reached abroad: in **1889** the American newspaper **The New York Times** called him **"the Robin Hood of India"**.

It is believed that after the execution his body was thrown into the forest at **Patalpani** near Indore, close to the Khandwa railway line. Today the samadhi (memorial) and temple of **Tantya Mama** stand there.

## Legacy

The memory of Tantya Bhil runs deep in the Bhil society of Nimar and Malwa. Folk songs are sung in his name and he is worshipped like a deity. There is a popular belief about Patalpani that trains passing through stop for a few moments in his honour.

The Madhya Pradesh government observes **4 December** as his **Balidan Diwas** (sacrifice day). In **2021** the state government announced that Patalpani railway station and a bus stand in Indore would be named after him. A film on his life was also made in **2012**.

The story of Tantya Mama shows that in colonial India tribal society fought in its own way against forest laws, land revenue and the exploitation of moneylenders. He is remembered in the same tradition as Bhima Nayak, Birsa Munda and other tribal heroes who refused to bow before injustice.
