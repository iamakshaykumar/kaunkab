## Background: After the Salt Satyagraha

Gandhi had started the **Civil Disobedience Movement** with the **Dandi March** of **March–April 1930**. Across the country the salt law was broken, there were pickets at shops selling foreign cloth and liquor, and campaigns of non-payment of land revenue were run. The government imposed harsh ordinances, banned Congress and sent thousands of people to jail. Gandhi himself was also arrested.

Meanwhile in London the **First Round Table Conference** (1930–31) was held without Congress, and the British Prime Minister Ramsay MacDonald wanted Congress to take part in the next meeting. Against this background the Viceroy **Lord Irwin** released Gandhi and the members of the Congress Working Committee unconditionally from **Yerwada Jail** and other jails on **26 January 1931**.

## The Negotiations

Moderate leaders such as **Tej Bahadur Sapru** and **M. R. Jayakar** played a part in bringing both sides to the table. On **14 February 1931** the Congress Working Committee authorised Gandhi to talk to the Viceroy, and on **17 February** the first meeting took place in Delhi. Gandhi's demands included a general amnesty, an end to repression, the return of confiscated property, freedom to make salt and an **inquiry into police excesses**. Irwin was not ready to agree to a police inquiry. After eight meetings over about three weeks, agreement was reached on the night of **4 March**, and on **5 March 1931** the two signed the pact at the Viceroy's House. It is also formally called the **"Delhi Pact"**.

## Main Terms of the Pact

**The government agreed:**
- The **ordinances** connected with the movement would be **withdrawn** and the cases ended.
- **Political prisoners** would be **released**, except those accused of violence.
- The **confiscated property** of satyagrahis (if not yet sold) would be returned.
- People living **near the seacoast** could **make or collect salt** for their own use.
- **Peaceful picketing** of liquor and foreign cloth shops would be allowed.

**Congress agreed:**
- The **Civil Disobedience Movement** would be suspended.
- Congress would take part in the **Second Round Table Conference**.

It is worth noting that the movement was "discontinued" (suspended), not "ended", so that it could be restarted if needed.

## Criticism and Controversy

The pact was criticised from both sides. In Britain, hardline imperialists such as **Winston Churchill** objected that the Viceroy was talking on equal terms with a "seditious" leader. In India many young leaders felt that the movement had been halted at a time when its pressure was at its peak, and that no firm promise had been obtained on the way to Purna Swaraj.

The biggest controversy was over the hanging of **Bhagat Singh, Rajguru and Sukhdev**. Gandhi urged the Viceroy to commute their sentence, but it could not become part of the pact, and on **23 March 1931** all three were hanged. How much effort Gandhi made is still debated by historians and the public today — some consider it sufficient effort, while others think he should have made it a condition of the pact.

## The Karachi Session and After

The Congress **Karachi session**, which began on **26 March 1931**, approved the pact on **30 March**, despite the anger caused by Bhagat Singh's hanging, and chose Gandhi as Congress's sole representative at the Round Table Conference. Gandhi went to London in the second half of 1931, but the **Second Round Table Conference** ended without result over the question of communal representation. As soon as he returned to India the government's policy became harsh again, and the Civil Disobedience Movement was restarted in early 1932.

Even so, the pact was important in that for the first time the British government negotiated with Congress and its leader **on an equal footing** and made a written agreement. This strengthened Congress's standing as the main representative of the Indian people.
