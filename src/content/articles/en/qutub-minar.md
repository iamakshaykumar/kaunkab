## Background: A New Power in Delhi

After the Second Battle of Tarain in 1192, the armies of Muhammad of Ghor took Delhi. Ghor's commander **Qutb-ud-din Aibak** had a congregational mosque and, near it, a huge minar begun here, in the area of the old Qila Rai Pithora (today's Mehrauli), as symbols of the new power. After Ghor's death in 1206, Aibak became the first independent sultan of Delhi.

There are two views on the name of the minar — some link it to Qutb-ud-din Aibak, others to the Sufi saint Khwaja Qutbuddin Bakhtiyar Kaki.

## Building the Minar, Storey by Storey

- Around **1199** Aibak built the first storey.
- His son-in-law and successor **Iltutmish** added three more storeys by about **1220**.
- In 1368–69 the upper storey was damaged by lightning. **Firuz Shah Tughluq** had two storeys built in its place, using white marble. Thus the minar has five storeys today.
- After the earthquake of 1505, Sikandar Lodi also had it repaired.

The height of the minar is about **72.5 metres**. Its diameter is about 14.3 metres at the base and about 2.7 metres at the top. This red and brown sandstone minar has raised flutings, a balcony on every storey and honeycomb-like carving beneath them. On its walls are carved verses of the Quran in Arabic (Naskh script) and inscriptions of the builders. Its purpose is believed to have been both a tower for the call to prayer and a victory tower.

## The Quwwat-ul-Islam Mosque

The **Quwwat-ul-Islam Mosque**, built near the minar, is among the oldest surviving mosques of Delhi; its construction began in the 1190s. According to the inscription on its eastern gate, materials of 27 Hindu and Jain temples were used in it. On the pillars of the courtyard one can still see carvings of bells, urns and floral designs.

The high arched screen in front of the mosque was built by local craftsmen. In these arches the stones are joined by projecting them one above another (the corbel technique), and Indian-style creepers and flowers are carved on them along with verses of the Quran. Historians regard this as an early example of the blending of two architectural traditions. Later Iltutmish and Alauddin Khilji had the mosque extended.

## The Iron Pillar: The Oldest Part of the Complex

The **iron pillar** standing in the mosque courtyard is about eight hundred years older than the sultanate. It is about 7.2 metres high and weighs more than six tonnes. It bears a Sanskrit inscription (in Brahmi script) of a king **'Chandra'**, who is generally taken to be the Gupta emperor **Chandragupta II** (c. 375–415 CE). The inscription says it was set up as a Vishnu-standard on a 'Vishnupada' hill.

Where the pillar originally stood (Udayagiri, Vidisha or Mathura), and when and by whom it was brought to Delhi — scholars do not agree on this.

## Other Monuments of the Complex and Legacy

In the same complex are also the **tomb of Iltutmish**, the **Alai Darwaza** (1311) built by Alauddin Khilji, and his unfinished **Alai Minar**. Alauddin wanted to build a minar twice as high as the Qutb, but after his death the work stopped at the first storey.

In 1993 UNESCO declared "Qutb Minar and its Monuments" a World Heritage Site. Having withstood earthquakes and lightning over the centuries, the minar still stands today and is the most recognised symbol of the early architecture of the Delhi Sultanate.
