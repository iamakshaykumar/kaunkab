## Life: Fact and Tradition

Very little about the life of Goswami Tulsidas comes from firm documents; most information comes from later-written biographies and folk tradition. His childhood name is said to be **Rambola**. There is disagreement about his birth year — most often **about 1532 CE** is accepted, while some traditions give 1511 or 1497 CE. As birthplace, the claim of **Rajapur (Chitrakoot, Uttar Pradesh)** is the most prominent, although Soron (Kasganj) also makes its own claim. He died in Kashi in **about 1623 CE**.

According to tradition, after being separated from his family in childhood he received the protection of the saint **Narharidas**, from whom he heard the Ram story. The famous story that detachment from the world was awakened in him by a harsh word from his wife Ratnavali is also part of folk tradition; some scholars even doubt his marriage.

## 1574: The Beginning of the Ramcharitmanas

In the Bala Kanda of the Ramcharitmanas the poet himself says that he began writing this story at **Ayodhya** on the ninth day of Chaitra in **Vikram Samvat 1631** — that is, on **Ram Navami**. This year falls at about **1574 CE**. The larger part of the book is believed to have been written in **Kashi (Varanasi)**. One popular tradition says the composition took about two years and seven months to complete, but there is no independent evidence for this.

This was the time when Akbar ruled in northern India and the Bhakti movement was at its peak — saints such as Kabir, Surdas and Mirabai were singing devotion to God in the popular languages.

## Composition and Style of the Book

The Ramcharitmanas is divided into **seven kandas** — Bala, Ayodhya, Aranya, Kishkindha, Sundara, Lanka (Yuddha) and Uttara Kanda. Its main metrical scheme is the **chaupai**, with **dohas**, sorathas and other metres coming in between.

Tulsidas took material from the Valmiki Ramayana as well as the Adhyatma Ramayana and other Puranic traditions, but told the story in his own way. His Ram is 'Maryada Purushottam', and the idea of devotion, duty, friendship and the ideal state — 'Ramrajya' — is central to the story. The story is woven into a frame of dialogues between Shiva and Parvati, Kakbhushundi and Garuda, and Yajnavalkya and Bharadvaja.

## The Choice of a Popular Language

Tulsidas was a scholar of Sanskrit, yet he wrote the Manas in **Awadhi** — the language spoken by ordinary people of the Awadh region. According to tradition, some pandits of Kashi even objected to writing a sacred text in 'bhakha' instead of Sanskrit. But this very choice became the Manas's greatest strength: both the educated and the unlettered could hear and understand it.

Besides the Manas, Tulsidas's other major works are **Vinaya Patrika**, **Kavitavali**, **Gitavali**, **Dohavali**, **Janaki Mangal** and **Parvati Mangal**. These are in both Awadhi and Braj Bhasha. The popular **Hanuman Chalisa** is also believed to be his work, though some scholars question this.

## Ramlila and Folk Culture

The tradition of staging **Ramlila** in Kashi is linked to Tulsidas and his disciples. Later the Ramlila of Ramnagar (Varanasi), which lasts about a month, became very famous. Traditions of Manas recitation, Akhand Ramayana and katha narration made this book a part of the religious and cultural life of northern India.

## Historical Significance

The Ramcharitmanas is among the most widely read and sung texts of Hindi literature. It gave Awadhi literary stature and gave the 'saguna' stream of the Bhakti movement a lasting base. In the nineteenth century indentured labourers carried it to Mauritius, Fiji, Trinidad and Suriname, where it is still part of cultural identity today. Historians of literature also debate some of the social ideas of the text, but almost all agree on its poetic art and wide influence.
