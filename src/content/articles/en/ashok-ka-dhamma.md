## A New Path After War

In the eighth year of his coronation (about **261 BCE**) the Mauryan emperor **Ashoka** invaded **Kalinga**. In the Thirteenth Major Rock Edict he writes himself that in this war one and a half hundred thousand people were taken away as captives, one hundred thousand were killed and many times that number died — and that he felt deep remorse over this. After this, Ashoka declared **"conquest by dhamma"** (dhamma-vijaya) instead of **conquest by war** as his goal.

For Ashoka, **dhamma** (the Prakrit form of the Sanskrit "dharma") was not a new sect. It was a general path of moral conduct that anyone — Buddhist, Brahmin, Ajivika or Jain — could follow. Ashoka had personally become a Buddhist lay follower, but the dhamma of his inscriptions does not promote any one sect.

## What Dhamma Contained

According to the inscriptions, the main elements of dhamma were:

- **Non-violence** — in the First Rock Edict, a ban on animal sacrifice for rituals and on fairs and festivals where animals were killed.
- **Obedience** to parents, **respect** for elders and teachers, and **good treatment** of servants and slaves.
- **Gifts** to Brahmins and shramanas, generosity towards friends and relatives, and little expenditure and little hoarding.
- **Tolerance** — in the Twelfth Rock Edict Ashoka says that the essence of all sects should grow, and that no one should praise his own sect while condemning others.
- **Welfare of the people** — according to the Second Rock Edict, medical treatment for humans and animals, planting medicinal herbs, digging wells and planting trees along roads.

Ashoka described pointless rituals (ceremonies at illness, marriage and birth) as "of little fruit" and said that the true auspicious act is the practice of dhamma.

## The Administration of Dhamma

To spread dhamma, Ashoka used the machinery of government. According to the Third Rock Edict, the **yuktas, rajjukas** and **pradeshikas** had to tour their areas every **five years** and teach dhamma. According to the Fifth Rock Edict, in the **thirteenth year** of his coronation Ashoka created a new post — the **dhamma-mahamatra**. These officers worked among all sects, among the frontier Yavana, Kamboja and Gandhara peoples, and for the welfare of the poor, the aged and prisoners.

Ashoka replaced hunting expeditions with **"dhamma-yatras"** (dhamma tours); according to the Eighth Rock Edict, in his tenth year he went to **Sambodhi** (Bodh Gaya). In the Sixth Rock Edict he says that news of the people's affairs should be reported to him at all times — whether he is eating or in the inner apartments.

## Messages Written on Stone

More than thirty of Ashoka's inscriptions have been found in present-day India, Nepal, Pakistan, Bangladesh and Afghanistan. They are mainly divided into four groups:

- **Major Rock Edicts** — a series of 14 edicts carved on large rocks at places such as Girnar, Kalsi, Shahbazgarhi, Mansehra, Dhauli, Jaugada and Erragudi. At Dhauli and Jaugada there are also two **Separate Rock Edicts** meant for Kalinga.
- **Minor Rock Edicts** — which mention Ashoka's becoming a Buddhist personally.
- **Major Pillar Edicts** — 7 edicts dating from the 26th–27th year of his coronation; the seventh is found only on the Delhi-Topra pillar.
- **Minor Pillar Edicts** — such as the Lumbini pillar, which is evidence of Ashoka's visit to the Buddha's birthplace.

Most of the inscriptions are in the **Prakrit** language and the **Brahmi** script. In the north-west the **Kharosthi** script was used, and in Afghanistan the **Greek** and **Aramaic** languages — a bilingual (Greek-Aramaic) inscription has been found at Kandahar.

## Prinsep and the Inscriptions Read Again

After the Mauryas the Brahmi script changed gradually, and after a few centuries no one could read these inscriptions. **James Prinsep**, secretary of the Asiatic Society of Bengal, identified the Brahmi letters between 1836 and 1838 with the help of the small donation inscriptions at Sanchi; his work of **1837** is considered decisive. The king of the inscriptions called himself **"Devanampiya Piyadasi"** (Beloved of the Gods, of Gracious Looks) — the Sri Lankan chronicle *Dipavamsa* helped link this with Ashoka, but the identification was confirmed when, in **1915**, C. Beadon found a minor rock edict at **Maski** in Karnataka in which the king's name **"Ashoka"** was written plainly.

## Significance

Ashoka's inscriptions are the oldest legible examples in India of a ruler speaking in his own words. They give direct information about Mauryan administration, the borders of the empire and relations with the Greek kingdoms. Historians also remind us that these inscriptions are the king's own account — how far the ideals of dhamma were put into practice on the ground is hard to say. Even so, because of these ideas of tolerance and public welfare, Ashoka's **lion capital** and **Dhammachakra** are today part of the state emblems of independent India.
