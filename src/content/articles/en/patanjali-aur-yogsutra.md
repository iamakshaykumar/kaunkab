## Who Was Patanjali

In Indian tradition the name **Patanjali** is linked with three great works — the Yoga Sutras, the **Mahabhashya** on Panini's grammar, and a text on Ayurveda. Tradition regards all three as the work of one sage and calls him an incarnation of Shesha. But modern historians generally believe that the Patanjali who wrote the Mahabhashya (about the 2nd century BCE, the Shunga period) and the Patanjali of the Yoga Sutras were different persons, because there is a great difference in the language, style and subject of the two works.

Almost nothing is known for certain about the life of the author of the Yoga Sutras. His name appears in the colophons at the end of the text, and that is all.

## The Debate over the Date of Composition

Scholars differ greatly on the date of the Yoga Sutras — estimates range from 500 BCE to 450 CE. The more accepted view today is for the later date. The scholar **Philipp Maas** argues that the sutras and the oldest commentary on them together make up a single work — the **'Patanjala Yogashastra'** — composed between about 325 and 425 CE. The similarity of the sutras to the vocabulary of Buddhist philosophy also points to such a later date. Some scholars consider the fourth pada a later addition.

## The Structure of the Text

The Yoga Sutras has about **195-196 sutras** (the count varies slightly by tradition), divided into four **padas**:

- **Samadhi Pada** (51 sutras) — the definition of yoga and the kinds of samadhi;
- **Sadhana Pada** (55 sutras) — Kriya yoga, the afflictions and the first limbs of Ashtanga yoga;
- **Vibhuti Pada** (56 sutras) — dharana, dhyana, samadhi and the powers gained from them;
- **Kaivalya Pada** (34 sutras) — the state of liberation or 'kaivalya'.

The second sutra is the essence of the whole text — **"Yogash chitta-vritti-nirodhah"**, that is, yoga is the stilling of the fluctuations (vrittis) of the chitta (mind).

## Ashtanga Yoga

In the Sadhana and Vibhuti padas the **eight limbs** of yoga are described:

1. **Yama** — ahimsa, truth, asteya (not stealing), brahmacharya, aparigraha;
2. **Niyama** — cleanliness, contentment, austerity, self-study, devotion to God;
3. **Asana** — a steady and comfortable sitting posture;
4. **Pranayama** — control of the breath;
5. **Pratyahara** — withdrawing the senses from their objects;
6. **Dharana** — fixing the mind in one place;
7. **Dhyana** — the continuous flow of that concentration;
8. **Samadhi** — complete absorption.

## The Roots of the Philosophy

The philosophical basis of the Yoga Sutras is mainly the **Samkhya** philosophy — the distinction between **Purusha** (consciousness) and **Prakriti** (the inert world), and the three gunas. The difference is that the Yoga Sutras accept **Ishvara** as a special Purusha, so it is sometimes called "Samkhya with God". Its goal is **Kaivalya** — the seer (Purusha) separating from Prakriti and resting in its own nature. Its yamas match the Jain vows and many of its terms match Buddhist texts, which shows the mutual dialogue of the philosophies of that time.

## Commentaries, Translations and the Modern Revival

The oldest commentary on the sutras is famous under the name of **Vyasa**. Later scholars such as **Vachaspati Mishra** and **Vijnanabhikshu** wrote commentaries on them. At the beginning of the 11th century the Central Asian scholar **Al-Biruni** translated it into Arabic. After the 12th century its influence declined somewhat, but **Swami Vivekananda's** book **'Raja Yoga'** (1896) placed it before the world again. Translated into many languages in the 20th century, the Yoga Sutras became the most famous classical text of yoga.
