## From Faizabad to the Palace of Lucknow

Begum Hazrat Mahal's original name is said to have been **Muhammadi Khanum**. She was born around **1820** in **Faizabad** in Awadh, in an ordinary family. According to the sources, she reached the royal palace of Lucknow in childhood and later became one of the wives of Nawab **Wajid Ali Shah**. After the birth of her son **Birjis Qadr** she received the title **"Hazrat Mahal"**. She was not among the chief begums of the Nawab — which makes her rise in 1857 all the more remarkable.

## The Annexation of Awadh

In **February 1856** Governor-General **Dalhousie** annexed Awadh into the Company's territory on a charge of "misrule", and Wajid Ali Shah was sent to Calcutta. Hazrat Mahal stayed on in Lucknow. This annexation hurt the taluqdars, courtiers, soldiers and ordinary people of Awadh alike; many sepoys of the Company's army also came from Awadh.

## 1857: Rebel Rule in Lucknow

In May–June 1857 the revolt spread in Awadh. After the defeat of the British force at **Chinhat** on **30 June 1857**, British officers and their companions were besieged in the **Residency** at Lucknow; in the early days of the siege Chief Commissioner **Henry Lawrence** was killed.

In **July 1857** (often given as **5 July**) the rebel chiefs proclaimed the boy Birjis Qadr ruler of Awadh, and Hazrat Mahal began to rule as his **regent**. She held court, brought chiefs and taluqdars together, organised armies, and asserted the legitimacy of her rule in the name of the Delhi emperor Bahadur Shah Zafar. In September 1857 the forces of Havelock and Outram reached the Residency, but were themselves besieged; in November **Colin Campbell** took the besieged people out.

## The Fall of Lucknow and the Continuing Struggle

In **March 1858** Campbell's large army recaptured Lucknow after heavy fighting. Hazrat Mahal, with her son and supporters, left the city and went to the northern countryside of Awadh — toward **Bahraich** and the Nepal Terai — and continued the struggle. Here leaders such as Nana Sahib also joined her.

In Queen Victoria's proclamation of **1 November 1858**, the Company's rule was ended and a promise of pardon and non-interference in religious matters was made. In a **counter-proclamation** issued in the name of Birjis Qadr, which is linked with Hazrat Mahal, distrust of these promises was expressed and people were told not to trust the British.

## Exile in Nepal

By 1859 most of her companions had scattered. She reached Nepal, whose ruler **Jung Bahadur Rana** — who had helped the British in the battle of Lucknow — at first refused to give her refuge, but later let her stay. She did not accept the British offers to return in exchange for an allowance. She died at **Kathmandu** on **7 April 1879** and was buried near the Jama Masjid there.

Begum Hazrat Mahal was among those leaders of 1857 who governed a large region and never surrendered to the end. Many personal details of her life — especially her early life — are disputed or unclear, and doubts are also raised about the authenticity of her popular portraits; but her central role in the resistance of Awadh is clear from contemporary records.
