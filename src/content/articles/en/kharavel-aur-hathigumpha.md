## Kalinga After Ashoka

In the third century BCE the Mauryan emperor **Ashoka** conquered Kalinga (present-day Odisha and the surrounding region). After the Mauryan empire weakened, Kalinga rose again as an independent and powerful kingdom. The most famous ruler of this period was **Kharavela**, who describes himself as a descendant of the **Mahameghavahana** line and of the **Chedi** royal family.

Almost all our information about Kharavela comes from a single source — the inscription engraved on the **Hathigumpha** (Elephant Cave) on **Udayagiri hill** near Bhubaneswar. Scholars differ about its date; on the basis of the script most place it at about the **first century BCE**, while some place it in the second century BCE or the first century CE.

## The Nature of the Inscription

- The inscription is in the **Prakrit** language and **Brahmi** script and has **seventeen lines**.
- It is engraved on the front part and ceiling of the natural rock of the cave. Over time many letters have worn away, so the readings of many lines are still disputed.
- It begins with the Jain salutation, which shows that Kharavela was a follower of **Jainism**.
- According to the accepted reading, Kharavela spent fifteen years in childhood play and education, was crown prince for nine years, and was crowned at the age of twenty-four.

## A Year-by-Year Account of His Reign

The special feature of the inscription is that it narrates the events of Kharavela's regnal years in order (the details below are based on the accepted reading):
- **First year** — repair of the gates, walls and reservoirs of the capital Kalinganagari, damaged by a storm.
- **Second year** — sending an army to the west without regard for **Satakarni** (the Satavahana king).
- **Fifth year** — extending to the capital a canal dug by a **Nanda king**.
- **Eighth year** — a campaign towards Magadha and pressure on Rajagriha. In the same line the retreat of a Yavana (Greek) king towards Mathura has been read, but both this reading and the king's name are doubtful.
- **Twelfth year** — the submission of the king of Magadha, and the bringing back of the **Jina image** that the Nanda king had carried away from Kalinga. In the same year, receipt of tribute from the Pandya king is also mentioned.

The inscription also describes festivals, music and dance, tax remissions and donations. At the end Kharavela is called "one who respects all sects".

## The Caves of Udayagiri and Khandagiri

According to the inscription, Kharavela had caves made on the Kumari hill (today's Udayagiri hill) for the residence of Jain monks. In the Udayagiri and the nearby Khandagiri hills there are many rock-cut caves, some of which have beautiful carving. They are good examples of the early rock-cut architecture of ancient India.

## Discovery and Study

For a long time the inscription remained unknown to scholars. **A. Stirling** published information about it in *Asiatic Researches* in 1825. In 1837 **James Prinsep** published a copy made by Kittoe, and later scholars such as Alexander Cunningham, Bhagwanlal Indraji, K. P. Jayaswal and R. D. Banerji carried forward the work of reading it. Even today the meanings of many of its lines are debated.

## Significance

The Hathigumpha inscription is among the oldest inscriptions to give a year-by-year account of the rule of an ancient Indian king. It sheds light on Kalinga, the Satavahanas and Magadha in the post-Mauryan period, on royal patronage of Jainism and on the urban life of the time. Without this inscription, even the name of a powerful ruler like Kharavela would perhaps have been lost to history.
