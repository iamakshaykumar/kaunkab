## The Fear of Famine and 'Ship to Mouth'

Even after independence, India was not able to grow enough grain for its population. In the 1950s and 1960s the country imported huge quantities of wheat under America's **PL-480** programme; the dependence was so great that the situation came to be called 'ship to mouth'. The consecutive droughts of 1964–65 and 1965–66 deepened the crisis further. Farmers did not get cheap credit, and the yield of traditional varieties was falling short for the growing population.

## Seeds from Mexico

The American agricultural scientist **Norman Borlaug** had developed **dwarf, high-yielding varieties** (HYV) of wheat in Mexico, which did not fall over when given fertiliser and water and gave several times more grain. **M. S. Swaminathan**, a scientist at the **Indian Agricultural Research Institute** (Pusa) in New Delhi, took an interest in these varieties and invited Borlaug to India. In October 1963 about 100 kg of seed of four Mexican varieties reached India and trials began in different parts of the country.

Under Prime Minister **Lal Bahadur Shastri** and Minister of Food and Agriculture **C. Subramaniam**, the government adopted this new strategy. In 1966 it was decided to import about 18,000 tonnes of seed of the **Lerma Rojo** and **Sonora 64** varieties from Mexico; some sources record this import in 1967. These seeds were sown on a large scale in the 1966–67 rabi crop. Indira Gandhi's government also carried the programme forward.

## The Package: Seed, Water, Fertiliser and Price

The Green Revolution was not limited to seeds. Along with it came **irrigation by canals and tubewells**, **chemical fertilisers and pesticides**, agricultural credit, machines such as tractors, and extension services through agricultural universities. In 1965 the **Food Corporation of India** and the Agricultural Prices Commission were set up, so that farmers could be assured of government purchase of grain at a **minimum support price**. High-yielding varieties came in rice as well, but progress there was relatively slow and depended more on the spread of irrigation.

## Results

The biggest impact was seen in wheat. In **Punjab, Haryana and western Uttar Pradesh**, which had water facilities, yield and production rose rapidly, and within a few years India's wheat production increased several times over. In the 1970s India largely ended its dependence on food-grain imports and buffer stocks began to build up in government warehouses. In 1970 Borlaug received the Nobel Peace Prize for his work; Swaminathan is regarded as the chief scientific architect of the Green Revolution in India.

## Criticisms

The Green Revolution also has several serious criticisms:

- **Regional inequality** — The benefits went mainly to irrigated areas and wheat-growing regions; rain-fed and eastern regions were left behind.
- **Burden on small farmers** — Seeds, fertilisers, pesticides and irrigation were expensive, so many small farmers were trapped in high-interest debt; larger farmers benefited relatively more.
- **Environment** — Heavy use of chemical fertilisers and pesticides lowered soil quality and increased water pollution; over-extraction through tubewells caused the groundwater level in Punjab and Haryana to keep falling.
- **Biodiversity** — The wheat–paddy cropping cycle pushed pulses, millets and traditional varieties into the background.

Critics such as the environmentalist **Vandana Shiva** consider it harmful to sustainable farming, while supporters argue that without it a great famine would have been hard to avoid in the 1960s and 1970s.

## Significance

The Green Revolution took India out of the 'ship to mouth' situation and towards self-reliance in food grain, and also increased its independence in foreign policy. At the same time, its long-term side effects have set today's farming challenges of sustainability, crop diversity and groundwater conservation, which are discussed in ideas such as the 'Evergreen Revolution'.
