## The Sixteen Mahajanapadas and Magadha

Around the 6th century BCE there were **sixteen mahajanapadas** in North India — kingdoms such as Kosala, Vatsa, Avanti, Vajji, Anga and Magadha. Of these, **Magadha** (today's southern Bihar) went on to become the most powerful. Historians give the reasons: fertile land, elephants and timber from the surrounding forests, access to iron mines, and the convenience of trade along the Ganga and its tributaries.

The first major dynasty to give Magadha this advantage was the **Haryanka dynasty**, whose capital was **Rajagriha** (today's Rajgir). Surrounded by five hills, this capital was naturally secure.

## The Problem of Dates

According to the Sri Lankan Buddhist text **Mahavamsa**, Bimbisara ruled from about 544 to 492 BCE and Ajatashatru from about 492 to 460 BCE. The Mahavamsa says that Bimbisara became king at the age of fifteen. But because of disagreement over the date of the Buddha's nirvana, many modern scholars place these kings about a century later (from the 5th century to the beginning of the 4th century BCE). The dates given here are therefore traditional and approximate.

## Bimbisara — Marriage and Conquest

Bimbisara used both war and marriage to increase Magadha's power:
- **Kosala Devi** — sister of Prasenajit, king of Kosala; as dowry came the region of **Kashi**, and enmity with Kosala was calmed.
- **Chellana** — daughter of **Chetaka**, the Licchavi king of Vaishali.
- **Kshema (Khema)** — a princess of the Madra clan of Punjab.

In the east he conquered the kingdom of **Anga** (capital Champa), merged it into Magadha, and made his son **Kunika** (Ajatashatru) its ruler. This increased Magadha's control over the trade routes leading towards the mouth of the Ganga. In his court was the famous physician **Jivaka**.

Both the Buddhist and Jain traditions claim Bimbisara as a follower. According to Buddhist tradition, he gave the Veluvana near Rajagriha as a gift to the Buddha and his sangha.

## Ajatashatru

Bimbisara's end is described as tragic, but the accounts differ. According to Buddhist texts, under the influence of Devadatta, Ajatashatru imprisoned his father and had him killed; according to Jain texts, the imprisoned Bimbisara gave up his own life. Which account is correct is not certain.

Under Ajatashatru the Haryanka dynasty reached its greatest extent. He fought Kosala and, after a long war, defeated the powerful **Vajji confederacy** led by the Licchavis. Jain texts mention weapons such as the "Mahashilakantaka" (a machine that hurled large stones) and the "Rathamusala" (a chariot fitted with blades) in this war. The fortification of **Pataligrama** on the bank of the Ganga began in this period. According to Buddhist tradition, after the Buddha's nirvana the first Buddhist council was held at Rajagriha under Ajatashatru's patronage.

## Udayin and the End of the Dynasty

After Ajatashatru, **Udayin** became king, and in his time the capital was moved from Rajagriha to **Pataliputra** — the same city that later became the capital of the Maurya empire. The later kings were weak. According to the Mahavamsa, the minister **Shishunaga** removed the last Haryanka king **Nagadasaka** and began a new dynasty in about 413 BCE.

## Significance

The Haryanka kings turned Magadha from a small janapada into the leading power of North India. The huge ancient stone walls still visible on the hills of Rajgir (called the "Cyclopean Wall") are a reminder of that old city's defences, although their exact date is debated. Later, the Shishunaga, Nanda and Maurya dynasties built India's first great empires from this same Magadha.
