## Background: Vellore After Tipu

After the death of Tipu Sultan in the battle of Srirangapatna in 1799, the British placed his family — sons, queens and hundreds of dependants — under house arrest in **Vellore Fort** in Tamil Nadu. The same fort was also the garrison of Indian battalions of the Madras Army and of the British **69th Regiment**. Many old soldiers and courtiers of Mysore lived in the surrounding area, and they felt resentment toward the British.

## The Spark: New Dress Regulations

In November 1805 **Sir John Craddock**, Commander-in-Chief of the Madras Army, brought in new rules for the soldiers. According to them:

- Hindu soldiers on duty could not wear religious marks such as the tilak on the forehead;
- Muslim soldiers had to shave their beards and trim their moustaches;
- instead of the turban they had to wear a new round hat with a leather crest.

The soldiers felt this was a direct attack on their religion and caste, and a rumour spread that an attempt was being made to convert them to Christianity. Leather, which was suspected of being made from the hide of an animal, was objectionable to both communities. In May 1806 some soldiers who protested were also given harsh punishment by flogging, which inflamed discontent further.

## The Night of 10 July 1806

On **10 July 1806**, about two hours after midnight, the soldiers took up arms. They fired on the British barracks and killed many officers, including the fort commandant **Colonel St John Fancourt**. According to figures cited on Wikipedia, 14 British officers of the sepoy regiments and 115 soldiers of the 69th Regiment were killed.

The rebels hoisted the **flag of the Sultanate of Mysore** over the fort. Some servants of Tipu's second son **Fateh Hyder** also joined them. However, historians disagree about the role of Tipu's sons — some accounts speak of their participation in the plot, while it is also recorded that after the revolt broke out they hesitated to take up the leadership.

## Gillespie's Suppression

An officer who escaped from the fort carried the news to the **Arcot** garrison about 16 miles away. From there **Colonel Robert Rollo Gillespie** arrived with the 19th Light Dragoons (cavalry), guns and the Madras Cavalry, and within about nine hours of the outbreak blew open the gate of the fort and entered.

A terrible revenge followed. About 350 rebels were killed and as many wounded; about 100 soldiers hiding in the palace were shot on Gillespie's orders. Later, as formal punishment, some of the rebels were blown from the mouths of cannon, some were shot, some hanged and some exiled. The three battalions of the Madras Army involved were disbanded.

## Consequences

The Company withdrew the disputed dress regulations. Both the Governor **Lord William Bentinck** and the Commander-in-Chief **Craddock** were recalled. Tipu's family was removed from Vellore and sent to Calcutta, so that no rising in the south could again take place in his name.

## Historical Significance

The Vellore Mutiny ended in one day and could not spread, yet its significance is great. It showed how explosive interference with the religious feelings and customs of soldiers could be — the same question rose again fifty years later in 1857 in the form of the greased cartridges. For this reason it is often called the first major mutiny of Indian soldiers and a forewarning of 1857. In 2006, on its 200th anniversary, the Government of India issued a postage stamp.
