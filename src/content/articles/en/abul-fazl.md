## Born into a Scholarly Family

Abul Fazl was born on **14 January 1551** in **Agra**. His father, **Shaikh Mubarak**, was a famous scholar whose liberal religious views kept the orthodox ulama angry with him. Abul Fazl's elder brother **Faizi** was a great Persian poet and later became Akbar's poet laureate.

From childhood Abul Fazl studied philosophy, theology and Persian-Arabic literature deeply. Because of his father's opponents, the family also had to face difficulties several times.

## At Akbar's Court

In **1575** Abul Fazl came to Akbar's court. This was the time when Akbar was holding discussions among scholars of different religions in the **Ibadat Khana** at Fatehpur Sikri. Abul Fazl soon came to be counted among the emperor's most trusted advisers and is considered one of Akbar's 'Navratnas' (Nine Jewels).

In the 1580s and 1590s Akbar's religious policy grew more and more liberal. Abul Fazl gave words to this outlook as **'Sulh-i-Kul'** — peace with all. For him the emperor was a just ruler who governed standing above all religions and communities.

## The Akbarnama: History of a Reign

On Akbar's orders, Abul Fazl wrote the **Akbarnama** in Persian. It is in three volumes:
- **First volume** — the history of Timur's line, Babur, Humayun and the Sur rulers, and the birth of Akbar.
- **Second volume** — the events of Akbar's reign, up to 1602.
- **Third volume** — the **Ain-i-Akbari**, that is, the "Institutes of Akbar".

The Akbarnama is not merely a list of events. Abul Fazl wrote it by collecting official documents, farmans and people's accounts. At the same time its language is ornate and it openly praises Akbar.

## The Ain-i-Akbari: The Empire's Gazetteer

The Ain-i-Akbari is a sort of detailed official account of the Mughal Empire. It describes the emperor's household, the army, the **mansabdari** system, the geography of the provinces and the revenue system. Because of its figures on crop yields, prices and wages, it remains an invaluable source for economic history even today.

It also introduces the various schools of Indian philosophy, customs and branches of learning. Historians believe that Abul Fazl did not know Sanskrit, so he must have gathered this information through pandits and translators.

## The Deccan and a Tragic End

In **1599** Abul Fazl was sent to the Deccan, where he led the Mughal army. Meanwhile Prince **Salim** (later Jahangir) had rebelled against his father and was running an independent court at Allahabad. Salim felt that Abul Fazl was inciting the emperor against him.

In 1602, as Abul Fazl was returning from the Deccan to Agra, near **Narwar** the Bundela chief of Orchha, **Vir Singh Deo**, attacked and murdered him at Salim's instigation. His head was sent to Salim at Allahabad. The date of the murder is given variously in the sources within August 1602 (12 or 22 August). He was buried at **Antri**. It is said that Akbar was plunged into deep grief at the death of his friend.

## Assessment

Abul Fazl's works are among the most important sources of Mughal history. Even so, historians read him with caution, because he was a court writer and shaping Akbar's image was also part of his purpose. His contemporary **Abd al-Qadir Badauni**, in his 'Muntakhab-ut-Tawarikh', sharply criticised Akbar's religious policies — reading the two together gives a far more balanced picture of that era.
