## Background: The Dilemma After Non-Cooperation

In February 1922, after the Chauri Chaura incident, Gandhi withdrew the Non-Cooperation Movement, and in March 1922 he was sent to jail. The question before the Congress was — what next? One side wanted the boycott of the legislative councils formed under the reforms of 1919 to continue, and workers to engage in **constructive work** such as khadi, the removal of untouchability and Hindu-Muslim unity. They were called the **'No-Changers'**; Vallabhbhai Patel, Rajendra Prasad and C. Rajagopalachari were prominent among them.

The other side, called the **'Pro-Changers'**, believed that at a time when the movement had stalled, it was necessary to enter the councils and oppose the government, so that political enthusiasm would be maintained and government platforms would not be left to others.

## The Gaya Session and the Birth of the Party

The **Gaya Congress session** of December 1922 was presided over by **Chittaranjan Das**. His resolution for council entry was defeated by a majority. After this Das resigned the presidency and, together with **Motilal Nehru**, announced the 'Congress-Khilafat Swaraj Party' on **1 January 1923**. Das became its president and Motilal Nehru its secretary.

The Swarajists did not separate from the Congress — they continued to work as a group within the Congress. At a special session in Delhi in 1923 a compromise was reached and the Congress allowed its members to contest elections.

## The 1923 Elections and the Struggle in the Councils

In the elections of November 1923 the Swarajists achieved notable success. Of the elected seats of the Central Legislative Assembly they won more than about 40 seats (figures from 38 to 45 are found in sources). This was not a majority, but together with independents such as Muhammad Ali Jinnah they were able to defeat the government many times. Among the provinces they got a clear majority in the **Central Provinces** and became the largest party in **Bengal**; in both places they made it difficult to run dyarchy.

Their demands were — responsible government, release of political prisoners, repeal of repressive laws and a Round Table Conference on the constitution. On **24 August 1925** the Swarajist leader **Vithalbhai Patel** was elected the first elected Indian President of the Central Legislative Assembly.

## Weakness and End

The powers of the councils were limited; the Viceroy and Governors got budgets and laws passed through special powers, so the policy of 'obstruction from within' could not go very far. The death of Chittaranjan Das on **16 June 1925** was a great blow to the party. After this differences grew over the question of cooperation with the government; 'Responsivist' leaders such as N. C. Kelkar and M. R. Jayakar went their own way. In the elections of 1926 the party could not repeat its earlier success.

In 1928–29, with the opposition to the Simon Commission and then the Civil Disobedience Movement, the Swarajists merged back into the mainstream of the Congress, and by the mid-1930s the separate existence of the party had ended.

## Significance

The Swaraj Party kept national politics alive in the years when the movement had stalled and made the legislatures a platform for criticising the government. The constructive work of the No-Changers and the parliamentary struggle of the Swarajists together prepared for the next mass movement. This experience of contesting elections and legislative work later proved useful to the Congress in the provincial elections of 1937.
