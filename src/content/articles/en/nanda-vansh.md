## A New Dynasty on the Throne of Magadha

In the sixth-fifth centuries BCE, the Haryanka kings (Bimbisara, Ajatashatru) had made Magadha the most powerful of the sixteen Mahajanapadas. After them came the Shishunaga dynasty. Around **345 BCE** the **Nanda dynasty** replaced the Shishunaga dynasty, and its founder is believed to be **Mahapadma Nanda**. The capital remained **Pataliputra** (today's Patna).

Scholars do not agree on the exact dates of the Nandas. Generally c. 345–322 BCE is accepted, but some historians place the beginning around 364 BCE, and the Jain tradition's reckoning differs even from this.

## Mahapadma Nanda — "Ekarat"

Traditions differ about Mahapadma's birth. Greek-Roman and Jain accounts describe him as the **son of a barber**, while the Puranas say he was born to a Shudra wife of a Shishunaga king. In the Buddhist text *Mahavamsa*, the founder's name is given as **Ugrasena**.

The Puranas call Mahapadma **"Sarvakshatrantaka"** (destroyer of all Kshatriyas) and **"Ekarat"** (sole sovereign). This is taken to mean that he defeated many old dynasties of North India and brought them under Magadha. There is also disagreement about the length of his reign — the Matsya Purana gives 88 years and the Vayu Purana 28 years.

## The Nine Nandas and Dhana Nanda

The Buddhist, Jain and Puranic traditions all speak of **nine Nanda kings**, but they give different names. The Puranas list Mahapadma and his eight sons. The last king was **Dhana Nanda**, whose very name points to his wealth.

Around 326 BCE, when **Alexander** reached Punjab, Greek writers mentioned a powerful king of the Ganges valley, whom they call **Agrammes** or **Xandrames**. He is generally identified as Dhana Nanda. Greek writers also say that this king was not popular among his subjects.

## A Huge Army and Immense Wealth

Greek-Roman writers give figures for the Nanda army, which do not agree with one another. According to Curtius, it had 200,000 infantry, 20,000 cavalry, 3,000 elephants and 2,000 chariots; Plutarch gives even larger numbers. These figures may be exaggerations, but they show that the fame of Nanda power travelled far. It was amid talk of this very army that Alexander's tired soldiers refused to march further on the banks of the **Beas river**.

Behind the Nandas' wealth was an organised tax system. Later texts also accuse the Nandas of imposing heavy taxes. The grammar text *Kashika* mentions "Nandopakramani manani" — that is, the weights and measures introduced by the Nandas. Punch-marked silver coins of the Nanda period are also found.

## Expansion up to Kalinga

The **Hathigumpha inscription of Kharavela** (c. first century BCE), carved on the Udayagiri hill in Odisha, mentions a "Nandaraja" who had a canal dug in Kalinga and carried away a Jain image from there. This shows that the Nandas' influence reached Kalinga. However, this inscription is broken in many places, and the time span given in it ("ti-vasa-sata") is read by some scholars as 300 years and by others as 103 years. How far the Nanda empire extended in the south is also not clear.

## End and Legacy

Around **322 BCE**, **Chandragupta Maurya**, with the help of **Chanakya** (Kautilya), defeated Dhana Nanda and laid the foundation of the **Maurya dynasty**. The famous story of Chanakya's insult and revenge comes from later texts — such as Vishakhadatta's play *Mudrarakshasa* and Buddhist-Jain tales — so it cannot be considered fully historical.

The Nanda dynasty's rule was short, but its importance is great. The Nandas for the first time brought a large part of the Ganges plain under one central authority and built a large standing army and tax system. The Maurya empire raised its vast structure on this very foundation.
