## Background: Four and a Half Centuries of Rule

In 1510 the Portuguese commander Afonso de Albuquerque captured Goa. Gradually Daman, Diu and Dadra and Nagar Haveli also became part of Portuguese India (Estado da Índia). The British in 1947, and the French by 1954, chose to give up their settlements in India, but Portugal's dictator **António Salazar** maintained that Goa was not a colony but a 'province' of Portugal itself.

## 1946: Lohia and 'Revolution Day'

Under Portuguese rule there were severe restrictions on freedom of assembly and speech. The socialist leader **Ram Manohar Lohia** came to Goa in 1946 and, together with Dr. **Julião Menezes**, openly challenged these restrictions. On **18 June 1946** a meeting was held in Margao in defiance of the ban; Lohia was arrested. From this day the mass movement for civil rights and freedom in Goa gathered pace, and today 18 June is observed as 'Goa Revolution Day'.

## Diplomacy and the Satyagraha of 1955

After independence India asked Portugal for talks on 27 February 1950, which was rejected. In 1953 India withdrew its diplomatic mission from Lisbon. In 1954 volunteers freed **Dadra and Nagar Haveli** from Portuguese control.

On **15 August 1955** thousands of unarmed satyagrahis from across the country tried to enter Goa from several points. The Portuguese police opened fire; sources put the number killed at between 21 and 30. After this India closed its consulate in Goa and tightened the economic blockade. Even so, Portugal did not budge.

## Operation Vijay (December 1961)

After years of failed diplomacy, the Nehru government decided on military action. The joint operation of the army, navy and air force was named **'Operation Vijay'**. Some skirmishes on the border had begun from 17 December, and the main attack began early on **18 December 1961**. Indian forces entered Goa from several directions, while action was also taken simultaneously in Daman and Diu; on Anjediva island the navy faced stiff resistance.

Salazar had ordered the Governor-General **Manuel António Vassalo e Silva** to fight to the last soldier, but with very few troops and resources he did not obey this order. On **19 December 1961** he signed the formal surrender. In this operation of about 36 hours, about 22 Indian and 30 Portuguese soldiers were killed, and more than 4,600 Portuguese soldiers were taken prisoner (figures differ slightly among sources).

## International Reaction

Portugal complained to the United Nations Security Council. The United States, Britain, France and Turkey proposed that India withdraw its forces, but the **Soviet Union** vetoed it. Western countries criticised India, while many Asian and African countries saw it as the end of colonialism. Portugal did not accept Indian sovereignty until 1974.

## Significance

19 December is celebrated today as **'Goa Liberation Day'**. After liberation Goa, Daman and Diu became Union Territories; in 1987 Goa was given full statehood. The liberation of Goa closed the last major chapter of European colonial rule on Indian soil. At the same time, the debate that arose in the world at the time over the use of military force by an India that spoke of non-violence is also an important aspect of this event.
