## From Navsari to Bombay

**Jamsetji Nusserwanji Tata** was born on **3 March 1839** at **Navsari** in Gujarat into a **Parsi** family of priests. His father Nusserwanji broke with tradition, chose the path of trade and moved to Bombay. Jamsetji studied at Bombay's **Elphinstone College** and then joined his father's export business. In the course of this work he travelled to China, England and other countries, where he saw that England's wealth came from its factories.

In **1868**, at the age of 29, he started his own trading company with a capital of **Rs 21,000**. In **1869** he bought a bankrupt oil mill at **Chinchpokli** in Bombay, turned it into a textile mill and a few years later sold it at a profit.

## The Empress Mills — A New Kind of Factory

For his textile mill Jamsetji chose **Nagpur** instead of Bombay, because it was close to the cotton-growing region, the railway had already reached it, and coal and water were easily available. The mill started working on **1 January 1877** — the same day that **Queen Victoria** was proclaimed 'Empress of India'. That is why it was named the **Empress Mills**.

At the Empress Mills Jamsetji installed new machines and gave workers facilities that were rare in India at the time — schemes such as a provident fund, accident relief and pensions. These facilities were given long before any law required them. Later he also acquired more mills in Bombay, including one called the 'Swadeshi Mills'.

## Four Big Dreams

Jamsetji believed that India would become self-reliant only when it had **heavy industry, science, energy** and modern facilities. He set four big goals:

- **The Taj Mahal Hotel** — a world-class hotel in Bombay, which opened in **December 1903**. It is described as the first hotel in India with electricity. This was the only dream fulfilled in his lifetime.
- **An institute of science** — he planned to donate a large part of his property for advanced research by young Indians. For this he also sought the support of Swami Vivekananda. The institute was established after his death, in **1909**, in Bengaluru as the **Indian Institute of Science (IISc)**, with the support of the Maharaja of Mysore and the Government of India as well; the first students arrived in **1911**.
- **Hydroelectric power** — a plan to generate electricity for Bombay's mills from the monsoon water of the Western Ghats, which **Sir Dorabji Tata** realised in **1910** as the Tata Hydro-Electric company.
- **A steel plant** — the hardest and biggest dream.

## Tata Steel — The Dream of Iron

At that time the British government and many experts believed that a modern steel plant could not run in India. Jamsetji went to England and America to consult experts. The American geologist **Charles Page Perin** and his colleagues were given the task of finding the right site with iron ore, coal and water. The search was still going on when Jamsetji died on **19 May 1904** at **Bad Nauheim** in Germany.

His son **Sir Dorabji Tata** and colleagues continued the search. After a hunt of nearly three years, the village of **Sakchi**, near the confluence of the **Subarnarekha** and **Kharkai** rivers in today's Jharkhand, was chosen. On **26 August 1907** the **Tata Iron and Steel Company (TISCO)** was founded. Its capital was raised not from the London market but from a large number of **Indian investors**, which is seen as linked to the **swadeshi** enthusiasm of the time.

Construction began in **1908**, pig iron began to be produced in **1911**, and on **16 February 1912** the first steel ingot was rolled. During the **First World War** Tata steel met wartime needs, which raised the company's standing. In **1919** Viceroy **Lord Chelmsford** renamed Sakchi **Jamshedpur** in honour of the founder.

Tata Steel also set new examples in workers' welfare — the **eight-hour working day** in **1912**, free medical care in **1915**, and later schools for employees' children, a provident fund and accident compensation.

## Legacy

Jamsetji Tata is called the **father of Indian industry**. He laid the foundation of heavy industry at a time when India was seen only as a country that exported raw materials. His thinking was not limited to profit — he regarded education, science and the welfare of workers as part of industry. In **1892** he also set up the **J. N. Tata Endowment** to help Indian students study abroad.

Today the Tata Group is among India's largest industrial groups, and a large part of the ownership of its companies lies with philanthropic trusts. The steel plant at Jamshedpur, the Indian Institute of Science in Bengaluru and the Taj hotel in Mumbai — all three still bear witness to the vision of a man who, already in the nineteenth century, dreamed of an industrial India.
