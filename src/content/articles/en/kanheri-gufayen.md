## An Ancient Monastery in the Heart of Mumbai

In the northern part of Mumbai, deep in the dense forest of **Sanjay Gandhi National Park** near Borivali, there is a hill of dark **basalt** rock. The **Kanheri Caves** are cut into this hill. The number of caves counted here is **109**, which makes it one of the largest rock-cut Buddhist complexes in India.

The name "Kanheri" is believed to come from the Sanskrit **"Krishnagiri"**, meaning "black mountain" — because of the dark basalt rock.

## A Thousand Years of History

The caves at Kanheri were not made at one time. The earliest caves are thought to date from around the first century BCE or the first century CE (scholars differ on this), and excavation continued until about the **tenth century**. By the third century CE it had become a major Buddhist centre of the Konkan coast.

Over this long period one can also see the changing forms of Buddhism here. In the early caves the Buddha is shown only through a symbol such as the **stupa**, while the later caves have large images of the Buddha and bodhisattvas. **Cave 41** has an image of **Avalokiteshvara with eleven heads**, which points to the Mahayana tradition.

## The Great Chaitya and the Darbar Cave

- **Cave 3 (the Great Chaitya)** — This prayer hall is about 86 feet long. It has **34 pillars** around the stupa, and its plan is similar to the famous chaitya at Karle. At its entrance is an inscription from the time of the Satavahana king **Yajna Sri Satakarni** (c. 170–199 CE), so this chaitya was probably built during his reign.
- **Cave 11 (the Darbar Cave)** — This was a large assembly hall where monks gathered. It is dated to about the sixth century.

## Inscriptions and Trade

Kanheri has dozens of inscriptions in **Brahmi** and, later, Nagari script. They show that merchants, craftsmen, ordinary lay worshippers and members of the royal family gave donations for making the caves and for the food and clothing of the monks. The monastery was linked with trade routes connecting ports such as **Sopara** and **Kalyan** with cities such as Nasik, Paithan and Ujjain — the prosperity of trade was the main source of the donations here.

The most unusual are the **Pahlavi** (Middle Persian) inscriptions in **Cave 90**. According to James Burgess's study, they were written by Iranian (Parsi) travellers who came here in the early eleventh century — around **1009 and 1021 CE**.

## Water Management and Famous Visitors

It was not easy to gather water all year round for hundreds of monks. So **channels** were cut into the rock of the hill, which carried rainwater to **stone cisterns**. Caves 5 and 6 are not really caves but cisterns of this kind, donated by a minister.

According to tradition, the famous Buddhist teacher **Atisha Dipankara** (980–1054), who later went to Tibet, came to Kanheri to study meditation.

## Significance

Kanheri tells us that where there is a crowded metropolis today, there was for about a thousand years a settlement of Buddhist monks, connected with sea trade. The inscriptions here give valuable information about Satavahana history, society and economy. Today it is a monument of national importance protected by the Archaeological Survey of India.
