## Background — Punjab after the Sikh Kingdom

In **1849** the British annexed the **Sikh Empire** built by Maharaja Ranjit Singh. After the loss of political power, insecurity and fragmentation grew in Sikh society. Many historic gurdwaras were managed by **mahants**, some of whom acted arbitrarily and followed customs contrary to the teachings of the Gurus. The censuses of the time also seemed to show the number of Sikhs falling.

In this same period **Christian missionaries** were opening schools and preaching centres in Punjab. On the other side, the **Arya Samaj**, the **Brahmo Samaj** and Muslim reform movements were also active. Within the Sikh Panth, the **Nirankari** and **Namdhari** (Kuka) movements had earlier prepared the ground for reform.

The immediate shock came in **1873**, when **four Sikh students** of a mission school in Amritsar announced their wish to embrace Christianity. Alarmed by this, **Sardar Thakur Singh Sandhawalia** called a meeting of leading Sikhs in Amritsar.

## The Amritsar Singh Sabha (1873)

From this meeting the first **Sri Guru Singh Sabha** was formed in Amritsar in **1873**. (Sources differ slightly on the exact date of its founding — some give July 1873 and some 1 October 1873.) **Thakur Singh Sandhawalia** became its first president and **Giani Gian Singh** its secretary; **Baba Khem Singh Bedi**, a descendant of Guru Nanak, was also among its leading figures.

The declared aims of the Sabha were to restore the Sikh religion to its original and pure form, to print the history of the Gurus and religious texts, to promote the **Punjabi** language and literature in **Gurmukhi**, and to spread modern education among the Sikhs. The Sabha decided that it would remain loyal to the government and would work on religion, education and society while keeping away from politics. The leadership of the Amritsar Sabha was mainly in the hands of an aristocratic and traditionalist class.

## The Lahore Sabha and the 'Tat Khalsa'

In **1879** a second Singh Sabha was formed in **Lahore**. Its leaders were **Professor Gurmukh Singh** of Oriental College and the writer and preacher **Bhai Ditt Singh**. The Lahore Sabha was more democratic, and Sikhs of lower castes also joined it on an equal footing. Its ideology came to be called **'Tat Khalsa'** (the true Khalsa). This stream stressed the worship of one God, the centrality of the **Guru Granth Sahib**, opposition to idol worship and caste discrimination, and **Khalsa identity**.

The traditionalist stream of the Amritsar Sabha, later called the **'Sanatan Sikhs'**, saw Sikhism within the broader Hindu social framework and wanted to keep many old customs. There were sharp debates between the two streams, but gradually the influence of the Tat Khalsa grew. By the 1880s **more than a hundred** Singh Sabhas had been formed in Punjab and beyond, and in **1883** the **Khalsa Diwan** was set up to link them.

## Education, Publishing and the Identity Debate

The Singh Sabhas made full use of the **printing press** to spread their message. The **'Khalsa Akhbar'** came out from Lahore, and in **1894** **Bhai Vir Singh** set up the **Khalsa Tract Society** to print cheap religious booklets. Later he also started the newspaper **'Khalsa Samachar'**. His novels and poems laid the foundation of modern Punjabi literature.

The greatest achievement in education was **Khalsa College, Amritsar**, founded in **1892**, for which the rulers of Sikh princely states and ordinary people contributed money. Besides this, Khalsa schools and girls' schools opened in village after village.

In the same period, criticism of the Sikh Gurus by some Arya Samaj preachers soured relations between the two communities. In response, **Bhai Kahn Singh Nabha**, a scholar of Nabha state, wrote **'Ham Hindu Nahin'** (We Are Not Hindus, around 1898–99), which argued for a distinct religious identity for the Sikhs. The book became a symbol of the great debate of that time. In **1902** the **Chief Khalsa Diwan** was formed to bring Sikh institutions onto one platform, and amid these efforts the **Anand Marriage Act** was passed in **1909**, giving legal recognition to the Sikh marriage ceremony **'Anand Karaj'**.

## Legacy

The Singh Sabha movement connected Sikh society with modern education, printed literature and organised institutions. Because of it, the Gurmukhi script and the Punjabi language gained new life and a standard form. The scholarly work done in this period on Sikh history, the meaning of Gurbani and the code of conduct (rehat maryada) is still regarded as foundational.

The awareness awakened by this movement later prepared the background for the **Gurdwara Reform Movement** (Akali movement) of the **1920s**, as a result of which the management of gurdwaras passed from the mahants to the elected **Shiromani Gurdwara Parbandhak Committee**. Historians count the Singh Sabha movement among those streams of the nineteenth-century **Indian renaissance** that gave their communities self-respect and a new direction.
