## The situation after the war

In the war of **December 1971**, the Pakistani army surrendered at Dhaka on **16 December** and **Bangladesh** became an independent country. At the end of the war India held more than **90,000 Pakistani prisoners of war** (most sources say about 93,000), and on the western front the Indian army occupied more than **13,000 square kilometres** of Pakistani territory. On the other side, in Pakistan **Zulfikar Ali Bhutto** had become president and was under heavy pressure to bring back his soldiers and land.

To settle post-war relations between the two countries, a summit began at **Shimla** at the end of **June 1972**.

## The talks at Shimla

The talks were held at **Barnes Court** (today the **Raj Bhavan** of Himachal Pradesh). For several days the talks kept stalling, and at one point it seemed that there would be no agreement. In the end, after a face-to-face conversation between **Indira Gandhi** and Bhutto, agreement on the draft was reached. The agreement is dated **2 July 1972**, although the signing technically took place a little after midnight on 3 July. Pakistan ratified it on **15 July** and India on **3 August 1972**, and it came into force on 4 August 1972.

## Main points of the agreement

According to the text of the agreement:

- Relations between the two countries would be guided by the principles of the **United Nations Charter**.
- Both countries would settle their differences by **peaceful means, through bilateral talks** or any other peaceful means mutually agreed upon. Until a final settlement, neither side would unilaterally alter the situation.
- Both would respect each other's **territorial integrity, sovereignty** and political independence, and would not threaten the use of force.
- Postal, telegraph, road, sea and air links, trade and cultural exchanges would be restored gradually.
- Both armies would withdraw to their own side of the international border; this withdrawal was to be completed within **30 days**.
- In **Jammu and Kashmir**, both sides would respect the line resulting from the ceasefire of **17 December 1971**, "without prejudice to the recognised position of either side". This line later came to be called the **Line of Control (LoC)**.

## Land and prisoners of war

After the agreement, India returned most of the land won on the western front, but some strategic areas inside the Line of Control in Kashmir, such as **Turtuk** and nearby villages in Ladakh's Chorbat valley, remained with India.

The question of prisoners of war was not resolved at Shimla, because Bangladesh was also a party to it. The matter was settled by the **Delhi Agreement** of **28 August 1973**, signed by India, Pakistan and Bangladesh; after this the prisoners of war went home. The Simla Agreement also opened the way for Pakistan's later recognition of Bangladesh.

## Assessment and controversy

In India the Simla Agreement is seen as having made **Kashmir a bilateral issue**, and India has opposed third-party mediation ever since. Some Indian officials later said that Bhutto had given a verbal assurance that the Line of Control would gradually be made a permanent border; the Pakistani side denied any such promise. Critics also say that India returned prisoners and land but got no firm final settlement on Kashmir in exchange.

The agreement could not completely prevent confrontation between the two countries; **Siachen** (1984) and **Kargil** (1999) are examples. In **April 2025**, after India put the Indus Waters Treaty in abeyance following the Pahalgam terror attack, Pakistan announced that it was suspending the Simla Agreement. Even so, for more than half a century the agreement remained a basic document of India-Pakistan relations.
