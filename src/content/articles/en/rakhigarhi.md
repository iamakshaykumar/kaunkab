## A City Buried Beneath a Village

About 150 kilometres north-west of Delhi, in the Hisar region of Haryana, lies the village of **Rakhigarhi** (after recent administrative changes some sources place it in the new Hansi district). It lies in the plain of the Ghaggar river, and some scholars place it in the valley of the ancient **Drishadvati** river. Beneath the mounds around the village lies buried a huge city of the Harappan civilisation.

Archaeologists have numbered the mounds here **RGR-1 to RGR-11**. Of these, RGR-1 to RGR-5 are part of the main city and **RGR-7 is a cemetery**.

## How Big?

Before 2014 most scholars thought the urban area of the mature phase was somewhat more than 80 to 100 hectares. After the identification of new mounds (RGR-8 and RGR-9) in 2014, the Archaeological Survey of India put the total spread at **300 to 350 hectares**, on the basis of which it began to be called the **largest known Harappan site**. But many scholars caution that it is not certain that all the outlying mounds belong to a single settlement — so the claim to be "the largest" is not entirely undisputed.

## A Long Series of Excavations

Early work was done here by the Archaeological Survey of India in 1963 and by Kurukshetra University in 1969. Large-scale excavation was done in **1997–2000** by **Amarendra Nath** (Archaeological Survey of India). After this, excavation in **2011–2016** was led by **Vasant Shinde** of Deccan College, Pune, and from 2021 the Survey resumed work.

Excavation showed that the settlement here began in the **Pre-Harappan** phase and continued through Early Harappan (c. 3300–2600 BCE) and Mature Harappan (c. 2600–1900 BCE). In the city, streets about 1.92 metres wide, brick-built drains, a granary with seven chambers, fire altars, seals and traces of cotton have been found.

## Stories from the Cemetery

In the cemetery of RGR-7 many graves were found, in which pottery and ornaments had been placed with the dead. In one grave a woman and a man were buried together, which newspapers called the "Rakhigarhi love birds". One skeleton taken from Rakhigarhi is today displayed in the Harappan gallery of the **National Museum** in New Delhi.

## The 2019 DNA Study

In September 2019 a research paper by Vasant Shinde, Vagheesh Narasimhan, Niraj Rai, David Reich and other scientists was published in the science journal **Cell**. In it 61 skeletons from Rakhigarhi were examined, but enough ancient DNA was obtained from only one woman's skeleton (sample **I6113**).

The main findings of the study were:

- No trace of **steppe (Central Asian grassland) pastoralists** was found in this individual.
- There was also no ancestry from **early farmers of the Iranian plateau**; instead an ancient lineage linked to Iran was found that had separated from those farmers more than 12,000 years earlier, and that was mixed with the ancient hunter-gatherers of South Asia.
- This mixture is the largest source in the ancestry of present-day South Asians.

The researchers themselves cautioned that final conclusions about a whole civilisation cannot be drawn from the DNA of a single individual, and that the study also does not prove that farming began independently in South Asia. There was also considerable public debate over the interpretation of this study.

## Rakhigarhi Today

In 2012 the Global Heritage Fund counted Rakhigarhi among the most endangered heritage sites of Asia, because village houses have been built on parts of the mounds. In the Union Budget of February 2020 it was announced that Rakhigarhi would be developed as an **"iconic site"**. The Haryana government also runs a museum here.
