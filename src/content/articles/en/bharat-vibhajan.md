## Background: The End of the Last Attempts at Unity

After the collapse of the Cabinet Mission plan in 1946, the violence in Calcutta, and then the riots that spread through Noakhali, Bihar and Punjab, the scope for agreement between the Congress and the Muslim League had almost vanished. In February 1947, British Prime Minister Attlee announced that Britain would leave India by June 1948. In March 1947, **Lord Mountbatten** arrived as the last Viceroy.

## The Mountbatten Plan (3 June 1947)

On **3 June 1947**, Mountbatten announced the plan, which was accepted by the leaders of both the Congress and the League. According to it, British India would be divided into two dominions — India and Pakistan. The legislative assemblies of Punjab and Bengal would decide whether the province should be partitioned; a referendum would be held in the North-West Frontier Province and in the Sylhet district of Assam. The date of the transfer of power was moved up from June 1948 to August 1947.

The British Parliament passed the **Indian Independence Act** on **18 July 1947**. Pakistan became independent on **14 August** and India on **15 August 1947**.

## The Radcliffe Line

Two boundary commissions were set up, for Punjab and for Bengal; both were chaired by the British lawyer **Cyril Radcliffe**, and each commission had two judges nominated by the Congress and the League. Radcliffe reached India on 8 July 1947. Working with old maps, an incomplete census and heavy pressure, he drew the line through villages, canals and railway lines. The boundary decision was made public on **17 August 1947** — two days after independence. People in many areas did not even know until the day of independence which country they were in.

## Displacement and Violence

People who had become minorities on either side of the border — Hindus and Sikhs from West Punjab and Sindh, Muslims from East Punjab, and in Bengal in both directions — began leaving their homes out of insecurity. People crossed the border in foot convoys, bullock carts and overcrowded trains. It is estimated that **about 12 million to 20 million** people were displaced. The exodus from Bengal continued for years.

The violence was not committed by one community alone; people of every community became both attackers and victims. Scholars differ greatly on the number of deaths — estimates range from **about 200,000 to 2 million**. Women suffered the deepest agony: according to the claims of the governments of both countries, thousands of women were abducted, and later both governments ran campaigns to find them and return them.

In this darkness there were also examples of humanity — countless people saved the lives of neighbours of another religion by hiding them or taking them to safety. **Mahatma Gandhi** worked for peace in Noakhali and Calcutta, and in January 1948 fasted in Delhi for communal harmony.

## Refugees and Rehabilitation

Large camps were set up in Punjab, Delhi and Bengal for the refugees who came to India. The **Kurukshetra** camp was among the largest, where Prime Minister Nehru and Vinoba Bhave also visited in April 1948. At **Kingsway Camp** in Delhi, more than 35,000 refugees lived at any one time, while the Purana Qila became a camp for Muslim refugees heading to Pakistan. Gradually, rehabilitation took place through land, houses, loans and new settlements; many colonies of Delhi and cities such as Faridabad were founded or grew in this period.

## Legacy

Partition changed the map, the politics and the memories of millions of families of the subcontinent forever. The Kashmir dispute, the new identity of the refugees, and literature — the works of writers such as Saadat Hasan Manto, Bhisham Sahni and Amrita Pritam — are all tied to this experience. Today historians emphasise that Partition should be remembered not merely as a political event, but also as a story of the suffering and courage of ordinary people and of rebuilding life anew.
