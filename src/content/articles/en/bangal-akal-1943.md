## Background: A Bengal Already Weakened

From the beginning of the 20th century, Bengal's agriculture had been stagnant. Between 1901 and 1941 the population grew fast, but paddy yields stayed nearly the same. Because of debt and loss of land, the number of landless agricultural labourers in the villages kept rising. Such people filled their stomachs by buying rice from the market, so when prices rose they were the first to fall into crisis.

## Shocks of the War

The Second World War struck this weak system with several blows:

- **The fall of Burma (1942):** Japan occupied Burma, from where rice came to Bengal. Refugees fleeing Burma also reached Bengal.
- **The 'denial policy':** Fearing a Japanese attack, the British administration removed surplus rice from coastal areas and seized or destroyed thousands of boats, which affected the livelihoods of fishermen, boatmen and trade.
- **Cyclone and crop disease:** The cyclone and floods that hit the Midnapore coast in October 1942 ruined crops; a fungal disease also spread in the paddy.
- **Wartime inflation:** Heavy spending by the army and war industries pushed prices up fast, while the real earnings of labourers fell.

## Policy Failures

The government gave priority in grain to workers of Calcutta's war industries, government employees and the army; the poor of the villages were left behind. Provinces put bans on the movement of grain, price controls pushed rice into the black market, and hoarding and speculation deepened the crisis. The provincial government never formally declared a "famine", so the measures of the relief code were not applied.

In London, the war cabinet led by Churchill rejected or reduced several requests for grain imports coming from India, citing a shortage of ships. Some historians regard this as a wartime compulsion, while others — such as Madhusree Mukerjee — see it as serious neglect or worse.

## Deaths and Relief

By the middle of 1943, hungry people from the villages began coming toward Calcutta in search of food. In October 1943 the new viceroy **Lord Wavell** put the army to work on relief and grain distribution, after which conditions improved somewhat. A good harvest came in December 1943, but deaths from disease continued until 1944.

There is no exact figure for the dead. The **Famine Inquiry Commission** (1945) estimated about **1.5 million** deaths; Amartya Sen about **3 million**; in various studies the range runs from **800,000 to 3.8 million**.

## Debate on the Causes

In *Poverty and Famines* (1981), the economist **Amartya Sen** argued that the total availability of rice in Bengal in 1943 was not much lower than normal; the real crisis was that inflation took away the poor's ability to buy grain — their "entitlement". This theory changed the way famines are understood. On the other hand, some scholars hold that the fall in yield from the cyclone and crop disease was much larger than Sen's assessment. Most modern historians believe this famine was more the result of failures of war, markets and governance than of a natural disaster — though disagreements remain over the weight of each factor.
