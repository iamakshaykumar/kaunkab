## Background: Humiliation in a Foreign Land

At the beginning of the twentieth century thousands of Punjabi farmers, ex-soldiers and labourers went in search of work to the Pacific coast of Canada and the United States. There they had to work hard on farms, in lumber mills and on railway lines, but in return they got racial discrimination and insult. Canada made strict rules to stop Indians from coming.

The overseas Indians felt that they were respected nowhere simply because they were citizens of an enslaved country, and that the British government would not protect them. This experience turned them towards the fight for freedom.

## The Founding of the Ghadar Party

In 1913 these emigrants formed the 'Pacific Coast Hindustan Association', which came to be called the **Ghadar Party** after the name of its newspaper. There is disagreement among sources about the date and place of founding; many accounts link it to a meeting held on 15 July 1913 at **Astoria** in Oregon, while Indian textbooks usually give the name of **San Francisco**, where the party's headquarters, the **Yugantar Ashram**, was located.

**Sohan Singh Bhakna** became its president and the learned revolutionary **Lala Har Dayal** its general secretary. From 1 November 1913 the newspaper 'Ghadar' began to appear, openly calling for revolt against British rule. Its poems became very popular under the name 'Ghadar di Goonj'. After his arrest in the United States in March 1914, Har Dayal was released on bail and went to Europe.

## The Komagata Maru: The Blow of 1914

In 1914 the Singapore businessman **Gurdit Singh** chartered the Japanese ship **Komagata Maru**, on which 376 Indian passengers (mostly Sikhs) travelled to Canada. The ship reached Vancouver on 23 May 1914, but after being held for about two months only a handful of passengers were allowed to land, and on 23 July the ship was sent back.

On 29 September 1914 the ship reached **Budge Budge** near Calcutta. There a clash occurred between the police and the passengers, and about 20 passengers were killed in the firing. This incident further inflamed the anger of the overseas Indians.

## The Failed Revolution of 1915

When the First World War broke out in August 1914, the Ghadar leaders saw it as a golden opportunity. A large number of Ghadarites returned to India so that a mutiny could be raised in the Indian units of the army. In Punjab **Kartar Singh Sarabha** and **Rash Behari Bose** built contacts in the cantonments, and **21 February 1915** was fixed as the date for a simultaneous uprising.

But a police informer had entered the organisation. News of the plan reached the government; despite the change of date, large-scale arrests took place and the uprising failed. Rash Behari Bose escaped and went to Japan.

## The Lahore Conspiracy Case and Sarabha's Martyrdom

The arrested revolutionaries were tried in the **Lahore Conspiracy Case**, in which many were sentenced to death and many to Kala Pani (transportation for life). **Kartar Singh Sarabha**, born in 1896 in Sarabha village of Ludhiana, was hanged in Lahore Central Jail in November 1915 (the date is usually given as 16 November). He was only 19 years old at that time.

## Legacy

The Ghadar Party failed in its immediate aim, but for the first time it rose above religion and caste to dream of a secular, democratic republic. **Bhagat Singh** regarded Sarabha as his ideal and kept his picture with him. Many leaders who came out of the Ghadar movement were later active in the peasant and Left movements of Punjab.
