## What the Charter Was

The East India Company received its right to trade through a **charter** from the British government. After the Regulating Act of **1773** and Pitt's India Act of **1784**, it became the practice for the British Parliament to renew the Company's charter about every **twenty years** and to make changes in the government of India on that occasion. After **1793** the next renewal came in **1813** and then in **1833**. These two Acts are important milestones in the story of the Company's transformation from a trading body into a ruling body.

## The Charter Act of 1813: A Crack in the Monopoly

At the beginning of the nineteenth century the **Industrial Revolution** was advancing rapidly in Britain. Factory owners and merchants of cities like Manchester and Liverpool wanted the Indian market opened to all. At that time Napoleon's **"Continental System"** in Europe had also blocked routes of British trade. The ideas of "free trade" of thinkers like **Adam Smith** were also becoming popular.

The main provisions of this Act, approved on **22 July 1813**, were:

- The Company's **monopoly** of trade with India **ended** and it was opened to all British merchants; but the Company kept its monopoly over the **tea** trade and trade with **China**.
- The Company's charter was extended for the next **twenty years**.
- The **sovereignty of the British Crown** over the Company's territories in India was clearly declared.
- **Christian missionaries** were allowed to come to India and carry out preaching and educational work. People like **Charles Grant** and **William Wilberforce** had run a long campaign for this.
- Provision was made to spend at least **one lakh rupees** every year for the revival of literature, the encouragement of learned Indians and the spread of scientific knowledge among Indians. This is regarded as the first legal provision for government spending on education in India.

After the monopoly ended, British machine-made cloth began to arrive in the Indian market in large quantities. Many historians link this to the process of decline of the Indian handloom industry.

## The Charter Act of 1833: Governor-General of India

This Act, approved on **28 August 1833** and in force from **22 April 1834**, made far bigger changes. It is also called the "Government of India Act 1833".

- The **Governor-General of Bengal** was now called the **"Governor-General of India"**, and his council received the power to make laws for the whole of British India. **Lord William Bentinck** became the first Governor-General of India.
- The governments of **Madras and Bombay** lost their separate law-making powers. In this way the administration and law-making of British India were **centralised**.
- All the Company's **commercial activities were stopped**, including tea and the China trade. The Company now remained only a body running the **administration** of India on behalf of the British Crown.
- A fourth member, the **law member**, was added to the Governor-General's council, who took part only in law-making meetings. The first person in this post was **T. B. Macaulay**.
- Provision was made to set up a **Law Commission** to systematise and codify India's laws; in 1834 the first Law Commission was formed under Macaulay's chairmanship.
- The Act said that no Indian would be barred from any post under the Company merely because of his **religion, place of birth, descent or colour**.
- The government was directed to take measures to mitigate and end **slavery** in India; slavery was legally abolished in British India in 1843.
- Europeans were allowed to settle and hold land in India without a licence.

The Act also attempted **open competition** for the civil service, but because of opposition from the Company's directors this could not be put into effect at that time.

## Limits and Criticism

Despite the promise of equality, in practice very few Indians were appointed to high posts, and according to critics this provision remained on paper for a long time. Centralisation reduced the autonomy of the provinces. The arrival of missionaries and social reforms also created apprehensions in a section of Indian society. On the other hand, the opening of the Indian market to British industry increased the tilt towards an economy that exported India's raw materials and imported finished goods.

## Significance

Together, the Acts of 1813 and 1833 show how a trading company gradually became a body that only ran a government. The centralised law-making framework created in 1833 is regarded as the beginning of the later **Indian legislature**, and the work of the Law Commission led to codes such as the **Indian Penal Code**. The 1813 provision of one lakh rupees later became the basis of the **Orientalist versus Anglicist** education debate and the English education policy of 1835. The next Charter Act came in **1853**, and in 1858 Company rule came to an end.
