## Background: Whose Forests?

The life of the villages of the Garhwal Himalaya rested on the forests — fuel wood, fodder for animals, water springs, farm tools, all came from there. But in the forest policy carried over from the colonial period, forests were regarded mainly as a source of revenue. The Forest Department auctioned trees and big contractors from the plains did the felling, while local people did not get permission even for the trees they needed.

In 1964 the Gandhian worker **Chandi Prasad Bhatt** formed the **Dasholi Gram Swarajya Sangh** at Gopeshwar in Chamoli district, which wanted to run small village industries — such as a workshop making farm tools — with forest resources. The devastating flood in the Alaknanda valley in **July 1970** made people realise the dangerous result of reckless felling in the mountains.

## Mandal: The First Confrontation (1973)

The Forest Department refused to give the Sangh's workshop some ash trees (angu), but gave the same trees to a sports goods company of Allahabad to make tennis rackets. In **1973**, when the company's workers reached the forest of Mandal village, the villagers and the Sangh's workers blocked their way beating drums and shouting slogans, and the workers had to turn back (the date of this event is given as March in some sources and April 1973 in others). It was here that the decision was taken to adopt the nonviolent method of 'clinging' to trees — that is, saving them by embracing them. After this, a similar protest succeeded in the Phata region in June 1974.

## Reni and Gaura Devi (March 1974)

About 2,500 trees near **Reni** village, on the bank of the Alaknanda, had been auctioned. In **March 1974**, on the day the felling was to take place, the men of the village had gone to Chamoli on news that compensation was to be paid, and the Sangh's workers were also there. A girl informed **Gaura Devi** (1925–1991), head of the village's Mahila Mangal Dal, of this. Gaura Devi reached the forest with 27 women. Despite threats they stood firm, clung to the trees, and guarded the forest all night. In the following days neighbouring villages also joined, and after a standoff of some days the contractor's men went back. (The date of this event is given in sources as 25 or 26 March 1974.)

## The Government's Response and Spread

**Hemwati Nandan Bahuguna**, then Chief Minister of Uttar Pradesh, set up an inquiry committee, which regarded the Reni forest as ecologically sensitive and recommended stopping felling there; later felling was banned for ten years over a large area. The movement gradually spread across Uttarakhand. In some places women tied rakhis on trees and vowed to save them, while in others social issues such as prohibition of liquor also became linked to the movement.

The Gandhian **Sunderlal Bahuguna** carried Chipko's message far and wide through foot marches; in 1981–83 he made a long march across the Himalaya. In **1980** the government of Prime Minister Indira Gandhi imposed a 15-year ban on commercial felling of green trees in the Himalayan forests of Uttar Pradesh.

## Different Streams Within the Movement

Chipko was not a single organisation. Historians point out that Bhatt's stream emphasised local people's livelihood and community rights over the forest, while Bahuguna's stream emphasised protecting the ecology and a complete ban on felling. There has also been academic debate about the role of women in the movement — some regard it as an example of 'eco-feminism', while others regard it basically as a struggle for livelihood.

## Significance

Chipko showed that the question of the environment is a matter of life and death for the poor. Inspired by it, the **Appiko movement** began in Karnataka in 1983, and debate on the role of local communities in the country's forest policy grew sharper. Chandi Prasad Bhatt received the Ramon Magsaysay Award in 1982 and Sunderlal Bahuguna the Padma Vibhushan in 2009. The image of women clinging to trees is still a symbol of the nonviolent environmental movement across the world.
