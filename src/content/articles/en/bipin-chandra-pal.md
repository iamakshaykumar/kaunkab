## From Sylhet to Calcutta

**Bipin Chandra Pal** was born on **7 November 1858** into a well-to-do Kayastha family in the village of **Poil** in **Sylhet** district of the Bengal Presidency. This area is today in **Bangladesh**. He came to Calcutta for higher education, where he studied at the Church Mission Society College and later also taught there for some time.

In Calcutta the young Bipin came into contact with the **Brahmo Samaj**. The Brahmo Samaj opposed caste discrimination, idol worship and harmful customs. Pal too remained all his life an opponent of the **caste system** and a supporter of **widow remarriage**. His social ideas included the interests of workers as well — he supported limiting working hours and the demand for better wages.

## A Warrior of Pen and Voice

Bipin Chandra Pal was counted among the most influential **orators** and **journalists** of his time. He wrote for and edited several newspapers and journals, the most important being **'Bengal Public Opinion'**, **'The Tribune'** of Lahore and his own English weekly **'New India'**. He was also associated from the start with the English daily **'Bande Mataram'**, launched in 1906, in which **Aurobindo Ghose** later played the leading role.

He began attending the sessions of the **Indian National Congress** from its early years. At the Madras session of **1887** he criticised the **Arms Act** as discriminatory. In 1899–1900 he also went to Oxford to study comparative theology, though he did not complete the course. His deep interest in religion and philosophy shows in his books too, such as **'The Soul of India'** and his study of Vaishnavism in Bengal.

## The Partition of Bengal and 'Lal-Bal-Pal'

When Lord Curzon **partitioned Bengal** in **1905**, Bipin Chandra Pal was in the front rank of the protest. The Moderate leaders trusted the path of petitions and prayers, while Pal believed that nothing would come from pleading alone. He preached a four-point programme:

- **Swadeshi** — using goods made in the country;
- **Boycott** — boycotting foreign goods and government institutions;
- **National education** — education with an Indian outlook, free of government control;
- **Swaraj** — self-rule, which he regarded as the final goal.

**Lala Lajpat Rai** of Punjab, **Bal Gangadhar Tilak** of Maharashtra and **Bipin Chandra Pal** of Bengal — this trio was called **'Lal-Bal-Pal'**. They were the leading faces of the Congress **Extremists** and tried to carry the national movement beyond the circle of the educated middle class to ordinary people. Because of his fiery speeches he is also called the **"Father of Revolutionary Thoughts"** in India.

In **1907** Pal toured the **Madras Presidency** and spoke on swadeshi in place after place. His speeches inspired many young people in south India; leaders such as **V. O. Chidambaram Pillai** of Tuticorin and the poet **Subramania Bharati** were part of that same upsurge.

## The 'Bande Mataram' Case and Jail

In 1907 the British government prosecuted the newspaper **'Bande Mataram'** for sedition and charged **Aurobindo Ghose**, treating him as its editor. The court summoned Bipin Chandra Pal as a witness, so that his testimony could prove that Aurobindo was the editor. Pal flatly **refused** to testify. This was treated as contempt of court, and in **September 1907** he was sentenced to **six months** of imprisonment. Meanwhile, with no firm proof that he was the editor, Aurobindo Ghose was acquitted.

Pal's firmness made him a hero of the nationalists. Aurobindo Ghose wrote an article on him in 'Bande Mataram' and later called him one of the "mightiest prophets of nationalism". Pal came out of jail in **March 1908**. After this he lived in **England** for some years, where he went on writing and speaking about the Indian question.

## Differences with Gandhi and the Final Years

After the First World War, leadership of the national movement passed into the hands of **Mahatma Gandhi**. Bipin Chandra Pal disagreed with many of Gandhi's methods. He criticised the programme of **non-cooperation** launched under Gandhi's leadership and its method, and believed the movement should run on reason and a clear political programme rather than emotional appeals. Because of these differences he gradually drifted away from the Congress mainstream in the 1920s.

In the last years of his life he lived almost in seclusion and kept writing; his autobiographical book **'Memories of My Life and Times'** is regarded as an important record of the Bengal of that era. He died in Calcutta on **20 May 1932**.

## Legacy

Bipin Chandra Pal's greatest contribution was that he put forward swaraj as a clear goal and linked swadeshi, boycott and national education to it. The 'Lal-Bal-Pal' trio gave the national movement new self-confidence at a time when many people had come to regard British rule as permanent. His contributions in all three fields — social reform, journalism and politics — give him a place among the makers of modern India. In **1958**, his birth centenary, the Government of India issued a postage stamp in his honour.
