## Who Mangal Pandey Was

**Mangal Pandey** came from a Brahmin family in the village of Nagwa in **Ballia** district of present-day Uttar Pradesh. His date of birth is not known with certainty — 1827 is commonly given, and the 1984 postage stamp also carries this year. In 1849 he joined the Bengal Army of the East India Company, and by 1857 he was a sepoy in the 5th company of the **34th Bengal Native Infantry**, stationed at the **Barrackpore** cantonment near Calcutta.

## The Fear of the Cartridges

In early 1857 there was great unrest in the Bengal Army over the cartridges of the new **Enfield rifle**. There was a rumour that they were greased with cow and pig fat, and they had to be bitten with the teeth — so both Hindu and Muslim sepoys feared losing their religion. In February 1857 the 19th Regiment at Berhampore had refused to accept the cartridges and was being brought to Barrackpore to be disbanded. Tension in the cantonment was at its peak.

## 29 March 1857: What Happened

On the afternoon of 29 March, Mangal Pandey came out onto the parade ground with a loaded musket and began calling on his comrades to revolt. According to accounts cited in Wikipedia, he was shouting that the Europeans were coming and that by biting the cartridges they would lose their religion.

- On the adjutant **Lieutenant Baugh**, who came on horseback on hearing the news, he fired; the bullet hit the horse and the officer fell. Then he wounded him with a sword.
- **Sergeant-Major Hewson** was also attacked.
- The sepoy **Shaikh Paltu** tried to stop him and was himself wounded.
- **Jemadar Ishwari Prasad**, in charge of the quarter-guard, did not order his soldiers to arrest Mangal Pandey.

In the end **Major-General John Hearsey** reached the spot. Seeing himself surrounded, Mangal Pandey shot himself in the chest, but survived.

## Trial and Hanging

Within a week he was court-martialled. At the trial he said that he had taken bhang and opium and was not aware of what he had done; he did not name his comrades in any conspiracy. On **8 April 1857** he was hanged at Barrackpore in front of the whole cantonment. On **21 April** Jemadar Ishwari Prasad was also hanged. On **6 May 1857**, as collective punishment, seven of the ten companies of the 34th Regiment were disbanded in disgrace.

## Hero or Accident? The Historians' Debate

Historians do not agree about Mangal Pandey's intentions. The nationalist tradition regards him as the **first martyr of the struggle of 1857**, while scholars such as the historian Rudrangshu Mukherjee ask whether it was a deliberate plan or a lone act taken in religious fear and agitation. There is little solid evidence for the claims describing him as the mastermind of any larger conspiracy.

Even so, it is certain that his incident brought the sepoys' discontent out into the open. A few weeks later, on **10 May 1857**, the revolt flared up at Meerut and soon spread across North India. The British began to call the rebel sepoys **"Pandy"** after his name — this word itself shows that Mangal Pandey had become the symbol of 1857.
