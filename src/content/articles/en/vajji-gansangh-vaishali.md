## A State Without a King

In the time of the Buddha there were sixteen **Mahajanapadas** in northern India. In most of them kings ruled, but some states were called **gana** or **sangha**, where power was not in the hands of one person. The most famous of these was the **Vajji (Vriji) confederacy**, which spread north of the Ganges, in today's North Bihar. Its capital was **Vaishali** — the village of Basarh in today's Vaishali district, identified by Alexander Cunningham in 1861.

## Who Was in the Confederacy

The Vajji was a confederacy of several clans (tribes). Among them the **Licchavis** were the most prominent; besides them, the **Videhas** (Mithila region), the **Jnatrikas** (the clan in which Mahavira was born) and the people of the **Vajji** clan itself were included. It is believed that the Licchavis created this gana system in place of the monarchy of the old Videha kingdom. The time of its founding is roughly taken to be the sixth century BCE, but the exact date is not known.

## The Assembly and Governance

The Vajji confederacy was an **aristocratic republic** — that is, not like today's democracy, where every citizen votes. Power was with the heads of the main Kshatriya clans, who called themselves "rajas". They gathered in an assembly hall called the **Santhagara** to deliberate and take decisions. The confederacy also had a chief (ganamukhya).

According to the Buddhist **Ekapanna Jataka**, Vaishali had **7707 rajas**, and as many sub-rajas, commanders and treasurers. Historians do not take this number as literal truth, but it certainly indicates that many people took part in governance.

## The Buddha's Seven Conditions

There is an episode in the *Maha-parinibbana Sutta*. **Ajatashatru**, king of Magadha, wanted to destroy the Vajjis. He sent his chief minister, the Brahmin **Vassakara**, to the Buddha on Gridhrakuta hill at Rajagriha. Instead of answering directly, the Buddha asked Ananda questions about the Vajjis and said that as long as the Vajjis kept up these seven things, they would prosper and not decline:

- They should hold assemblies often and in full numbers;
- They should meet in harmony, rise in harmony and act in harmony;
- They should not arbitrarily break old rules, nor impose new ones;
- They should respect elders and listen to their advice;
- They should not abduct or use force on the women of the clans;
- They should honour the shrines (chaityas) inside and outside the city;
- They should protect the arahants (saints).

## A Centre of Religion and Culture

Vaishali is sacred to both Buddhist and Jain traditions. According to Jain tradition, the 24th Tirthankara **Mahavira** was born at **Kundagrama**, a suburb of Vaishali; his mother **Trishala** was the sister of the Licchavi chief **Chetaka**. The Buddha came to Vaishali many times; here he first admitted women (his aunt Mahaprajapati Gautami) into the Sangha, and the courtesan **Amrapali** donated her mango grove to him — this story comes from Buddhist texts. After the Buddha's nirvana the Licchavis received a share of his relics. Later, in about 383 BCE, in the time of King Kalashoka, the **Second Buddhist Council** was also held at Vaishali.

## The Struggle with Magadha and Significance

Finally Ajatashatru attacked the Vajjis. According to Buddhist tradition Vassakara played a role in sowing dissension among the Vajjis. After a long struggle the Vajji region came under Magadha in the fifth century BCE, though the identity of the Licchavis survived — centuries later the Gupta emperor Chandragupta I married the Licchavi princess **Kumaradevi**.

The Vajji confederacy is evidence that in ancient India, besides monarchy, there was also a tradition of collective rule, where decisions were made by discussion in an assembly. For this reason Vaishali is often called "one of the world's earliest republics", though participation there was limited to a few aristocratic clans.
