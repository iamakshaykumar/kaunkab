## Background: A Pact and an Execution

In **1930**, with the **Salt Satyagraha**, the Congress launched the **Civil Disobedience Movement**. Thousands of people went to jail. After about a year of struggle, on **5 March 1931**, the **Gandhi-Irwin Pact** was concluded between Mahatma Gandhi and the Viceroy, **Lord Irwin**. Under it the government agreed to release non-violent satyagrahis and to allow people near the coast to make salt for their own use, while the Congress agreed to suspend the movement and to take part in the Second Round Table Conference.

Not everyone was satisfied with this pact. Among the young there was a complaint that the pact did not include a condition to stop the hanging of **Bhagat Singh, Rajguru** and **Sukhdev**. Gandhi had asked the Viceroy to commute the sentence, but on **23 March 1931** the three revolutionaries were hanged in Lahore jail. It was in this atmosphere of grief and anger that, only a few days later, the annual session of the Congress was held at **Karachi**.

## The Session: Patel Presides

The session was presided over by **Sardar Vallabhbhai Patel**, who had become famous across the country after the success of the **Bardoli Satyagraha** of 1928. The session was held in **the last week of March 1931**; most sources place it between 26 and 31 March, while some give 29 March as the date the main session opened.

When Gandhi reached Karachi, some young people linked to the **Naujawan Bharat Sabha** protested against him with **black flags** and slogans. Gandhi accepted the protest calmly. The session paid tribute to the sacrifice and courage of Bhagat Singh and his comrades, while the Congress also repeated its policy that it did not support political violence of any kind.

The session approved the **Gandhi-Irwin Pact** and reaffirmed the goal of **Purna Swaraj** (complete independence). The decision to send Gandhi to London as the Congress's **sole representative** at the **Second Round Table Conference** was also taken here.

## The Fundamental Rights Resolution

The Karachi session is remembered above all for its resolution on **'Fundamental Rights and the National Economic Programme'**. Its draft was prepared mainly by **Jawaharlal Nehru**, and Gandhi also gave suggestions. It was the first time the Congress stated clearly that swaraj meant not only the departure of the British but also the **political and economic freedom** of the common people. The resolution said that to end the exploitation of the masses, **economic freedom** was necessary along with political freedom.

The main **civil rights** in the resolution were:

- **Freedom of expression and of the press**, and the right to assemble peacefully and to form associations.
- **Equality before the law**, without discrimination of caste, creed or sex.
- **Equal rights** for all in the use of public roads, wells, schools and other public places.
- Elections on the basis of **adult franchise**.
- **Free and compulsory primary education**.
- Protection of the culture, language and script of **minorities** and of different linguistic areas.
- **Neutrality** of the state towards all religions, that is, a secular state.

## The National Economic Programme

The second part of the resolution was economic, and was considered quite forward-looking for its time:

- A substantial reduction in **rent and land revenue**, and exemption from rent for uneconomic small holdings.
- **Relief from debt** for peasants and control of usury.
- For industrial workers, a **living wage**, limited hours of work, healthy working conditions, and protection in old age, sickness and unemployment.
- Protection for **women workers**, and the right of peasants and workers to **form unions**.
- State ownership or control of **key industries, mineral resources, railways, waterways and shipping**.
- Abolition of the **salt tax**, and cuts in military expenditure and high official salaries.

Some historians believe the resolution was also an attempt to satisfy the leftist and younger section within the Congress, which was unhappy with the Gandhi-Irwin Pact.

## Significance and Legacy

The Karachi resolution gave the national movement a **socio-economic direction**. It made clear that free India would be a **democratic, secular** state in which every citizen would have equal rights and the interests of poor peasants and workers would be protected. In later years the Congress's policies and election manifestos were based on this framework.

The echo of this resolution can clearly be heard in the **Constitution of India**, which came into force in **1950**. **Part III (Fundamental Rights)** of the Constitution contains rights such as freedom of expression, equality and the end of untouchability, while **Part IV (Directive Principles of State Policy)** includes goals such as a living wage, free education and a just distribution of resources. That is why the Karachi session is counted among the important milestones in the intellectual foundation of the Indian Constitution.
