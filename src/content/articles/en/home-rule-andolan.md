## Background: War and Stagnation

After the Surat split of 1907 the Congress was in the hands of the Moderates and the national movement had slowed down. The Extremist leader **Bal Gangadhar Tilak**, after serving a six-year sentence, returned from Mandalay (Burma) jail in 1914. The First World War began the same year. The war increased price rises and discontent, and Indian leaders felt that this was the right time to raise the demand for self-government.

**Annie Besant**, a British Theosophist leader of Irish origin who had lived in India for years, wanted to run a similar campaign in India on the lines of the Irish 'Home Rule' movement.

## Two Leagues, One Goal

- **Indian Home Rule League (Tilak)** — established in April 1916 during the Bombay Provincial Conference at **Belgaum**. Its field of work was Maharashtra (excluding Bombay city), Karnataka, the Central Provinces and Berar.
- **All India Home Rule League (Besant)** — established in September 1916 at **Adyar**, Madras. Its branches spread to the rest of the country, and **George Arundale** was the chief organiser behind it.

The two leagues divided areas between themselves to avoid conflict. Their goal was **self-government (home rule)** for India while remaining within the British Empire.

## Methods of the Movement

The Home Rule Leagues took politics to ordinary people through meetings, pamphlets, booklets, libraries and newspapers. Tilak's **'Kesari'** (Marathi) and **'Mahratta'** (English) and Besant's **'New India'** and **'Commonweal'** were its main media.

In this period Tilak's famous slogan rang out — **"Swaraj is my birthright and I shall have it."** It is first linked to the Belgaum conference of 1916. In English its words are found with either "shall" or "will", and according to some sources the slogan was coined by Tilak's associate **Joseph 'Kaka' Baptista**, and Tilak adopted it and made it immortal.

At the **Lucknow session** of December 1916 the Moderates and Extremists united again and the Congress–Muslim League agreement (the Lucknow Pact) was made. In this atmosphere of unity the Home Rule movement gained further strength; leaders such as **Muhammad Ali Jinnah** also joined it.

## Besant's Internment

Alarmed by the growing popularity of the movement, the Madras government in June 1917 interned Annie Besant and her associates George Arundale and **B. P. Wadia** at **Ootacamund (Ooty)**. This had the opposite effect — there were protests across the country and many new people, including some Moderate leaders, joined the Home Rule League.

On 20 August 1917 the Secretary of State for India, **Edwin Montagu**, declared that the aim of British policy was "the gradual development of self-governing institutions... the progressive realisation of responsible government" in India. In September 1917 Besant was released and in December she was elected president of the Congress.

## The End of the Movement

After 1918 the movement slowed down. The Moderates were satisfied by the Montagu declaration, Besant remained uncertain about the reforms, and Tilak went to England to fight a libel suit against the English journalist Valentine Chirol. Meanwhile Gandhi rose to prominence. In 1920 Besant's League became the 'Swarajya Sabha' under Gandhi's leadership and later merged into the Congress.

## Significance

The Home Rule movement took the demand for self-government out of the debate of a few educated people and carried it to towns and villages. It prepared a generation of workers who, only a few years later, became the backbone of Gandhi's Non-Cooperation Movement.
