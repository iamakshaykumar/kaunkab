## Background — Two Companies, One Coast

By the middle of the eighteenth century, on India's eastern (Coromandel) coast the centre of the English East India Company was **Madras** (Fort St George) and that of the French company was **Pondicherry**. This region was called the 'Carnatic', and the **Nawabs of Arcot** ruled here under the Nizam of Hyderabad. The weak central power after Aurangzeb and the Anglo-French enmity in Europe pushed these trading companies into a political and military rivalry.

## The First Carnatic War (1746–1748)

The effect of the **War of the Austrian Succession** in Europe reached India. The French naval commander **La Bourdonnais** captured **Madras** on **21 September 1746**. The Nawab of Arcot, **Anwar-ud-din**, objected to this fighting in his territory and sent an army against the French, but in the same year 1746, near the Adyar river, a small, disciplined French force defeated his far larger army. This event showed that even a small army trained in the European manner could prevail over Indian armies. After the **Treaty of Aix-la-Chapelle** of **1748**, Madras was returned to the English.

## The Second Carnatic War (1749–1754)

After the death of the **Nizam-ul-Mulk** in 1748, succession disputes broke out in Hyderabad between **Nasir Jang** and **Muzaffar Jang**, and in the Carnatic between Anwar-ud-din and **Chanda Sahib**. The French governor **Dupleix** (Governor-General from 1742) supported Muzaffar Jang and Chanda Sahib. In the Battle of **Ambur** on **3 August 1749**, Anwar-ud-din was killed and Chanda Sahib became Nawab of Arcot; Anwar-ud-din's son **Muhammad Ali** fled to Trichinopoly. For some time there were pro-French rulers in both the Deccan and the Carnatic, and the French commander **Bussy** became influential at the Hyderabad court.

The English supported Muhammad Ali. In 1751, while Chanda Sahib was besieging Trichinopoly, the young **Robert Clive** captured **Arcot** with a few hundred soldiers. Chanda Sahib's son Raza Sahib besieged Arcot from **23 September to 14 November 1751**, but the fort did not fall. This success made Clive famous, although some historians also see a role in it for the weaknesses of the opposing side and for luck. The French government was dissatisfied with Dupleix's costly policy; in 1754 he was recalled, and in the **Treaty of Pondicherry** Muhammad Ali Wallajah was recognised as the Nawab of the Carnatic.

## The Third Carnatic War (1756/57–1763)

As soon as the **Seven Years' War** (1756) broke out in Europe, fighting began again in India. In 1757 the English captured the French settlement of Chandernagore in Bengal, and after Plassey the revenues of Bengal began to come into English hands. France sent **Count de Lally**, who captured Fort St David in 1758 but failed in the siege of Madras.

On **22 January 1760**, in the decisive Battle of **Wandiwash** (Vandavasi), the English commander **Eyre Coote** defeated Lally. After this the French were confined to Pondicherry, which surrendered in January 1761 after a long siege. In the **Treaty of Paris** of **1763**, Pondicherry and Chandernagore were returned to France, but fortifying them and keeping an army there were prohibited.

## Results and Significance

The Carnatic Wars settled that among the European powers in India, the English would be ahead. These wars also taught several lessons — the importance of **sepoys** and armies trained in the European manner, the policy of building political influence by interfering in the succession disputes of Indian rulers (which Dupleix began and the English adopted), and the decisive role of sea power and a strong economic base. The resources of Bengal gave the English the advantage that the French did not have.
