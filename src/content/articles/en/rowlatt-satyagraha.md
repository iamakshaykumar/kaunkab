## Why It Became a 'Black Law'

During the First World War the government had extraordinary powers under the Defence of India Act (1915). To keep a check on revolutionary activity even after the war ended, a committee was formed under Justice **Sidney Rowlatt**, and two bills were prepared on its recommendations. On 6 February 1919 they were introduced in the Central Legislative Council and, despite the united opposition of Indian members, were passed on **18 March 1919**; they received assent on 21 March.

Its official name was the **Anarchical and Revolutionary Crimes Act, 1919**. People began to call it the 'Black Law'.

## Provisions of the Law

- Suspected persons could be kept in detention **without trial**.
- Arrest without warrant and strict control of the press were possible.
- A special court of three judges could hear cases in a closed room, without a jury, and there was no appeal to a higher court against its decision.
- Rights such as habeas corpus could be suspended.

In protest, **Madan Mohan Malaviya**, **Mazharul Haque** and **Muhammad Ali Jinnah** resigned from the Council.

## Gandhi's First All-India Satyagraha

After the local struggles of Champaran, Kheda and Ahmedabad, Gandhi for the first time called the whole country together. In February 1919 the **Satyagraha Sabha** was formed in Bombay, whose members took a pledge to disobey this law. It was decided that there would be a **hartal** (strike), fasting and prayer across the country on one day.

The date of the strike was first fixed as 30 March, then changed to **6 April 1919**. In Delhi the information arrived late, so the strike took place there on 30 March itself and several people were killed in police firing. On 6 April shops and businesses stayed closed in many cities, including Bombay, Ahmedabad, Lahore and Amritsar. In this movement, for the first time, common people of the cities, traders and artisans joined national politics in large numbers, and in many places scenes of Hindu–Muslim unity were seen.

## Violence, Repression and Suspension

The movement could not remain entirely peaceful. On 9 April, while travelling to Punjab, Gandhi was stopped near **Palwal**, arrested and sent back to Bombay. As this news spread, violence broke out in Ahmedabad, Viramgam and Punjab. On 10 April, tension rose in Amritsar after the arrest of **Dr. Saifuddin Kitchlew** and **Dr. Satyapal**, and on 13 April the massacre at **Jallianwala Bagh** took place. Martial law was imposed in Punjab.

Pained by the violence, Gandhi suspended the satyagraha on **18 April 1919**. He admitted that it was his mistake to call people to civil disobedience without giving them sufficient training in non-violence, a mistake which he called a 'Himalayan blunder'.

## Significance

The Rowlatt Satyagraha did not succeed in its immediate aim — the law was not withdrawn at once. Even so, it established Gandhi as a national leader and showed that the whole country could be made to stand together through strikes, fasting and satyagraha. The anger born of the Rowlatt Act and Jallianwala Bagh, joined with the Khilafat issue, became the ground for the **Non-Cooperation Movement** of 1920. The Rowlatt Act was finally repealed in March 1922.
