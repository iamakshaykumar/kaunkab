## Background

Sanitation has long been a major challenge in India. **Mahatma Gandhi** had called cleanliness as important as independence. After independence, programmes such as the **Central Rural Sanitation Programme** in 1986, the **Total Sanitation Campaign** in 1999 and the **Nirmal Bharat Abhiyan** in 2012 were run. Even so, according to the 2011 Census about half of the country's households, and a very large number in villages, had no toilet. Open defecation spread diseases such as diarrhoea, affected children's nutrition, and was also linked to questions of women's safety and dignity.

## The Launch of the Mission

On Gandhi Jayanti, **2 October 2014**, Prime Minister **Narendra Modi** launched the **Swachh Bharat Mission**, sweeping with a broom himself at **Valmiki Basti** in New Delhi and appealing to people to give 100 hours a year to cleanliness. The goal was set that India should become **open-defecation free (ODF)** by Gandhi's **150th birth anniversary**, that is **2 October 2019**.

The mission was made up of two parts:

- **Swachh Bharat Mission (Grameen)**: household and community toilets in villages, overseen by the Ministry of Drinking Water and Sanitation (now part of the **Ministry of Jal Shakti**). Eligible families were given an incentive of up to 12,000 rupees to build a household toilet.
- **Swachh Bharat Mission (Urban)**: toilets, solid waste management and cleaning in cities, run by the **Ministry of Housing and Urban Affairs**.

Film stars, sportspersons and well-known personalities were made brand ambassadors of the campaign. From 2016 the cleanliness ranking of cities began through the **Swachh Survekshan**, in which **Indore** in Madhya Pradesh stayed in first place for several consecutive years.

## Government Claims

According to the government, more than **100 million** household toilets were built in rural India between 2014 and 2019. On **2 October 2019** in Ahmedabad the Prime Minister announced that the rural areas of all the country's states had declared themselves open-defecation free. Government figures showed the availability of toilets in rural households rising from about 39 per cent in 2014 to about 100 per cent.

In February 2020 the Union Cabinet approved the **second phase (2020–21 to 2024–25)**, with an estimated total outlay stated as 1,40,881 crore rupees. Its emphasis is on **ODF Plus**, that is, sustaining ODF status and managing solid and liquid waste.

## Independent Assessments

Independent surveys confirmed a big improvement, but the picture they showed was somewhat different from the government figures:

- According to the **76th round of the National Sample Survey (NSS)** (July–December 2018), about **71 per cent** of rural households had access to a toilet, compared with about 41 per cent in 2012. At the same time government figures were showing much higher numbers.
- According to the **National Family Health Survey (NFHS-5, 2019–21)**, about **19 per cent** of the country's households did not use any toilet, compared with **39 per cent** in 2015–16.
- A **World Health Organization** study estimated that better sanitation could avert lakhs of deaths from diarrhoea in rural India; according to one estimate, at least 180,000 such deaths were averted after 2014.

Critics and some researchers said that the mission put more emphasis on **building** toilets and less on their **use** and on behaviour change. In some places there were allegations of pressure, such as shaming people who defecated in the open or withholding government benefits. Questions were also raised about emptying single-pit toilets, their usefulness in flood-affected areas, and the persistence of **manual scavenging**. Cases of irregularities in toilet construction also came to light in some states. The government maintained that the ODF declarations were verified and that the aim of the second phase is to remedy these very shortcomings.

## Significance

Despite the differences, almost all sides agree that the Swachh Bharat Mission expanded access to toilets in rural India on an unprecedented scale in a very short time and made sanitation a subject of national debate. It presented cleanliness not merely as a government scheme but as a mass movement. The challenge ahead is to ensure that the toilets built are used regularly, that excreta and waste are disposed of safely, and that the dignity and safety of the workers who do sanitation work are secured.
