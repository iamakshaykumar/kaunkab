## A War Recorded in Hymns

The oldest literary mention of a war in Indian history is the **Dasarajna Battle**, that is, the "Battle of the Ten Kings". All our information about it comes from a few hymns of the **seventh book of the Rigveda**, mainly **Hymn 18** (verses 5 to 21), along with **Hymns 33** and **83**. The seventh book is traditionally associated with the sage **Vasishtha** and his family.

These are not history books but poems composed in praise of the god **Indra**. In them the credit for the victory is given to Indra (and, in Hymn 83, to Indra-Varuna). So the details, numbers and sequence of the war are unclear in many places, and scholars interpret them differently.

## The Two Sides

On one side was **Sudas Paijavana** (son of Pijavana), king of the **Bharata** tribe, with the **Tritsu** people. Sudas's priest was **Vasishtha**; in Hymn 33 the descendants of Vasishtha say that through their prayer Indra protected Sudas in the battle of the ten kings.

On the other side was a confederation of many tribes. The hymns mention the **Puru, Yadu, Turvasha, Anu, Druhyu**, and also names such as **Alina, Paktha, Bhalana, Shiva and Vishanin**. "Ten kings" is perhaps a round number, since the names counted in the hymns are more than ten.

The cause of the war is not clearly stated in the hymns. The third book is associated with the sage **Vishvamitra**, who is believed to have been earlier the priest of the Bharatas. Some scholars have guessed that Vishvamitra, removed from the post of priest, sided with the opposing confederation, but this is only a hypothesis, and many scholars do not accept that there is evidence of such enmity between Vasishtha and Vishvamitra.

## On the Banks of the Parushni

According to Hymn 18, the battle took place near the **Parushni** river, which is identified with today's **Ravi**. In Griffith's translation, verses 8–9 say that "fools" tried to divert or divide the waters of the Parushni, but Indra stopped them. Later verses describe the enemies drowning in the water; verse 12 mentions the defeat of **Kavasha** and **Druhyu** in the water.

One interpretation is that the army of the confederation was swept away when a dam or embankment of the river broke, but this is only an interpretation of the verses, not a firm account. Verse 14 speaks of the killing of "six thousand six hundred and sixty-six" warriors of the Anu and Druhyu side; this appears to be a poetic number.

## A Second Front on the Yamuna

At the end of the hymn (verses 18–19) another battle is mentioned, connected with the region of the **Yamuna**. Here a chieftain named **Bheda** is defeated and his wealth seized, and tribes such as the **Aja, Shigru and Yakshu** brought tribute to Sudas. This suggests that Sudas's influence extended east of Punjab as well.

## Date and Historicity

There is no fixed date for this war. The composition of the Rigveda is generally placed between about **1500 and 1200 BCE**. The Vedic scholar **Michael Witzel** places the war roughly between **1450 and 1300 BCE**; on this basis "c. 14th century BCE" is written here, which is only an estimate.

Many translators have taken it as a real event. But the Rigveda translator **Stephanie Jamison** cautions that the description of the war in the hymns is "not at all clear", so care is needed in using it as a solid historical source. No archaeological evidence of this war has been found.

## Significance

The Dasarajna Battle gives us a glimpse of Rigvedic society: small **tribes** (janas), their kings and priests, and struggles over cattle and rivers. According to scholars, the Bharata and Puru tribes later merged to form the **Kuru** tribe, which became the most influential kingdom of the Later Vedic period. For this reason the war is seen as a turning point in Vedic history, even if its details remain forever uncertain.
