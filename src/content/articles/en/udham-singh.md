## From Orphanage to Revolution

Udham Singh was born on **26 December 1899** at **Sunam** in Punjab. His childhood name was **Sher Singh**. After his father's death he and his brother grew up at the **Central Khalsa Orphanage** in Amritsar, where he received the name Udham Singh.

The **Jallianwala Bagh massacre** of 13 April 1919 shook the whole of Punjab. According to a popular story, Udham Singh was serving water to people in the Bagh that day. Many historians question this claim since no contemporary evidence is found for it — but it is certain that the event became an inspiring memory of his life.

## O'Dwyer in His Sights

At that time the Lieutenant Governor of Punjab was **Sir Michael O'Dwyer**. The firing took place on the order of General Reginald Dyer, but O'Dwyer openly supported Dyer's action and had martial law imposed in Punjab. So many Indians regarded O'Dwyer as the real person responsible for the massacre. General Dyer had already died of illness in 1927.

## The Ghadar Party and Long Preparation

In the 1920s Udham Singh travelled to several countries and came into contact with the **Ghadar Party** active abroad. He regarded **Bhagat Singh** as his ideal. On returning to India in 1927 he was sentenced to five years for possessing arms without a licence. After his release in 1931 he reached London in **1934** via Germany and lived there for years doing various jobs.

## 13 March 1940: Caxton Hall

On **13 March 1940** there was a joint meeting of the East India Association and the Central Asian Society at **Caxton Hall** in London, at which O'Dwyer was a speaker. As soon as the meeting ended Udham Singh drew a revolver and fired. O'Dwyer died almost at once. The Secretary of State for India **Lord Zetland**, **Louis Dane** and **Lord Lamington** were wounded. Udham Singh made no attempt to escape and was arrested on the spot.

In custody he gave his name as **'Ram Mohammad Singh Azad'** — this name reflected the Hindu, Muslim and Sikh identities together with the aspiration for 'Azad' (freedom).

## Trial and Hanging

On **4 June 1940** the trial began at the **Old Bailey** (Central Criminal Court) in London. In court Udham Singh tried to speak about the atrocities of British rule, but large parts of his statement were not allowed to be made public at the time. He was found guilty of murder, sentenced to death and hanged at **Pentonville Prison** in London on **31 July 1940**.

## Reaction and Legacy

At that time the Congress leadership distanced itself from the killing; **Jawaharlal Nehru** condemned it, calling it a senseless act. But in Punjab and among overseas Indians Udham Singh became a hero. In later years in independent India his image changed and he came to be called **'Shaheed-e-Azam'**. In **1974** his remains were brought to India and cremated at Sunam. Districts and institutions in Punjab and Uttarakhand are named after him — the **Udham Singh Nagar** district of Uttarakhand is named after him.
