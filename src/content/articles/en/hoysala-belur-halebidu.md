## The Hoysala Dynasty and Vishnuvardhana

From the eleventh to the fourteenth century the **Hoysala** dynasty ruled a large part of today's Karnataka. In the beginning they were feudatories of the Chalukyas of Kalyani. The most famous king of this dynasty was **Vishnuvardhana** (reigned c. 1108–1152 CE), whose original name was **Bittideva**.

In 1116 CE Vishnuvardhana's army defeated the Cholas at **Talakadu**, after which he took the title "Talakadugonda". According to tradition he was first a Jain and later became a Vaishnava under the influence of the acharya **Ramanujacharya**. His chief queen **Shantala Devi** remained a follower of Jainism, and the king also patronised Jain institutions.

## The Chennakeshava Temple at Belur

In **1117 CE** Vishnuvardhana had the construction of the **Chennakeshava** (beautiful Keshava, that is Vishnu) temple begun at **Belur** (Velapura in inscriptions) on the bank of the **Yagachi river**. Belur was then the early capital of the Hoysalas. Many historians link it to the victory at Talakadu. According to tradition it took about **103 years** and three generations to complete.

The temple is famous for its **madanika** (bracket figure) sculptures — figures of women absorbed in dance, make-up and music, set on brackets beneath the eaves. Originally there were about 40 of them, of which 38 survive. The carved pillars inside, which look as if turned on a lathe and shine brightly, are also worth seeing. Nearby there is also a small temple sponsored by Shantala Devi.

## The Hoysaleswara Temple at Halebidu

In Vishnuvardhana's time the capital became **Dwarasamudra** (today's **Halebidu**). Here, in about **1121 CE**, the construction of the **Hoysaleswara** temple began. According to an inscription it was built by the king's officer **Ketamalla**.

It is a **twin temple** of Shiva — two sanctums, one **Hoysaleswara** (after the king's name) and the other **Shantaleswara** (after Queen Shantala's name). In front of each is a separate huge **Nandi** pavilion. On the outer walls, in many bands one above another, are carved elephants, lions, horsemen, creepers, and stories from the Ramayana, Mahabharata and Puranas. The temple's towers no longer survive, so it looks flat from above.

## Hallmarks of the Hoysala Style

Hoysala temples have some special features:
- **Stone** — soft **soapstone** (chlorite schist), which is soft when taken out of the quarry and hardens later. This made jewellery-like fine carving possible.
- **Star-shaped plan** — the walls of the sanctum turn in many angles, so that from above the plan looks like a star (stellate).
- **Jagati** — the whole temple stands on a high platform, which also serves as a circumambulation path.

This style was formed by combining elements of the northern Nagara and southern Dravida styles, so it is sometimes also linked to the "Vesara" tradition.

## Invasion, Decline and World Heritage

In the early fourteenth century the armies of **Alauddin Khalji** attacked Dwarasamudra, and in 1326 the army of **Muhammad bin Tughluq** plundered Belur and Halebidu. In Kannada "Halebidu" itself means "old camp", that is, the old capital. After the death of the last great king **Veera Ballala III** in the battle of Madurai in 1343, Hoysala power ended, and in this region the Vijayanagara Empire rose.

In **2023** UNESCO added the three temples of Belur, Halebidu and Somanathapura to the World Heritage List under the name **"Sacred Ensembles of the Hoysalas"**. Even today the Chennakeshava temple at Belur is a place of worship, and these temples are the most beautiful identity of Karnataka's art.
