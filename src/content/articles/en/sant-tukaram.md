## An Ordinary Householder of Dehu

**Tukaram** was born in the village of **Dehu** on the bank of the Indrayani river near Pune. Scholars differ on his date of birth: both 1598 and 1608 are given; the more popular view is about **1608**. His family was connected with farming and small trade and moneylending, and had been devotees of **Vitthal** (Vithoba) of Pandharpur for generations.

Tukaram calls himself a "Shudra" and a "Kunbi", that is, he was not a Brahmin. Yet he went on to become the greatest poet of Marathi devotion, and this is what makes his life socially important too.

## Crisis and Detachment

According to traditional biographies, his parents died while he was still young. Around 1630 a terrible famine struck the Deccan, in which his first wife and a son died and his business was also ruined. These sorrows made him indifferent to the world. It is said that he threw the papers of his debts into the Indrayani and began to practise solitary meditation on hills such as Bhamnath.

According to the Warkari tradition, the saints **Namdev** and Vitthal inspired him in a dream to compose abhangs, and he accepted the saint-poet **Babaji Chaitanya** as his guru.

## Abhangs and the Story of the Indrayani

Tukaram's compositions are called **abhangs**, a Marathi metre whose name means "that which is not broken". The collection in circulation in his name, the **Tukaram Gatha**, has about **4,500** abhangs according to tradition. But no manuscript of all the abhangs in his own handwriting has been found, and there are variant readings in different copies, so the authenticity of every abhang is not certain.

The most famous story is that some scholars, angered by the religious teaching of a Shudra (tradition names Rameshwar Bhat), told him to drown his manuscripts in the **Indrayani river**. Tukaram did so and sat fasting for thirteen days on the riverbank; it is said that on the thirteenth day the manuscripts rose up from the water dry and safe. This miracle story is a matter of faith, but it certainly gives a glimpse of the caste opposition of that time.

## The Tone of His Utterances

Tukaram's abhangs are in everyday Marathi, sometimes gentle, sometimes sharp. He satirises ritualism, hypocritical holy men, greed and caste pride openly, and says that true devotion needs neither high birth nor knowledge of the scriptures. Collective **kirtan** and remembrance of the Name were at the centre of his practice. A Brahmin woman, **Bahinabai**, became his disciple, which shows that his influence crossed the limits of caste and gender.

The head of the monastery at Dehu, **Mambaji Gosavi**, strongly opposed him, but tradition says that he too later became his devotee. A story of his meeting with Shivaji is also current, which historians regard as a legend.

## End and Legacy

Tukaram died around **1649–1650**; most historians accept 1650. The Warkari tradition holds that he went bodily to **Vaikuntha**, which is called "Vaikunthagaman".

In the Warkari stream that began with Sant Dnyaneshwar, Tukaram is regarded as the last and most popular peak. There is a saying, "Dnyandeve rachila paya, Tuka jhalase kalas" (Dnyandev laid the foundation, Tuka became the finial). In the nineteenth century a modern printed edition of his Gatha was published, Mahatma Gandhi translated his abhangs in jail, and in 2002 the Government of India issued a postage stamp on him. Even today his abhangs are sung in village after village in Maharashtra.
