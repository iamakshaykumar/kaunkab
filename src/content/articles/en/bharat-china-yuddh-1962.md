## Roots of the Border Dispute

Along the roughly 3,500-kilometre Himalayan border between India and China, two large areas were disputed. In the west, India regarded **Aksai Chin** as part of Ladakh, while China described it as part of its Xinjiang-Tibet region and in the 1950s had built a road through it. In the east, India regarded the **McMahon Line**, which came out of the 1914 Shimla Conference, as the border, while China rejected it as a line imposed in the colonial period and claimed a large part of NEFA (today's Arunachal Pradesh).

After the 1954 Panchsheel agreement and the era of 'Hindi-Chini Bhai-Bhai', relations worsened rapidly. After the revolt in Tibet in March 1959, the **Dalai Lama** came to India and was given asylum, which China regarded as interference in its internal affairs. In the same year there were violent clashes on the border, including at the **Kongka Pass** in Ladakh. Correspondence between Prime Minister Nehru and Premier **Zhou Enlai** and the talks of 1960 produced no solution.

## The Forward Policy

From the end of 1961 India adopted a policy of moving forward in the disputed areas and setting up small posts, which was called the **'Forward Policy'**. India's argument was that it was setting up posts only inside the territory it claimed; China saw it as a gradual attempt to seize its territory. In September–October 1962, clashes took place in the eastern sector near the **Dhola Post** and the **Thag La Ridge**.

## The First Phase of the War

On **20 October 1962** the Chinese army attacked simultaneously in the east at **Namka Chu** and **Tawang**, and in the west in the Chip Chap Valley and the **Pangong Lake** areas. Indian soldiers did not have enough warm clothing, weapons, supplies and roads for fighting at high altitude. Within a few days the Indian brigade at Namka Chu was scattered and Tawang fell into Chinese hands. After this the fighting paused for about three weeks, during which China offered talks and India rejected them on its own terms.

## The Second Phase and the Ceasefire

From **14 November** the fighting flared up again. In the east the defence lines at **Sela** and **Bomdila** broke and the Indian army also had to fall back at **Walong**. In the west, on 18 November at **Rezang La**, a company of 13 Kumaon under Major **Shaitan Singh** held the position to the last; he was posthumously awarded the Param Vir Chakra.

On the night of 20 November China announced a **unilateral ceasefire**, which came into effect on 21 November, and said it would withdraw behind the 1959 'Line of Actual Control'. According to official figures, 1,383 Indian soldiers were killed, 1,696 went missing, 548 to 1,047 were wounded and 3,968 became prisoners of war; China stated that 722 of its soldiers were killed and 1,697 wounded.

## The Henderson Brooks Report and Political Impact

After the defeat, Defence Minister **V. K. Krishna Menon** had to resign and changes were made in the army's command too. Army Chief General J. N. Chaudhuri assigned Lieutenant General **Henderson Brooks** and Brigadier **P. S. Bhagat** to inquire into the military causes. Their report, prepared in 1963, criticised a policy imposed by Army Headquarters that was inconsistent with military sense, and the shortcomings of the high command. The Government of India has kept it secret on grounds of national security; the Government has not authenticated the excerpt leaked in 2014, although scholars generally regard it as authentic.

## Significance

This war changed India's defence policy: the army was expanded and modernised on a large scale and attention to border roads increased. The outcome of the war is generally regarded as a Chinese military victory, but the border dispute remained unresolved. The claims of both countries over Aksai Chin and Arunachal Pradesh still stand today, and the Line of Actual Control (LAC) remains the most sensitive issue in India-China relations.
