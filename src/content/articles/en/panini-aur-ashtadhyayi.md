## The Grammarian of Shalatura

**Panini** is linked with the village of **Shalatura** in the ancient region of **Gandhara**, which is believed to lie near the confluence of the Indus and Kabul rivers, in the north-western part of present-day Pakistan (around the village of Lahore). In the 7th century the Chinese traveller **Xuanzang** also mentioned the town on the bank of the Indus where Panini was said to have been born. From his name "Dakshiputra" it is inferred that his mother's name was **Dakshi**.

Very little is certain about Panini's life. What is found about his teacher, his years and his place of learning is mostly based on later stories.

## When Did Panini Live?

Scholars differ about Panini's time. Different estimates range from the 7th-6th century BCE to the 4th century BCE, but most scholars place him between the **5th and 4th centuries BCE**. According to the scholar George Cardona, on the basis of the mention of coins in the Ashtadhyayi, Panini cannot be earlier than about 400 BCE. One tradition also describes him as a contemporary of a Nanda king, but this is not proven.

## The Structure of the Ashtadhyayi

"Ashtadhyayi" means **"a work of eight chapters"**. Each chapter is divided into four **padas**. The whole work has about **3,959 sutras** (some counts give about 3,996). **Sutras** are very short sentences — often only two or three words — that were easy to remember, because at that time knowledge was passed on mainly orally.

Some supporting lists are also attached to the Ashtadhyayi:

- **Shiva Sutras (Maheshvara Sutras)** — a list dividing Sanskrit sounds into 14 short groups, from which short codes such as "pratyahara" are formed;
- **Dhatupatha** — a list of the root verbs;
- **Ganapatha** — groups of words to which the same rules apply.

## A Machine of Rules

Panini looked at language as a system. How words are formed from roots and suffixes, how sandhi works, how compounds and cases operate — all this was written as an ordered series of rules. There are also rules for deciding which one applies when two rules clash. Along with spoken Sanskrit (bhasha), he also recorded separately the special forms of the Vedic language.

The Ashtadhyayi is the oldest available Sanskrit grammar, although Panini himself names many grammarians before him. The standard form of Sanskrit established after this is called **classical Sanskrit**.

## Katyayana and Patanjali

Two great scholars later worked on the Ashtadhyayi. **Katyayana** wrote **vartikas** (comments and corrections) on its sutras. Then **Patanjali** composed a detailed commentary called the *Mahabhashya*, which is thought to belong to the Shunga period (about the 2nd century BCE). Panini, Katyayana and Patanjali together are called the **"Munitraya"** (three sages) in tradition.

There are also some folk tales about Panini — the *Kathasaritsagara* has a story of his penance to Shiva, and a verse of the *Panchatantra* says he was killed by a lion. These should be read as tradition, not as history.

## Impact on the Modern World

When European scholars read the Ashtadhyayi in the 19th-20th centuries, they were impressed by its precision. Linguists such as Ferdinand de Saussure, Leonard Bloomfield and Roman Jakobson are believed to have been influenced by it. Some scholars compare it with the formal grammar methods of computer science (such as Backus-Naur Form). Even so, Panini's greatest contribution is that, thanks to him, the form of the Sanskrit language remained stable for about two and a half thousand years.
