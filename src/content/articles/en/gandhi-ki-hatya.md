## Tension After Partition

The Partition that came with independence in August 1947 brought terrible communal violence in north India and the displacement of millions of people. In these months **Mahatma Gandhi** was busy restoring peace in Calcutta and then in Delhi. In January 1948 he fasted in Delhi for communal harmony. In the same period the Government of India reversed its decision to withhold the instalment of cash due to Pakistan under the partition settlement. These events bred bitter opposition to Gandhi among some Hindu nationalist groups, which the assassins later gave as their reason.

## The Failed Attempt of 20 January

Some people connected with Pune and Ahmednagar — **Nathuram Godse**, **Narayan Apte**, **Vishnu Karkare**, **Madanlal Pahwa**, **Gopal Godse**, **Digambar Badge** and **Shankar Kistayya** — planned Gandhi's murder. On 20 January 1948 a bomb exploded during the prayer meeting at Birla House, causing a stampede but killing no one. Only Madanlal Pahwa was caught on the spot; the rest escaped. Later there was sharp criticism that, despite the information received after this incident, the police could not break up the conspiracy in time.

## The Evening of 30 January 1948

On 30 January 1948 at about 5:17 in the evening, as 78-year-old Gandhi was walking through the garden of Birla House towards the prayer meeting, Nathuram Godse fired three bullets at him from very close range. Gandhi died on the spot. Godse was captured at the scene. It is popularly believed that his last words were 'He Ram', though eyewitness accounts differ on this.

The funeral procession in Delhi the next day is estimated to have been joined by more than two million people; he was cremated at **Raj Ghat** on the bank of the Yamuna. The whole country was plunged into mourning. On 4 February 1948 the government banned the Rashtriya Swayamsevak Sangh (RSS), a ban that was lifted about a year and a half later, in July 1949. Godse had earlier been associated with the RSS; the organisation denied any connection with the murder.

## The Red Fort Trial

On 27 May 1948 the trial began before Justice **Atma Charan** in a special court set up in the **Red Fort** in Delhi. The prosecution produced 149 witnesses. Digambar Badge became an approver after being granted a conditional pardon. In a long statement in court, Godse held Gandhi responsible for the tragedy of Partition and for the decision to pay money to Pakistan; this was his own version, which the court did not accept as a justification of the murder.

The verdict came on 10 February 1949: death sentences for Godse and Apte, and life imprisonment for six others. **V. D. Savarkar** was acquitted for lack of evidence. From May 1949 the appeal was heard at Peterhoff in Shimla; the High Court upheld most of the sentences but acquitted Dattatreya Parchure and Shankar Kistayya. On 15 November 1949 Godse and Apte were hanged at **Ambala Jail**.

## The Kapur Commission

In 1964, after some convicts who had served their life sentences were released, the question was raised again whether there had been advance knowledge of the conspiracy. A one-member commission of inquiry was then set up under **Jivanlal Kapur**, a former judge of the Supreme Court; most sources give its appointment as 21 November 1966, while some write 1965. The commission completed its inquiry on 30 September 1969. It criticised the laxity of the Delhi and Bombay police and in its conclusions pointed to a role for Savarkar and his group in the conspiracy. This conclusion remains disputed even today: Savarkar had died in 1966 and so could not present his side before the commission, and in 2018 the Supreme Court declined to reconsider the truth of the report's findings.

## Significance

Gandhi's assassination dealt a deep shock to newly independent India and acted as a turning point in stopping the communal violence that followed Partition. Birla House is today a museum called **Gandhi Smriti**, and 30 January is remembered in the country as **Martyrs' Day**.
