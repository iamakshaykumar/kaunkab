## From Badaun to Delhi

**Nizamuddin Auliya** was born in 1238 in a Sayyid family of Badaun (in today's Uttar Pradesh). His father Sayyid Abdullah died when he was about five years old. His mother **Bibi Zulekha** brought him up in hard circumstances, and later the two moved to Delhi. Delhi was then the capital of the Sultanate and a major centre of learning, where the young Nizamuddin received religious education.

## Disciple of Baba Farid

At about the age of twenty he went to Ajodhan (today's Pakpattan, Pakistan) and became a disciple of the Chishti saint **Fariduddin Ganjshakar**, that is, **Baba Farid**. He visited his master several times during the month of Ramadan, and on the third visit Baba Farid made him his successor (khalifa). In this way he became a link in the tradition that had reached him from **Khwaja Moinuddin Chishti** of Ajmer through Qutbuddin Bakhtiyar Kaki and Baba Farid.

## The Khanqah at Ghiyaspur

Returning to Delhi, he settled in the locality of **Ghiyaspur** near the Yamuna, which is known today as Nizamuddin. In his **khanqah** here a langar (free kitchen) ran every day and rich and poor, scholars and the unlettered all came. The essence of his teaching was — love of God means love of the human beings He made, and feeding the hungry and sharing the sorrow of the sad is the greatest worship. According to the Chishti tradition, he kept away from courts and state posts.

In his company the music gathering, the **sama**, had a special place. In this very atmosphere the poetry and music of his beloved disciple **Amir Khusrau** flourished. People lovingly began to call him **Mahbub-e-Ilahi** (the Beloved of God) and **Sultan-ul-Mashaikh** (Sultan of the Saints).

## Distance from the Sultans

During Nizamuddin's lifetime, the descendants of Balban, the Khaljis and then the Tughluq sultans came and went on the throne of Delhi. Some rulers also felt uneasy at the saint's popularity. Sultan **Qutb-ud-din Mubarak Shah Khalji** stopped nobles from going to Ghiyaspur and demanded that the saint attend court, but he did not accept this. In 1320 Mubarak Shah was murdered.

A famous story is told about his tension with Ghiyas-ud-din Tughluq — that returning from Bengal, the sultan sent an order to the saint to leave Delhi, and the saint said "Hunuz Dilli dur ast" (Delhi is still far); the sultan was killed in 1325 by the collapse of a pavilion before reaching Delhi. This saying is still spoken today, but the story belongs to tradition; it is not confirmed by contemporary sources.

## Disciples and 'Fawa'id al-Fu'ad'

His disciple **Amir Hasan Sijzi**, from 1308, wrote down his conversations over many years, which became famous as **Fawa'id al-Fu'ad**. It is regarded as an early and important example of 'malfuzat' (collections of a saint's sayings) in Indian Sufi literature, and it also gives a glimpse of the social life of Delhi of that period.

His spiritual successor was **Nasiruddin Chiragh-e-Dehli**, while other khalifas carried the Chishti tradition to the Deccan, Bengal and other regions.

## The Dargah and Legacy

Nizamuddin Auliya died on 3 April 1325. A few months later Amir Khusrau also passed away and was buried near his master. The original structure of the dargah was built in the time of Muhammad bin Tughluq, Firuz Shah Tughluq had it repaired, and today's white dome was built in **1562** by Faridun Khan. The complex also has the Khalji-era **Jamaat Khana Mosque** and a stepwell completed in 1321.

Today, qawwali is performed at the **Hazrat Nizamuddin Dargah** every evening, and on Thursday evenings there is a special crowd. People of every religion and class take part in the annual **Urs**. Even after seven hundred years, this place recalls the inclusive Sufi tradition that placed love and service at the centre of religion.
