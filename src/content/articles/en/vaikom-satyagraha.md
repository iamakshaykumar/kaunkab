## Background: Untouchability Even on the Roads

In the early twentieth century the caste system in the princely state of **Travancore** was very rigid. People of the Ezhava, Pulaya and other 'avarna' castes could not only not enter temples, but were not even allowed to walk on the public roads surrounding the **Vaikom Mahadeva temple**. Boards put up at various places ordered them to keep away from these roads, while Christians and Muslims could use the same roads.

**T. K. Madhavan**, an Ezhava community leader and journalist, had begun raising the question of temple entry in his writings from 1917. The social reform movement of Sree Narayana Guru also strengthened this awareness. At the **Kakinada Congress session** of 1923, through Madhavan's efforts, a resolution for removing untouchability was passed, after which the Kerala Provincial Congress chose Vaikom as the centre of the struggle.

## 30 March 1924: The Start of the Satyagraha

On **30 March 1924** three satyagrahis dressed in khadi — a caste Nair, an Ezhava and a Pulaya — advanced together to walk on the prohibited road. The police stopped and arrested them. After this, new batches came every day. Within a few days leaders such as Madhavan and **K. P. Kesava Menon** were also arrested.

The government put up barricades on the roads and posted police. The satyagrahis sat before the barricade, sang bhajans and often fasted. Incidents of sitting in protest standing waist-deep in the monsoon floods are also recorded. Some caste-Hindu fanatics also attacked the satyagrahis.

## Expansion of the Movement

In April 1924 **E. V. Ramasamy 'Periyar'** came to Vaikom from the Madras Presidency. He breathed life into the movement with his speeches and went to jail twice. In the Tamil tradition he is called 'Vaikom Veerar'; Kerala's historiography puts more stress on the role of local leaders, and the assessment of Periyar's contribution is sometimes debated. Leaders such as **George Joseph** and **K. Kelappan** also remained active.

On Gandhi's advice, from November 1924 caste Hindus too sent marches from Vaikom to Trivandrum and presented a memorial to the Maharani saying they had no objection to people of lower castes walking on the roads. The Nair community leader **Mannathu Padmanabhan** was among the prominent names associated with these caste-Hindu marches. In February 1925 a resolution to open the roads in the Travancore Legislative Council was defeated by just **one vote**.

## Gandhi's Visit and the Agreement

In **March 1925** Gandhi himself came to Vaikom. He spoke with the satyagrahis, the orthodox Namboodiri Brahmins, **Sree Narayana Guru** and the Regent Maharani **Sethu Lakshmi Bayi**. Under an agreement the police barricade was removed and the satyagrahis gave an assurance that they would not forcibly enter the prohibited roads.

Finally in **November 1925** the government opened the roads on the **north, south and west** of the temple to all castes, while the eastern road and the inner part of the temple remained closed. The satyagraha formally ended on **23 November 1925**.

## Consequences and Legacy

This victory was incomplete, and reformers such as Narayana Guru were not satisfied with it — they wanted not just roads but the right of temple entry. Even so, the Vaikom Satyagraha is regarded as the first major organised non-violent movement against untouchability in India, in which people of different castes and religions stood together.

In the same tradition came the Guruvayur Satyagraha of 1931–32, and finally on **12 November 1936** the Maharaja of Travancore, **Chithira Thirunal Balarama Varma**, issued the **Temple Entry Proclamation**, opening the state's government temples to all Hindus. This change too was limited — temple entry in Cochin and British Malabar became possible much later.
