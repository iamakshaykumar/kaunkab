## The Two Brothers of Ghor

Muhammad of Ghor's full name was **Muizzuddin Muhammad bin Sam**. He was born around **1144** in **Ghor**, a hilly region of central Afghanistan. The Ghurid dynasty then had a unique joint arrangement: the elder brother **Ghiyasuddin Muhammad** was the chief Sultan and looked towards the west (Khurasan), while the younger brother Muizzuddin became ruler of Ghazni in **1173** and advanced to the east, that is, towards India. After Ghiyasuddin's death in 1203 he became the sole Sultan.

## Early Campaigns and First Defeat

His first major Indian campaign was in **1175** against **Multan**, which he seized from the Qarmatian rulers. Then he attacked Gujarat, but in **1178** at **Kasahrada**, near Mount Abu, he was badly defeated by the army of the Chalukya (Solanki) king **Mularaja II**. At that time Mularaja was a minor, and Gujarati tradition credits this victory to the leadership of his mother, Queen Naikidevi.

After this defeat Ghori changed his route and advanced towards Punjab. By taking **Peshawar** in **1179** and **Lahore** in **1186**, he captured Khusrau Malik, the last ruler of the Ghaznavid dynasty. Now the way to the plains of northern India was open.

## The Two Battles of Tarain

In **1191**, in the first battle of **Tarain** (in today's Haryana), the Chauhan king of Ajmer-Delhi, **Prithviraj III**, defeated Ghori; Ghori himself was wounded and his army retreated. The very next year, in **1192**, he returned with a larger and better-organised army. In the second battle of Tarain he won a decisive victory through the tactics of his mounted archers. Prithviraj was captured and later killed. About Prithviraj's death, the story of the sound-guided arrow in the 'Prithviraj Raso' is famous, but historians regard it as a legend.

## Chandawar and Further Expansion

In **1194**, at **Chandawar** (near today's Firozabad), Ghori defeated the Gahadavala king **Jaichand** of Kannauj-Banaras; Jaichand was killed in the battle. After this most campaigns were conducted by his generals:
- **Qutb-ud-din Aibak** carried out campaigns such as those against Delhi, Ajmer, Koil (Aligarh), the attack on Gujarat (1197) and Kalinjar (1202).
- **Bakhtiyar Khalji** advanced towards Bihar and Bengal and spread Ghurid influence there; Bengal coins of 1204 were struck in Ghori's name.

## Defeat, Revolt and Assassination

Despite success in India, Ghori suffered a heavy blow in Central Asia. In **1204**, at **Andkhud**, the army of Khwarazm, together with Qara-Khitai allies, inflicted a heavy defeat on him. As soon as news of this defeat spread, the **Khokhars** revolted in Punjab, which he suppressed with Aibak's help.

While returning from Lahore to Ghazni, on **15 March 1206**, at **Dhamyak** near the Indus (in today's Jhelum district of Pakistan), he was assassinated at the time of the evening prayer. Sources do not agree on who the assassins were — some blame the Khokhars, others the Ismaili assassins. He was buried at Ghazni.

## Legacy

Muhammad of Ghor himself did not settle in India, but his conquests changed the politics of northern India. After him his Indian territories came under his slave generals — **Qutb-ud-din Aibak** at Lahore-Delhi, **Nasiruddin Qabacha** at Multan-Uch and **Tajuddin Yildiz** at Ghazni. Of these, Aibak and then Iltutmish built up the **Delhi Sultanate**, which remained the leading power of northern India for the next three hundred years and more.
