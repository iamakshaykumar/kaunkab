## What Is a Council?

The word "**sangiti**" means "singing together" or collective recitation. The Buddha's teachings were not written down — monks passed them on by **memorising** them. So large assemblies were called in which the teachings and rules were recited together, so that the correct text could be fixed and differences settled. Information about these assemblies comes mainly from later texts such as the **Vinaya Pitaka** and Sri Lanka's **Dipavamsa-Mahavamsa**, and the accounts of the Buddhist schools do not fully agree with each other.

## The First Council: Rajagriha

According to tradition, soon after the Buddha's **Mahaparinirvana**, about 500 arhat monks gathered at **Rajagriha** (present-day Rajgir), the capital of Magadha, near the **Saptaparni cave** on the Vaibhara hill. The assembly was presided over by **Mahakassapa**, and King **Ajatashatru** gave patronage.

- **Ananda** — the Buddha's close companion — recited the discourses (**sutta**).
- **Upali** recited the rules of the monastic order (**Vinaya**).

The date of the Buddha's nirvana is itself disputed (views range from about 483 BCE to about 400 BCE), so the date of this council is also uncertain. Many modern scholars do not believe that the whole Tipitaka was recited then; some regard the whole account as a later composition.

## The Second Council: Vaishali

About **100 years after** the Buddha, the second council was held at **Vaishali**; the Sri Lankan chronicles name its patron as **Kalashoka**, a king of the Shishunaga dynasty. The dispute was over the **"ten points"** of the Vinaya — for example, whether monks may accept **gold and silver (money)**. The monk **Yasa** had objected to the laxity of the Vaishali monks, and the assembly declared these ten points improper. Tradition places the first major split of the Sangha — **Sthaviras** (ancestors of the Theravadins) and **Mahasanghikas** — around this time, although some scholars regard this split as later.

## The Third Council: Pataliputra and Ashoka

According to **Theravada** sources, in the time of Emperor **Ashoka** (about 250 BCE) the third council was held at **Pataliputra**, presided over by **Moggaliputta Tissa**. The purpose was to expel monks of wrong views who had crept into the Sangha. It is believed that the **Kathavatthu** was added at this assembly, and afterwards **dhamma missionaries** were sent to many regions, including Sri Lanka (under Mahinda).

Note that the detailed account of this council is **only in Theravada texts**. Ashoka's own inscriptions do not name any council, although some of his pillar edicts warn monks who **create schisms** in the Sangha.

## The Fourth Council: Kashmir and Kanishka

According to the **Sarvastivada** tradition, under the patronage of the Kushan emperor **Kanishka** (early part of the 2nd century CE) a council was held in **Kashmir**, presided over by **Vasumitra**. In it huge commentaries on Buddhist texts — called **Vibhasha** — were prepared. The Theravada tradition does not recognise this assembly, and its historicity is also debated.

The Theravadins place their own "fourth council" at **Aluvihara** in Sri Lanka in the 1st century BCE, in the time of King Vattagamani, where the Pali texts were first **written down**.

## Significance

Even if the exact details of these councils are debated, they show that the Buddhist Sangha adopted a **collective, assembly-based** method for preserving its texts, reaching agreement on rules and handling differences. This tradition continued into modern times — the councils of 1871 and **1954–56** in Myanmar are examples of this same chain.
