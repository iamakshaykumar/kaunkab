## From Patiali to the Delhi Court

**Amir Khusrau** was born in 1253 at Patiali (in today's Kasganj district of Uttar Pradesh). His father, Amir Saifuddin Mahmud, was a Turkish military officer who had come from Central Asia, and his mother was from an Indian family. After his father's death in childhood he was brought up in Delhi in his maternal grandfather's house, where he was introduced both to Persian literature and to the Indian environment.

## Court Poet to Several Sultans

Khusrau's life was spent in the turbulent period of the Delhi Sultanate, and he served in the courts of several rulers:
- Under the patronage of Balban's sons — Bughra Khan and Prince Muhammad at Multan
- Jalaluddin Khilji, who gave him the title 'Amir'
- **Alauddin Khilji** and his son Mubarak Shah
- Ghiyasuddin Tughluq, founder of the Tughluq dynasty

Many of his works are also sources of history. The *Qiran-us-Sa'dain* (1289) describes the meeting of Bughra Khan and his son Sultan Kaiqubad. The *Khaza'in-ul-Futuh* describes Alauddin's campaigns and building works, the *Ashiqa* the story of Deval Rani and Khizr Khan, the *Nuh Sipihr* the reign of Mubarak Shah, and the *Tughluqnama* the rise of the Tughluqs. Being a court poet, he also lavishly praises his patrons in them, so historians read these works with caution.

## Disciple of Nizamuddin Auliya

Khusrau was among the most beloved disciples of the Chishti Sufi saint **Nizamuddin Auliya**. Apart from court politics, the khanqah at Ghiyaspur (today's Nizamuddin neighbourhood) was the centre of his spiritual life. In the Chishti tradition music assemblies (sama) had a special place, and Khusrau's poetry and music flourished in this atmosphere.

Nizamuddin Auliya died in 1325 and only a few months later, in October 1325, Khusrau also passed away. He was buried near his teacher's dargah. Tradition says that in grief at separation from his teacher he gave up everything.

## Hindavi and the 'Parrot of India'

Khusrau wrote mainly in **Persian**, and he is counted among the greatest Persian poets in India. Because of his sweet voice he was called **Tuti-i-Hind** (the Parrot of India). He took pride in the language of his birthplace and called it **'Hindavi'**.

Today many Hindavi riddles, mukaris and songs are current in Khusrau's name. But scholars point out that for most of them no early manuscript is found; many compositions do not appear in sources before the 18th century. It is therefore hard to say with certainty that they are Khusrau's works — they are part of a living folk tradition that came to be attached to his name.

## The Musical Legacy: Fact and Legend

Khusrau is believed to have shaped the **qawwali** from a blend of Persian, Arabic, Turkish and Indian singing traditions, and that is why he is called the "father of qawwali". The **tarana** style of singing is also linked to him.

The invention of the sitar and tabla is also popularly credited to Khusrau, but there is no contemporary evidence for this and most music historians regard it as legend. Similarly there is not enough evidence to credit him with the beginning of styles such as khayal.

Even so, there is no doubt that Khusrau acted as a bridge between Persian and Indian cultures. Even today his compositions are heard in the qawwalis sung at the Nizamuddin dargah, and the Urs held on his death anniversary is a celebration of this shared heritage that has continued for seven hundred years.
