## Background: A Himalayan Monarchy

From the seventeenth century Sikkim was ruled by Buddhist kings of the **Namgyal dynasty**, who were called **Chogyals**. In the nineteenth century it became a protectorate of British India. After independence this arrangement continued through the India-Sikkim treaty of **1950**: defence, foreign affairs and communications remained with India, while Sikkim stayed autonomous in internal matters.

In Sikkim's population the Bhutia and Lepcha communities had become a minority, and people of Nepali origin were the majority. In the electoral system Bhutia-Lepcha votes were given greater weight, which caused discontent. **Palden Thondup Namgyal** (1923–1982) became Chogyal in 1963. Because of his American wife **Hope Cooke**, this royal court also drew international attention.

## The 1973 Movement and the New Constitution

In April 1973 a large movement broke out demanding 'one person, one vote' and democratic government. Through Indian mediation, an agreement was reached in May 1973 between the Chogyal and the political parties. In the 1974 election the Sikkim National Congress, led by **Kazi Lhendup Dorjee**, won 31 of the 32 seats.

The new Assembly passed the **Government of Sikkim Act, 1974**, which the Chogyal approved on 4 July 1974. This greatly limited the Chogyal's powers. At the Assembly's request, India gave Sikkim the status of 'associate state' through the **35th Constitutional Amendment** (1974) and gave it representation in the Indian Parliament. The Chogyal called this a violation of the 1950 treaty.

## Crisis and Referendum (April 1975)

In early 1975 tension grew between the Chogyal and the democratic parties. There were demonstrations and clashes in Gangtok. At the request of Chief Minister Kazi Lhendup Dorjee, the Indian Army disarmed the palace guards. After this the Assembly passed a resolution to end the monarchy and merge fully with India, and a referendum on these questions was held on **14 April 1975**. According to official results, about **97.5%** of voters supported the resolution.

## The 36th Constitutional Amendment: The 22nd State

After the referendum the Government of India introduced the **36th Constitutional Amendment**. After it was passed by Parliament and received the President's assent, Sikkim became the **22nd state** of India on **16 May 1975**, and the office of Chogyal ended. (Some sources also mention 26 April 1975 as the effective date of statehood, but 16 May is the day celebrated as State Day.) Kazi Lhendup Dorjee became the state's first Chief Minister. By adding **Article 371F** to the Constitution, special provisions were made for Sikkim.

## Differing Views

According to the Government of India and supporters of the merger, this was the result of the democratic will of the people of Sikkim and its elected Assembly, and there was wide discontent with the monarchy. The Chogyal called the referendum 'illegal and unconstitutional'. Critics such as the journalist **Sunanda K. Datta-Ray** questioned the arrangements for voting in remote areas in such a short time. China and Pakistan criticised the merger, while the Soviet Union's reaction was positive but restrained, and the United States regarded it as almost inevitable from a geopolitical point of view. In 1978 Prime Minister Morarji Desai called the merger 'not a desirable step', but also admitted that most of the people there wanted it.

## Significance

The merger of Sikkim was important for India's borders and security in the eastern Himalaya. The last Chogyal, Palden Thondup Namgyal, died in 1982. Today Sikkim is known for its democratic system, its mixed Buddhist-Nepali-Lepcha culture and its environmental conservation, and it celebrates State Day on 16 May.
