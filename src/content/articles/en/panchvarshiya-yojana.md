## Challenges After Independence

In 1947 independent India faced heavy economic challenges. Partition had put irrigated wheat lands and large jute-growing areas in Pakistan, while the jute mills remained in India. Millions of refugees had to be rehabilitated, food had to be imported from abroad because of shortage of grain, and poverty was widespread. From the time of the national movement itself — such as the Congress's National Planning Committee in 1938 — leaders held the idea that the development of independent India should be through careful planning.

## Formation of the Planning Commission

On **15 March 1950** the **Planning Commission** was formed by a resolution of the Central Government. Prime Minister **Jawaharlal Nehru** was its chairman and **Gulzarilal Nanda** the first deputy chairman. It was a body created not by the Constitution but by a government resolution. Its work was to assess the country's resources, set priorities and make five-year plans.

## The First Five-Year Plan (1951–56)

The first plan began in 1951 and its period is taken as 1 April 1951 to 31 March 1956. Its basis was the **Harrod-Domar model**, in which growth is linked to savings and investment. The young economist **K. N. Raj** played an important role in its draft.

The initial outlay of the plan was fixed at about **2,069 crore rupees**, which was later raised to about 2,378 crore rupees. The largest share went to **irrigation and power**, then to **agriculture and community development**, transport and communications, and social services. The aim was to remove the shortage of grain, settle the refugees and lay the foundation for later industrialisation.

## Bhakra-Nangal and the Big Dams

The most famous project of this period was **Bhakra-Nangal**. Construction of the Bhakra Dam on the Sutlej river began in 1948; about 10 km below it is the Nangal Dam. This dam, about 226 metres high, was completed by the end of 1963 and dedicated to the nation in October 1963. It gave irrigation and power to large parts of Punjab, Haryana and Rajasthan, which later prepared the ground for the Green Revolution. The photograph above is from November 1953, when Nehru inspected the construction site.

The **Hirakud** project on the Mahanadi in Odisha and the **Damodar Valley** projects in Bihar-Bengal also advanced in this period. In this very plan period, on 2 October 1952, the **Community Development Programme** began, whose aim was the development of agriculture, health and education in villages.

## Achievements and Limits

The first plan had set a target of about **2.1%** annual growth, while the actual rate was about **3.6%**. Grain production rose, although good monsoons are also considered to have contributed greatly. Critics say that big dams displaced people and that questions such as land reform were left behind. Even so, the plan was considered a success, and with the confidence it gave, a big step towards heavy industries was taken in the second plan (1956–61).

## Significance

The First Five-Year Plan began the tradition of 'planned development' in independent India, which lasted more than six decades. Dams, canals, power stations and community development blocks — many institutions and structures of this period are still the backbone of the country's economy today.
