## The Indo-Greek Kingdom

After Alexander's return, the successors of his generals established a Greek kingdom in Bactria (today's northern Afghanistan). In the 2nd century BCE these Greek kings crossed the Hindu Kush and came as far as Gandhara and Punjab. They are called **Indo-Greeks**, and in Indian texts they are called **"Yavanas"**.

The most famous among them was **Menander I**, whom the Pali tradition calls **Milinda**. Scholars differ on the dates of his rule; it is generally placed at **about 165/155 to 130 BCE**.

## Kingdom and Capital

Menander's capital was **Sagala**, identified with **Sialkot** in present-day Pakistan. His kingdom is believed to have extended from the Kabul River in the west to the Ravi River in the east and to the Swat Valley in the north.

Some Indian sources, such as **Patanjali's Mahabhashya** and the *Yuga Purana*, mention the Yavanas reaching Mathura, Saketa and Pataliputra. These are often linked with Menander's campaigns, but his name does not appear in these texts, so the link is uncertain.

## The Milindapanha: A Book of Questions

The *Milindapanha* ("Questions of Milinda") is a **Pali** Buddhist text. It consists of a dialogue in the form of questions and answers between King Milinda and the Buddhist monk **Nagasena**. The king asks that if there is no permanent "soul", who is reborn, who experiences the fruit of karma, and what is nirvana.

Nagasena answers with everyday examples such as a chariot: just as a "chariot" is nothing apart from wheels, axle and frame, so a "person" is the name for various parts and experiences. In the text Milinda is described as a very learned and logical king.

The text developed gradually into its present form, and some of its parts are believed to have been added later. It has been held in great respect in the Theravada tradition of Sri Lanka, Myanmar and Thailand.

## Did Menander Become a Buddhist?

According to the Milindapanha, after the dialogue the king placed his faith in Buddhism. Historians view this claim cautiously, because it is the account of a religious text. Still, some evidence points to his connection with Buddhism: the **Shinkot casket** found at Bajaur carries an inscription about the enshrining of the Buddha's relics in the reign of "Maharaja Minedra", and the wheel on some of his copper coins is probably the Dharmachakra.

## Coins and Legacy

Menander issued the most coins among the Indo-Greek kings. They show the figure of the Greek goddess **Athena** and carry inscriptions in both Greek and Kharosthi scripts. This bilingual tradition shows that Greek and Indian subjects lived together in his kingdom.

Menander's story shows that ancient India was not cut off from the outside world. Here Greek rulers, Indian languages and Buddhist philosophy mixed, and from this mixing, traditions such as Gandhara art later flourished.
