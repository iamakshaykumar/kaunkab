## An Island in the Sea

About 10–11 kilometres east of the Gateway of India in Mumbai, inside Mumbai Harbour, is a small island that the local people call **Gharapuri**. The caves cut into the rocks in the hills of this island are called the **Elephanta Caves**.

In all seven caves are counted on the island: **five Hindu caves** on the western hill and **two Buddhist caves** on the eastern hill (the Stupa Hill). The Buddhist remains are believed to date from around the second century BCE. The most famous is **Cave 1**, called the "Great Cave". It is a temple of Shiva.

## Who Built Them, and When?

No inscription has been found at the Elephanta Caves that clearly says who built them. So scholars differ about their date. Most scholars consider them to be of the **fifth to sixth century CE**, and according to many the main cave was complete by about **550 CE**. Some estimates take them to the seventh century or later.

Many scholars today link them to the time of the **Kalachuri** king **Krishnaraja**. In the colonial period they were linked to the Rashtrakutas, but that view is now considered weak.

## The Great Cave and Sadashiva

The Great Cave is about 39 metres long and wide and looks like a vast pillared hall. Inside, in a separate sanctum, a **Shiva linga** is installed.

The most famous sculpture of the cave is the image of **Sadashiva** on the southern wall, commonly called the **Trimurti**. In this image, about 6–7 metres high, three faces of Shiva are seen:
- **The middle face (Tatpurusha)**: calm and serious, the sense of sustaining the world.
- **The left face (Aghora/Bhairava)**: the form of anger and destruction, with snakes in the hair.
- **The right face (Vamadeva/Uma)**: soft and gentle, the sense of creation.

Note that this is not the "Trimurti" of Brahma-Vishnu-Mahesh; these are different forms of Shiva alone. Scholars consider it a form of the five-faced Sadashiva of which three faces are visible from the front.

## Shiva's Stories on the Walls

On the walls of the Great Cave, stories connected with Shiva are carved in large panels:
- **Ardhanarishvara**: half Shiva, half Parvati.
- **Gangadhara**: Shiva receiving the Ganga descending from the sky in his matted locks.
- **Kalyanasundara**: the marriage of Shiva and Parvati.
- **Ravananugraha**: Ravana trying to lift Mount Kailasa.
- **The slaying of Andhakasura**, **Nataraja** and the meditating **Yogishvara** Shiva.

## The Portuguese Period and the Damage

In the sixteenth century this region came under Portuguese control. They found a large stone elephant on the island, because of which they began to call it **"Elephanta"**. Many sculptures are broken today. There is disagreement about who did this damage: some writers hold Portuguese soldiers responsible, while others also give earlier vandalism and long neglect as causes.

## World Heritage

In **1987** UNESCO declared the Elephanta Caves a **World Heritage Site**. Today they are maintained by the **Archaeological Survey of India**. Thousands of tourists reach here by boat from Mumbai every day. Despite the damage, the image of Sadashiva is counted among the most impressive works of Indian sculpture.
