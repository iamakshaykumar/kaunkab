## The Young Guru of Kiratpur

**Guru Har Rai** was born on **16 January 1630** at **Kiratpur Sahib** in Punjab. He was the grandson of the sixth Guru, **Guru Hargobind**; his father **Baba Gurditta** died when Har Rai was about eight. Guru Hargobind chose this gentle grandson as his successor rather than his own sons. On **3 March 1644**, after Guru Hargobind's death, Har Rai became the **seventh Sikh Guru** at just **fourteen years** of age.

In Guru Hargobind's time there had been several battles with the Mughals, and he had raised an armed Sikh force. Guru Har Rai kept this force, and mounted soldiers stayed with him, but he himself fought no war. Most of his time was spent preaching to the sangat in and around Kiratpur and the hill country, and on missionary tours. His wife was **Mata Kishan Kaur** (Sulakhni), and their children were **Ram Rai**, **Har Krishan** and a daughter, **Roop Kaur**.

## Compassion, Medicine and Nature

Sikh tradition remembers Guru Har Rai as a tender-hearted Guru who loved nature. At Kiratpur he had a garden, called the **Naulakha Bagh**, said to contain many medicinal plants. When he went hunting, instead of killing animals he captured them and kept them in his animal enclosure.

A well-known episode concerns the Mughal prince **Dara Shikoh**. It is said that when this eldest son of Shah Jahan fell gravely ill after being poisoned, the Guru sent rare herbs for his treatment. The Guru had good relations with Dara Shikoh, who was influenced by Sufi ideas. Sikh tradition also records that the Guru blessed a boy named **Phul**, whose descendants later became the rulers of the princely states of Patiala, Nabha and Jind.

## Ram Rai and Aurangzeb

**Aurangzeb** won the war of succession of **1658** and later had Dara Shikoh executed. Because of the Guru's ties with Dara, Aurangzeb summoned him to Delhi around **1660**. The Guru did not go himself; he sent his elder son **Ram Rai**, who remained at court more or less as a hostage.

At court an objection was raised to a line of the Adi Granth (Guru Granth Sahib), which was said to be against Muslims. To please the emperor, Ram Rai changed the words of that line and so altered its meaning. When this reached Guru Har Rai, he regarded it as an insult to Gurbani, broke off relations with Ram Rai and disinherited him. Ram Rai later settled in **Dehradun** under Mughal patronage.

Guru Har Rai died at Kiratpur on **6 October 1661**. He was only **31 years** old. Before his death he passed the guruship to his younger son, **Har Krishan**.

## The Child Guru Har Krishan

**Guru Har Krishan** was born at Kiratpur in **1656** (most sources give **7 July 1656**). At about **five years** of age he became the **eighth Sikh Guru** — the youngest Guru in Sikh history. For this reason he is also lovingly called the "child Guru".

Ram Rai did not accept his younger brother's guruship and asked Aurangzeb to intervene. The emperor had the boy Guru called to Delhi through **Mirza Raja Jai Singh** of Amber. In **1664** Guru Har Krishan reached Delhi and stayed at Raja Jai Singh's bungalow. According to the sources, his proposed meeting with the emperor did not take place.

## Delhi's Epidemic and 'Baba Bakale'

At that time diseases such as **smallpox** and cholera were spreading in Delhi. Sikh tradition says the boy Guru served the sick without discrimination, and water from the well (baoli) of Raja Jai Singh's bungalow was distributed among the people. During this service he himself caught smallpox, and on **30 March 1664** he died at only about **seven years** of age.

In his final moments he was asked who the next Guru would be. He said only **"Baba Bakale"** — meaning the next Guru is in the village of Bakala. For a while many people claimed to be the Guru, but finally the sangat recognised **Tegh Bahadur**, Guru Hargobind's son living in Bakala, who became the **ninth Guru**.

## Legacy

Raja Jai Singh's bungalow is today Delhi's famous **Gurdwara Bangla Sahib**, whose pool water devotees regard as holy. On the bank of the Yamuna where Guru Har Krishan was cremated stands **Gurdwara Bala Sahib**.

The period of these two Gurus was one of growing Mughal pressure, yet no major war was fought. While keeping an army, Guru Har Rai stressed peace, compassion and service, and through the Ram Rai episode he established the principle that the words of Gurbani cannot be changed to please any ruler. Guru Har Krishan's spirit of service at so young an age is still remembered in the Sikh Ardas: "Sri Har Krishan dhiaaiai, jis dithai sabh dukh jaae" — "meditate on Sri Har Krishan, at whose sight all sorrows depart."
