## Background: Broken Roads and Crowded Cities

By the end of the 1990s India's economy was growing fast, but roads and urban transport lagged far behind. Most of the country's national highways had only two lanes, on which trucks crawled along slowly. In the big cities buses were packed, and in a city like Delhi vehicle smoke and traffic jams had become a major problem. In these years the government of Prime Minister **Atal Bihari Vajpayee** made infrastructure a priority.

## The Golden Quadrilateral: Four Metros, One Highway

The first and best-known part of the **National Highways Development Project (NHDP)** was the **Golden Quadrilateral**. Its aim was to connect the country's four biggest metropolitan cities — **Delhi** (north), **Kolkata** (east), **Mumbai** (west) and **Chennai** (south) — with wide, modern highways. On the map these roads form the shape of a quadrilateral, which gave the project its name.

Vajpayee laid its foundation stone on **6 January 1999**, and construction began on a large scale from around **2001**. Responsibility for the project was given to the **National Highways Authority of India (NHAI)**. Work gathered special pace under Road Transport Minister **B. C. Khanduri**. To raise money, a cess was levied on petrol and diesel and a system of toll collection was set up. At the same time the North–South (Srinagar to Kanyakumari) and East–West (Silchar to Porbandar) corridors were also planned.

The Golden Quadrilateral is about **5,846 kilometres** long in total. These are **four- or six-lane** highways, on which flyovers, bypasses and railway overbridges were built. The project also suffered years of delay — from land acquisition, contractor problems and court disputes in some stretches. The government announced its completion in **January 2012**. Its final cost was put at about **₹32,500 crore**.

A tragic incident linked to this project is also remembered. **Satyendra Dubey**, a young NHAI engineer, had written to the Prime Minister's Office complaining of corruption in contracts in Bihar; in **November 2003** he was murdered in Gaya. After this incident, the demand for a law to protect **"whistleblowers"** (those who expose corruption) gained strength in the country.

## The Delhi Metro: A Railway Below and Above the Capital

A metro rail for Delhi had been discussed for several decades. On **3 May 1995** the **Delhi Metro Rail Corporation (DMRC)**, a joint company of the central and Delhi governments, was formed, and **E. Sreedharan**, the experienced engineer who had built the Konkan Railway, was made its Managing Director. Japan provided concessional loans for the first phase. Construction began on **1 October 1998**.

Sreedharan strictly enforced deadlines and budgets; countdown clocks hung in the offices so that everyone knew how many days were left before a deadline. On **24 December 2002** Prime Minister Vajpayee inaugurated the first line from **Shahdara to Tis Hazari** (about 8 kilometres, part of today's **Red Line**), and on **25 December** it opened to the public. In the very first days people showed huge enthusiasm to ride it.

After this the metro kept expanding. The first phase was completed ahead of schedule in 2005-06. Several new lines opened before the 2010 Commonwealth Games, and in **2011** the **Airport Express** to the airport began. By 2025 the Delhi Metro network had grown to about **374 kilometres** and **more than 270 stations**, and it carries more than 6 million passengers a day on average.

## Impact: Trade, Jobs and Cities

The Golden Quadrilateral made freight transport faster and cheaper. Trucks began reaching one metropolis from another in less time than before, and in small towns along the highways, factories, warehouses, dhabas and new businesses opened. Studies by economists found that industry grew more in districts near the highway. It also paved the way for later large road programmes such as Bharatmala.

The Delhi Metro showed that world-class, clean and punctual public transport could be built in India too. After its success, metro projects were built in **Bengaluru, Kolkata (which already had a metro since 1984), Mumbai, Chennai, Hyderabad, Lucknow, Jaipur** and many other cities, in many of which DMRC acted as a consultant.

## Legacy

The Golden Quadrilateral and the Delhi Metro are counted among the most successful Indian infrastructure projects of the early 21st century. They strengthened the idea that roads, railways and urban transport are the backbone of economic development. E. Sreedharan came to be called the "Metro Man" for his contribution and was honoured with the **Padma Vibhushan**. The expressways and metro networks being built across the country today stand on the foundation of these projects.
