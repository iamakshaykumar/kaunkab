## A Buddhist Centre on the Krishna

In Palnadu district of Andhra Pradesh, on the right bank of the Krishna river, lies the village of **Amaravati**. Close by is the ancient settlement of **Dharanikota**, believed to be ancient **Dhanyakataka** — a major political and trading centre of the Krishna valley. Outside this city a huge Buddhist stupa was built, which was locally called the "Mahachaitya".

The beginning of the stupa is placed in the **3rd century BCE**. Some scholars link it with the time of Ashoka, but there is no firm proof. After that, it continued to be expanded and decorated in several phases until about **250 CE**.

## Patrons: The Satavahanas, Sadas and Ikshvakus

Traditionally the flowering of Amaravati is linked to the **Satavahana** kings, who had a long rule over the Deccan and Andhra. Recent research has also stressed the role of the local **Sada** dynasty. In the 3rd and the early part of the 4th century CE this region was ruled by the **Andhra Ikshvaku** dynasty, in whose time great Buddhist monuments were also built at nearby **Nagarjunakonda**.

But Amaravati was not merely a monument of kings. The inscriptions carved on its slabs record donations by merchants, craftsmen, monks, nuns and ordinary householders.

## Structure and Art of the Stupa

At its peak the stupa was very large; the diameter of its final outer railing was about 192 feet (about 58 metres). The brick dome was faced with slabs of pale green-white **limestone**. These are often called the "Amaravati Marbles", but they are not marble.

The Amaravati style is counted among the three great styles of ancient Indian art — along with Gandhara and Mathura. Its characteristics:
- **Dynamic scenes** — scenes full of crowds, processions, dances and dramatic poses, in which the figures look supple and elongated.
- **Both forms of the Buddha** — in the early slabs the Buddha is shown through symbols (the Bodhi tree, an empty seat, footprints), while in the later period also in human form.
- **A picture of the stupa itself** — many "dome slabs" carry carvings of the stupa itself, which shows how it looked in its golden days.

The influence of this style is believed to have reached the Buddhist art of Sri Lanka and South-East Asia. In the seventh century the Chinese traveller **Xuanzang** also came to the Dhanyakataka region.

## The Story of Destruction

With time Buddhism weakened and the stupa turned into a mound of earth. In 1797 the British surveyor **Colin Mackenzie** saw the site. But when he returned in 1816, a large part of the stupa had already been destroyed by the excavations of the local landlord **Vasireddy Venkatadri Naidu**, and its stones had been used in houses nearby.

In 1845 the Madras official **Walter Elliot** had excavations carried out here and sent many sculptures to Madras — which is why they are also called the **"Elliot Marbles"**.

## From Madras to London

In the late 1850s many of these slabs were sent to London on the orders of the East India Company. There they were first kept in a warehouse and then at Fife House, where they also suffered some damage from the weather. When the India Museum closed in 1880, they reached the **British Museum**. It holds about 120 sculptures and inscriptions connected with Amaravati, many of which are displayed in a humidity-controlled gallery (Room 33a) opened in 1992.

Even so, the largest and richest collection of Amaravati sculptures is in the **Government Museum, Chennai**. At Amaravati itself there is also a museum of the Archaeological Survey of India at the site.

## Significance

The Amaravati stupa shows how great a centre of Buddhism and art South India was in ancient times. Its story also teaches us how a priceless heritage was scattered through negligence and colonial-era excavations — today its history is divided among museums in India and abroad.
