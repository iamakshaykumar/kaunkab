## Early Life

**Gopal Krishna Gokhale** was born on **9 May 1866** in the village of **Kotluk** in Ratnagiri district of the Bombay Presidency, in a humble Chitpavan Brahmin family. After his father's early death his studies continued with the help of his elder brother. After graduating in **1884** from **Elphinstone College**, Bombay, he joined the **Deccan Education Society** of Pune and for about two decades taught English, history and economics at **Fergusson College**.

## Ranade's Disciple

In Pune Gokhale met Justice **Mahadev Govind Ranade**, whom he regarded as his guru all his life. From Ranade he learnt the discipline of public life, reasoning based on facts and figures, and patience. He edited the journal of the 'Poona Sarvajanik Sabha' and, with Agarkar, the English section of the 'Sudharak'. In 1889 he joined Congress. His testimony in 1897 before the Welby Commission, which examined Indian expenditure, gave him national recognition.

## A Voice in the Legislative Council

Gokhale was a member first of the Bombay Legislative Council and then, from 1902, of the **Imperial (Central) Legislative Council**. His **budget speeches** became famous — using the government's own figures he showed that heavy spending on the army, high taxes and neglect of education were impoverishing India. In 1905 he became president of Congress at the **Benares session**. His advocacy is believed to have played a role in shaping the **Morley–Minto Reforms** of 1909.

In March 1911 he introduced a bill for **free and compulsory primary education**. In March 1912 the Council rejected it, but this debate made the demand for universal education a national agenda.

## The Servants of India Society (1905)

On **12 June 1905** Gokhale founded the **'Servants of India Society'** in Pune. Its members pledged to live simply, on low pay, and to devote their lives to national service — education, relief work, the uplift of backward classes and political education. People like Srinivasa Sastri and N. M. Joshi joined it. Gokhale believed that politics had to be 'spiritualised', that is, a spirit of selfless service had to be brought into it.

## Differences with Tilak and Gandhi's Guru

Gokhale and **Bal Gangadhar Tilak** both came from Pune and were once together in the Deccan Education Society, but in methods their paths differed. Tilak favoured mass movement and a sharp demand for 'swaraj', while Gokhale believed in gradual reform and dialogue with British liberals. On the Age of Consent Bill of 1891 too the two were in opposite camps. This tension burst out in the **Surat split** of 1907.

In the struggle of Indians in South Africa Gokhale supported **Gandhi** and went there in 1912. When Gandhi returned to India in 1915, Gokhale advised him to tour the country for a year and understand it before entering politics. In that same year, on **19 February 1915**, Gokhale died at the age of only 48. Gandhi regarded him as his political guru — this proved to be Gokhale's most lasting legacy.
