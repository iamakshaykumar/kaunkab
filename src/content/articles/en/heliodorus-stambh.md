## A Greek Who Became a Bhagavata

About a hundred and twenty-five years before the Common Era, North-West India was ruled by **Indo-Greek** kings, while the Vidisha region of central India was ruled by kings linked to the **Shunga** dynasty. A link between these two worlds was **Heliodorus**. He was the son of **Dion** and a resident of **Taxila**. The Indo-Greek king **Antialcidas** made him his ambassador and sent him to the court of **Kashiputra Bhagabhadra**, king of Vidisha.

While living in Vidisha, Heliodorus had a stone pillar raised in about **113 BCE** in honour of **Vasudeva** (Krishna). This pillar stands today at the ancient city of **Besnagar** near Vidisha and is called the **Heliodorus Pillar**. The date is not written directly in the inscription; it is worked out from estimates of the reign of King Antialcidas. Scholars themselves differ on the dates of Antialcidas's rule (some say about 130–120 BCE, others 115–95 BCE), so 113 BCE is an approximate date.

## What the Inscription Says

The pillar has two short inscriptions in **Brahmi script** and the Prakrit language. According to the first inscription:

- This is the **Garuda standard** of **Vasudeva**, the **god of gods** (*devadeva*).
- It was made by the **Bhagavata** Heliodorus, son of Dion and resident of Taxila.
- He had come as ambassador from the great king **Antialcidas** to **Kashiputra Bhagabhadra**, who was then in the **fourteenth year** of his reign.

The second inscription names three **immortal steps** — **dama** (self-restraint), **tyaga** (charity) and **apramada** (vigilance) — which lead to heaven. Scholars link this idea to teachings found in the Mahabharata; translations render the words with slightly different meanings.

## The Build of the Pillar and the Temple

This pillar is not like Ashoka's smooth, polished pillars. Its height is about **6.5 metres** (nearly seventeen and a half feet) and it stands on a square platform about 12 feet wide. The lower part is **eight-sided**, above it a **sixteen-sided** part and then a **thirty-two-sided** part, and on the round section at the top is a bell-shaped capital. Above this there was once an image of **Garuda**, which is no longer there.

In **1913–15** **D. R. Bhandarkar** excavated here, and in the excavations of **M. D. Khare** in **1963–65** the foundation of a **Vasudeva temple** was found nearby. It is believed that there were once eight such pillars in front of the temple; of them only Heliodorus's pillar stands today.

## Khamb Baba

Local people call this pillar **Khamb Baba** (or Khamba Baba) — that is, "the Baba of the pillar". People of the area kept worshipping it like a deity and it kept being smeared with vermilion. **Alexander Cunningham** surveyed it in the 1870s (between 1874 and 1877), but he did not see the inscription hidden under the coating. When the coating was removed in **1909–10** the inscription came to light, and it was then learnt that this was a pillar built by a Greek ambassador. Today it is protected by the **Archaeological Survey of India**.

## Importance in History

The Heliodorus Pillar is unique for several reasons:

- It is one of the oldest archaeological proofs of the **devotion to Vasudeva-Krishna** (Bhagavata dharma). It shows that as early as the 2nd century BCE Vasudeva was being worshipped as the "god of gods".
- It shows that **people of Greek origin too were adopting Indian religion** — a foreign ambassador proudly calls himself a 'Bhagavata'.
- It is a rare written proof of **diplomatic relations** between the Indo-Greek kingdoms and the kings of central India.

Who King Kashiputra Bhagabhadra was is disputed. Many historians consider him a king of the Shunga dynasty, but his name does not directly match the Puranic Shunga genealogy, so the identification is not certain.
