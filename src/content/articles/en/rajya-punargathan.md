## Language and States: An Old Promise

The provinces of British India were formed for administrative convenience and by the order of conquest, not by language. In the Madras Presidency Tamil, Telugu, Kannada and Malayalam speakers were together; in the Bombay Presidency, Marathi, Gujarati and Kannada. During the national movement, after its Nagpur session of 1920 the Congress formed its provincial committees on the basis of language and also promised linguistic provinces.

After independence and Partition the attitude of the leadership changed. Anxiety about the unity of the country was deep. The **Linguistic Provinces Commission (Dar Commission)** formed in 1948 refused to make language the main basis for forming states, and in 1949 the committee of Nehru, Patel and Pattabhi Sitaramayya (the **JVP Committee**) also advised putting this demand off for the time being.

## Potti Sreeramulu's Fast

The demand for a separate **Andhra State** in the Telugu-speaking areas of Madras State was decades old. The Gandhian worker **Potti Sreeramulu** (born 1901), who had lived at the Sabarmati Ashram and had gone to jail in the Salt Satyagraha, began a fast unto death at Madras on **19 October 1952**.

At first the government did not take it seriously. On about the **58th day** of the fast, on the night of **15 December 1952**, Sreeramulu died (some sources give the date as 16 December). As soon as the news of his death spread, there were widespread demonstrations, violence and police firing in many cities of Madras and Andhra, in which many people were killed.

## Andhra State (1953)

Seeing the situation worsen, Prime Minister **Nehru** announced the creation of a separate Andhra State on **19 December 1952**. On **1 October 1953** the Telugu-speaking districts of Madras State were separated to form **Andhra State**, with **Kurnool** as its capital and **T. Prakasam** as the first chief minister. Andhra's claim to the city of Madras was not accepted. This was the first linguistic state of independent India.

## The States Reorganisation Commission

As soon as Andhra was formed, the demands of other linguistic groups also grew sharper. In December 1953 the Centre set up the **States Reorganisation Commission** (SRC), whose chairman was **Fazl Ali** and members **Hridayanath Kunzru** and **K. M. Panikkar**. The Commission took opinions from across the country and submitted its report on **30 September 1955**. It treated language as an important basis, but also said that national unity, administrative convenience and economic viability had to be kept in mind — that is, 'one language, one state' should not be applied blindly.

## The States Reorganisation Act, 1956

On the basis of the report Parliament passed the **States Reorganisation Act, 1956**, which came into force on **1 November 1956**. Along with it the **Seventh Constitutional Amendment** abolished the categories of Part A, B, C and D states. The country now had **14 states** and **6 union territories**.

- The Telangana region of Hyderabad State was merged with Andhra to form **Andhra Pradesh**, with Hyderabad as the capital.
- **Kerala** was formed by joining Travancore-Cochin, the Malabar district and Kasaragod.
- By adding Kannada-speaking areas the state of **Mysore** (today's Karnataka) was expanded.
- Saurashtra, Kutch and Marathi-speaking areas were added to **Bombay** state, so that it remained a Marathi-Gujarati bilingual state.

## The Story Afterwards and Assessment

Bombay's bilingual experiment could not last. After the Samyukta Maharashtra and Mahagujarat movements, Maharashtra and Gujarat were separated on **1 May 1960**; in 1966 Punjab too was reorganised on a linguistic basis and Haryana was formed.

At that time many leaders feared that linguistic states would break up the country. But most historians believe that by recognising linguistic identity, regional discontent could be resolved within the democratic framework and the Union became stronger. Even so, border disputes (such as Belgaum) and questions of linguistic minorities remain to this day.
