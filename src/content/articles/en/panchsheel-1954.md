## Background: New India, New China

India became independent in 1947 and on 1 October 1949 a Communist Party government was formed in China — the **People's Republic of China**. India was among the first countries to recognise it. In 1950–51 China established control over **Tibet**. From British times India had trading agencies, post and telegraph posts and some special rights in Tibet. In the new situation it became necessary to change these old arrangements.

For this very purpose, talks went on between the delegations of India and China from **December 1953** to **April 1954**. According to an account by an Indian diplomat, on 31 December 1953, while welcoming the Indian delegation, the Chinese Premier **Zhou Enlai** put these five principles forward publicly.

## The Agreement of 29 April 1954

On **29 April 1954** the agreement was signed in **Peking** (today's Beijing). Its official name was **"Agreement on Trade and Intercourse between the Tibet Region of China and India"**. On behalf of India Ambassador **N. Raghavan** and on behalf of China Deputy Foreign Minister **Chang Han-fu** signed. In the preamble of the agreement these **five principles** were recorded:

1. Mutual respect for each other's territorial integrity and sovereignty
2. Mutual non-aggression
3. Non-interference in each other's internal affairs
4. Equality and mutual benefit
5. Peaceful coexistence

These were called **"Panchsheel"** in India.

## What Was in the Agreement

The articles of the agreement were mainly about trade and pilgrimage. China received permission to open trade agencies at **New Delhi, Calcutta and Kalimpong**, and India at **Yatung, Gyantse and Gartok** in Tibet. Provisions were made for pilgrims of both countries — such as Indians going to Kailash-Mansarovar — and routes through the passes of **Shipki La, Mana, Niti, Kungri Bingri, Darma and Lipulekh** were fixed for traders and travellers. According to Article VI the agreement was to remain in force for **eight years**.

After this agreement India gave up its old privileges in Tibet. According to the author Bertil Lintner, in it for the first time Tibet was officially called the **"Tibet Region of China"**. It is worth noting that the agreement made **no mention of the border** — this became the root of later dispute.

## 'Hindi-Chini Bhai-Bhai'

At the end of **June 1954** Zhou Enlai came to India and in a joint statement with Nehru these principles were declared the basis of relations between the two countries. In the same year Nehru also visited China. In this period the slogan **"Hindi-Chini Bhai-Bhai"** became very popular.

Panchsheel was not limited to India and China. China and Burma (Myanmar) also adopted them, and in **April 1955** they were included in a modified form in the ten principles of the Asian-African Conference at **Bandung** (Indonesia). Later these ideas also became part of the thinking of the **Non-Aligned Movement**. Nehru had said that if all countries followed these principles, there would hardly be any conflict and certainly no war.

## Criticism and End

Even at that time not everyone agreed with this agreement. In the Rajya Sabha, **Dr. Ambedkar** said that Panchsheel is part of Buddhism, and if Mao had had even a little faith in it he would have treated the Buddhists of his own country differently. **Acharya Kripalani** in 1958 described it as resting on the destruction of Tibet.

After 1959 the border dispute and tensions kept growing. On completion of eight years the agreement expired in **June 1962** and was not renewed. A few months later, in **October 1962**, the India-China War broke out. Later many commentators called Panchsheel Nehru's "diplomatic blunder", while others believe that in the conditions of that time it was an honest attempt to preserve peace. Even today China repeatedly mentions these **"Five Principles of Peaceful Coexistence"** in its foreign policy.
