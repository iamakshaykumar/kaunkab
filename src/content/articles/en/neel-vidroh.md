## Background: Indigo and the Trap of 'Dadan'

From the end of the eighteenth century there was heavy demand in Europe for indigo to dye cloth, and Bengal became a major centre of indigo. European **planters (indigo planters)** controlled the farming of the entire area from their 'kothis' (factories).

Peasants were made to sign contracts after being given an advance sum called **dadan**, in return for which they had to grow indigo on their best land. The price of indigo was fixed so low that the peasant could never get out of debt, and this debt went on from generation to generation. If they refused, locking peasants up in the kothis, beating them, destroying crops and burning houses were common. Many British magistrates in the districts also sided with the planters.

## The Beginning of the Revolt

In 1859, in the village of **Chaugachha** in Nadia district and the surrounding area, the peasants decided that they would no longer sow indigo. This resistance was led by local peasants **Digambar Biswas** and **Bishnucharan Biswas**.

The movement soon spread to districts such as Jessore, Pabna, Murshidabad, Birbhum, Burdwan and Khulna. The peasants collectively stopped indigo cultivation, faced the planters' club-wielding men with sticks and spears, and socially boycotted those who worked for them. In many places the peasants also filed court cases against the planters. Historians regard this as an early example of disciplined and organised peasant resistance.

## Support of Intellectuals and Newspapers

A special feature of this revolt was that the educated middle class of Bengal openly supported the peasants. The journalist **Harish Chandra Mukherjee**, in his newspaper the **'Hindoo Patriot'**, continuously exposed the atrocities committed on the peasants. Some Christian missionaries also raised their voice in favour of the peasants.

Seeing the growing movement, the government in 1860 formed the **Indigo Commission**. The commission accepted that the system of indigo cultivation was oppressive. After this the government declared that peasants could not be forced to grow indigo and that disputes would be settled by legal means. Gradually indigo cultivation almost ended in this part of Bengal, and the planters moved towards regions such as Bihar.

## 'Nil Darpan' and the Trial of James Long

**Dinabandhu Mitra** wrote the Bengali play **'Nil Darpan'** in 1858–59, which was published from Dhaka in 1860 without the author's name. It gave a moving picture of the oppression of a peasant family by the indigo planters.

In 1861 **Reverend James Long** had an English translation of it published. The credit for the translation is generally given to the poet **Michael Madhusudan Dutt**, although there is no full agreement on this. The planters brought a libel suit against Long; in July 1861 the Calcutta Supreme Court found him guilty and sentenced him to a fine of 1,000 rupees and imprisonment. According to popular accounts, this fine was paid by the writer Kaliprasanna Singha.

## Historical Importance

The Indigo Revolt showed that even scattered peasants could unite and make a powerful system bend. It created a new relationship among peasants, journalists and writers, which later became the foundation of national consciousness. Right up to the Champaran Satyagraha (1917), the question of indigo kept returning to Indian politics.
