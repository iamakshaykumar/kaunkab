## Founding and the Capital

In the 14th century several political powers were rising in the Deccan and South India. Traditional accounts link the founding of the Vijayanagara kingdom in 1336 with the brothers **Harihara** and **Bukka**. Its name came from the capital **Vijayanagara**, meaning "the city of victory". The capital was settled near the Tungabhadra River; today its remains are known as Hampi.

## Empire and Governance

The influence of the Vijayanagara rulers spread at different times over a large part of South India. Local chiefs and military officers had a role in the empire. They are often called **nayakas**; they kept armies and collected revenue from the territories under them. This system and its forms kept changing over time.

The empire became powerful in the time of **Krishnadevaraya** (reigned c. 1509–1529). Under his rule, besides wars, irrigation, temples and literature also received patronage. The Telugu work *Amuktamalyada* is traditionally dedicated to him.

## City, Trade and Art

Hampi was not only a city of royal palaces. Around its temples there were markets, residential areas, fortifications and arrangements for bringing water. The accounts of travellers and archaeological remains give hints of the city's prosperity and its diversity. The trade in horses, spices, cloth and other goods was linked to regional and sea routes.

The tall gopurams of the temples, stone mandapas and decorative carving became the hallmarks of Vijayanagara-period architecture. The monuments of Hampi are today important evidence of this urban and religious life.

## Talikota and the Later Period

In 1565 the army of Vijayanagara fought near Talikota against an alliance of the Deccan sultans. After the defeat the capital was plundered and damaged on a large scale. Royal power did not end at once; later the rulers of Vijayanagara continued to rule from other capitals, but the old city never regained its political importance. The last phase of the empire lasted several decades, and its borders kept changing.

## Why Hampi Matters Today

In the ruins of Hampi, from temples to water structures and markets, many aspects of a great medieval city are recorded. UNESCO included the group of monuments here in the World Heritage list in 1986. These remains help in understanding the history, art and town planning of South India.
