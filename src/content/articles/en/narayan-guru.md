## Kerala of the Caste Walls

Nineteenth-century Kerala — especially the princely state of **Travancore** — was known for some of the harshest forms of caste discrimination. Many castes were barred not only from entering temples but even from walking on the roads near temples. Along with "untouchability" there was also the notion of being "polluted by distance". In such a society, large communities such as the **Ezhavas** were kept away from education, government jobs and places of worship.

**Sree Narayana Guru** was born on **20 August 1856** in the village of **Chempazhanthy** near Thiruvananthapuram, in an Ezhava family. His childhood name was **Nanu**; his father was Madan Asan and his mother Kuttiyamma, and the family was connected with Ayurvedic medicine. He studied Sanskrit, Vedanta and yoga deeply and for years wandered and practised spiritual discipline like an ascetic.

## The Night at Aruvippuram (1888)

On the night of **Shivaratri** in **1888**, the Guru reached **Aruvippuram**, where the **Neyyar river** forms a whirlpool. It is said that at midnight he dived into the river and took out a stone resembling a Shivalinga from the whirlpool spot ("Shankaran Kuzhi"). After deep meditation, at about three in the morning, he consecrated it as **Shiva** in a temporary shrine. (The exact date of Shivaratri — February or March 1888 — differs slightly among sources.)

At that time the right to consecrate idols was considered to belong only to Brahmins. So this was a religious act and at the same time a direct challenge to the caste system. When the orthodox side objected, the Guru's reply became famous — "This is not a Brahmin Shiva, it is an Ezhava Shiva." In some accounts his words are given as "We have consecrated our own Shiva." Whatever the words may have been, the message was clear: no single caste has a monopoly over God.

## "One Caste, One Religion, One God"

The Guru's most famous message is **"Oru jathi, oru matham, oru daivam, manushyanu"**, that is, **"One caste, one religion, one God for man."** He meant that all human beings belong to one "caste" — the human race. His disciple **Sahodaran Ayyappan** later gave this a still more radical form, declaring caste, religion and God — all three — unnecessary for man. This shows that even among the Guru's followers there were different interpretations of his ideas.

## SNDP, Education and Temples

The Guru's emphasis was not only on worship but on **education, organisation and enterprise**. On **15 May 1903**, through the efforts of **Dr. Padmanabhan Palpu**, the **Sree Narayana Dharma Paripalana (SNDP) Yogam** was formed, with the Guru as its founding president. The famous Malayalam poet **Kumaran Asan** was also his disciple.

In **1904** the Guru came to **Sivagiri (Varkala)** and opened a school for children of the backward classes; the **Sarada Matha** temple here was built in **1912**. He established many temples in Kerala and Tamil Nadu, which were open to all. Later, in several places, he also installed a **mirror** instead of an idol, or a plaque inscribed "Truth, Dharma, Compassion, Love".

At the Advaita Ashram in **Aluva (Alwaye)** he held an **All-Religions Conference** — sources give the year as 1923 or 1924. About 45 of his works exist in Malayalam, Sanskrit and Tamil, of which **'Atmopadesa Satakam'** (100 verses) and the universal prayer **'Daiva Dasakam'** (10 verses) are the most famous.

## Tagore, Gandhi and Legacy

In **November 1922**, **Rabindranath Tagore** met him at Sivagiri. On **12 March 1925**, during the **Vaikom Satyagraha**, **Mahatma Gandhi** also came to Sivagiri and spoke with him on the caste question. The Guru died at Sivagiri on **20 September 1928**.

Today Narayana Guru is remembered in many forms — some regard him as a saint and Advaita philosopher, others as a social reformer and sharp critic of the caste system and Brahminical dominance. Different political currents claim his legacy and there is debate over it too. But there is near agreement that from that night at Aruvippuram a long movement for social equality began in Kerala, whose echo was heard up to the Vaikom Satyagraha and the Temple Entry Proclamation of 1936.
