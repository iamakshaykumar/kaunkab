## The Beginning: TCS and the First Steps

The computer services business in India began in **1968**, when the Tata group set up a unit called **Tata Computer Systems**, which later came to be known as **Tata Consultancy Services (TCS)**. The group's chairman was **J. R. D. Tata**, and the company was led from 1968 to 1996 by **F. C. Kohli**, who is regarded as one of the fathers of the Indian software industry. The early work was modest — such as punched-card data work for Tata Steel (then TISCO) and accounting systems for banks.

In the 1970s India's economy was very closed. Importing computers was difficult and expensive, and foreign exchange was tightly controlled. Even so, in 1973 an export zone called **SEEPZ** was set up in Mumbai, and TCS began software work for overseas clients together with the American company **Burroughs**. At that time Indian programmers often travelled abroad to work in the client's office.

## The 1980s: Infosys, Wipro and Bengaluru

On **2 July 1981**, in **Pune**, seven young engineers — **N. R. Narayana Murthy**, **Nandan Nilekani**, Kris Gopalakrishnan, S. D. Shibulal, K. Dinesh, N. S. Raghavan and Ashok Arora — founded **Infosys** with very little capital. In 1983 the company moved to **Bengaluru**. At about the same time **Azim Premji's** **Wipro**, which had earlier traded in vegetable oil, entered the field of computers and software.

Bengaluru had many advantages: a pleasant climate, the **Indian Institute of Science (IISc)**, government science and defence institutions such as ISRO, HAL and BEL, and many engineering colleges. In the late 1970s an industrial area called **Electronics City** was also developed outside the city. In **1985** the American company **Texas Instruments** opened a software design centre in Bengaluru and connected directly to America by satellite link. This strengthened the idea that work for foreign clients could be done while sitting in India. In 1988 the industry body **NASSCOM** was also formed.

## 1991: STPI and the Economic Reforms

The real turning point came in **1991**. That year the central government's Department of Electronics set up **Software Technology Parks of India (STPI)**. Under it, software companies that exported received facilities such as high-speed **data communication (satellite links)**, government approvals in one place, income-tax exemptions and duty-free import of equipment. This was a huge help for small companies, which could not build expensive satellite links on their own.

The **economic liberalisation** that began the same year also eased the way — permission for foreign investment grew, imports became cheaper and the rules for rupee transactions were relaxed. In 1993 Infosys made its IPO on the stock market. Work for foreign clients was now done from India itself, which came to be called the **"offshore" model** — work that America handed over during its day was completed overnight in India.

## Y2K: A Problem and an Opportunity

In the late 1990s the world faced the **Y2K** or **"millennium bug"** problem. Old computer programs wrote the year with only two digits (such as "99"), so there was a fear that on 1 January 2000 computers would read "00" as 1900 and malfunction. Checking and fixing these old programs needed a very large number of programmers, especially those who knew old languages such as **COBOL**.

India had a large number of English-speaking engineers who worked at relatively low cost. According to NASSCOM, in 1998-99 about one-fifth of India's software exports was Y2K-related work. By the time the year 2000 arrived without major disruption, the world's big companies had seen what Indian companies could do and went on to give them large contracts. In **1999** Infosys became the first Indian company to be listed on America's **NASDAQ**. Around the same time the government created a **Ministry of Information Technology**, and the **Information Technology Act, 2000** was passed.

## Bengaluru: India's Silicon Valley

In the 2000s Bengaluru came to be counted among the world's leading technology hubs and was called **"India's Silicon Valley"**. Hyderabad, Pune, Chennai, Noida-Gurgaon and Kolkata also became major IT centres. Multinational companies opened their research centres here, and later startups such as Flipkart also emerged from here.

## Impact and Legacy

The IT revolution changed both India's economy and its society. The IT sector's share of India's gross domestic product has grown from about 1.2% in 1998 to more than about 7% today, and the industry directly employs millions of people. Engineering graduates from small towns found good jobs, and a new middle class emerged. India's image in the world changed from a "land of snake charmers" to a "software superpower".

Challenges came with it too — urban problems such as traffic, water shortages and expensive housing grew in Bengaluru, and critics say the industry has relied mainly on services and built few products of its own. Even so, this story shows how education, entrepreneurship and the right government policies can together change a country's direction.
