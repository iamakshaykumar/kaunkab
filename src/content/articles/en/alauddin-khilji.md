## Background: From Kara to the Throne of Delhi

Alauddin Khilji was the nephew and son-in-law of Sultan Jalaluddin Khilji, the founder of the Khilji dynasty. Jalaluddin made him governor of Kara (in today's Uttar Pradesh). In 1296 Alauddin made a successful raid on **Devagiri**, the capital of the Yadava kingdom of the Deccan, and returned with huge wealth.

In the same year Jalaluddin was murdered at Kara, and Alauddin reached Delhi and declared himself Sultan. This way of gaining power was violent, and his whole reign bore the stamp of harshness and a deep suspicion of conspiracies.

## Defence Against the Mongol Invasions

In the thirteenth and fourteenth centuries the Mongols of Central Asia (especially the Chagatai Khanate) were repeatedly attacking north-western India. Several major Mongol invasions took place during Alauddin's reign:
- Around 1299 the army of Qutlugh Khwaja came as far as **Kili**, close to Delhi. The Sultanate's army pushed it back, but the general **Zafar Khan** was killed in this battle.
- In 1303 the Mongols besieged Delhi; the Sultan defended firmly from his camp at Siri.
- In 1305 (Amroha) and 1306 (on the banks of the Ravi) the Sultanate's armies defeated the Mongols decisively.

After this the border forts were repaired, the standing army was increased and the Mongol threat subsided to a great extent.

## Market Reforms: An Experiment in Price Fixing

To maintain a large standing army on low pay, it was necessary that things in the capital stay cheap. According to the historian **Ziauddin Barani**, Alauddin introduced extensive price controls in Delhi:
- The government fixed the prices of grain, cloth, horses, cattle and even the slave market.
- Officials such as the **Shahna-i-Mandi** and spies were appointed to supervise the markets; giving short weight brought severe punishment.
- Revenue from the Doab region was collected in grain and stored in government granaries, so that prices would not rise even in a famine.
- Land revenue was fixed at about half of the produce, and emphasis was laid on the measurement of land.

These measures gave soldiers and townspeople cheap goods, but placed a heavy burden on peasants and traders. Many historians believe that this system was mainly limited to Delhi and collapsed soon after the Sultan's death.

## Campaigns in the North and the South

Alauddin's armies took **Gujarat** around 1299, **Ranthambore** in 1301 and **Chittor** in 1303; Malwa also came into the Sultanate. Later the general **Malik Kafur** led campaigns in the south — to Devagiri (1307–08), against the Kakatiyas of **Warangal** (1309–10), the Hoysalas of Dwarasamudra and as far as Madurai (1310–11).

Rather than annexing most of the southern kingdoms directly into the Sultanate, heavy tribute and submission were extracted from them. The legend of Rani Padmini connected with the Chittor campaign became popular through the later work *Padmavat* (1540); it is not mentioned in contemporary histories, so historians regard it as a literary tradition.

## Architecture, Final Years and Assessment

Alauddin founded the city of Siri, had the Hauz-i-Khas (Hauz-i-Alai) reservoir built, and had the **Alai Darwaza** (1311) constructed in the Qutb complex. The court poet **Amir Khusrau** was the leading writer of his time.

In his last years the Sultan was ill and Malik Kafur's influence at court grew very great. After his death in January 1316 the Khilji dynasty came to an end within a few years.

Historians regard Alauddin as a harsh, power-centred but effective ruler. The defence of north India against the Mongols and a centralised administration were his great achievements, while heavy taxes, spying and repression were the bitter side of his system.
