## A Young Fighter Pilot

**Rakesh Sharma** was born on **13 January 1949** in **Patiala**, Punjab. He went to school in Hyderabad. In 1966 he joined the **National Defence Academy (NDA)**, and in 1970 he was commissioned as a pilot in the **Indian Air Force**. He soon showed his ability: in the **1971 India–Pakistan war** he flew 21 combat sorties in the **MiG-21**. He later also became a skilled **test pilot**.

## Interkosmos: An India–Soviet Partnership

In the 1970s and 1980s the **Soviet Union** ran a programme called **Interkosmos**, under which citizens of friendly countries were sent into space aboard Soviet spacecraft. India and the Soviet Union already cooperated well in space — India's first satellite, **Aryabhata** (1975), had also been launched on a Soviet rocket. Under this cooperation it was decided to send an Indian into space.

After rigorous testing of many Air Force pilots, two names were chosen on **20 September 1982**: **Squadron Leader Rakesh Sharma** and **Wing Commander Ravish Malhotra**. Malhotra was made the backup astronaut. Both underwent tough training for about one and a half to two years at **Star City** near Moscow (the Yuri Gagarin training centre). They had to learn Russian, practise for weightlessness, and test their endurance by living for long periods in closed chambers.

## 3 April 1984: The Flight of Soyuz T-11

On **3 April 1984**, a **Soyuz-U** rocket lifted the **Soyuz T-11** spacecraft into space from the Soviet Union's **Baikonur** cosmodrome (today in Kazakhstan). It carried three people: commander **Yury Malyshev**, flight engineer **Gennady Strekalov** and research cosmonaut **Rakesh Sharma**. With this, India became the 14th country in the world to send its citizen into space.

The next day the craft docked with the **Salyut 7** space station, where three Soviet crew members — Leonid Kizim, Vladimir Solovyov and Oleg Atkov — were already living. So for a few days six people lived together on the station.

## Experiments in Space

On Salyut 7, Rakesh Sharma carried out several scientific experiments:

- **Remote sensing** — he photographed various parts of India from space, so that forests, water sources and Himalayan glaciers could be studied.
- **Biomedical experiments** — tests of how weightlessness affects the body.
- **Yoga** — he performed yoga postures in space to see whether yoga could help with the problems caused by weightlessness.
- **Materials science** — experiments in melting materials such as silicon in weightlessness.

He is said to have taken Indian food with him, prepared by the Defence Food Research Laboratory in Mysore.

## "Saare Jahan Se Achha"

The most memorable moment of the flight was a live conversation from space with Prime Minister **Indira Gandhi**, which the whole country watched and heard on television and radio. Indira Gandhi asked how India looked from up there. Rakesh Sharma answered without hesitation: **"Saare Jahan Se Achha"** (better than the whole world). This was a line from **Muhammad Iqbal's** famous song "Saare Jahan Se Achha Hindostan Hamara". The answer won the hearts of millions of Indians.

After spending about **7 days, 21 hours and 40 minutes** in space, the three crew members landed safely on the plains of Kazakhstan on **11 April 1984** aboard the **Soyuz T-10** spacecraft. (Soyuz T-11 was left at the station for the resident crew.)

## Honours and Legacy

On his return, Rakesh Sharma was welcomed as a national hero. The Soviet Union gave him the title **"Hero of the Soviet Union"**, and India honoured him with the **Ashoka Chakra**; Malyshev and Strekalov also received the Ashoka Chakra. He later retired with the rank of **Wing Commander**, and in 1987 became chief test pilot at **Hindustan Aeronautics Limited (HAL)**, where he contributed to the testing of indigenous aircraft.

This flight was not just one person's achievement. It showed that India could reach big scientific goals through international cooperation, and the pictures of India taken from space raised interest in the country's growing work in remote sensing. Rakesh Sharma's flight inspired millions of Indian children to dream of science and space. For about four decades he remained the only Indian citizen to have gone into space. His experience was also valued in the preparations for **Gaganyaan**, India's human spaceflight programme. His "Saare Jahan Se Achha" remains the best-loved symbol of India's journey into space.
