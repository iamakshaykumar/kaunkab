## The Merchant Srinivasa Nayaka

Purandaradasa's original name was **Srinivasa Nayaka**. He was born into the family of a rich merchant dealing in gold, silver and gems. His date of birth is not certain — different scholars give **1470, 1480, 1484 or 1494**; **1484** is the most commonly used. The manuscripts of the Tirumala Tirupati Devasthanams mention a meeting with the Telugu poet-composer **Annamacharya**, on the basis of which some scholars place his birth at **about 1470**. His age at death is said to be over 90 years, which fits this earlier date.

There has also been a long debate over his birthplace. Earlier, Purandargad near Pune was considered his birthplace, and then claims came from some places in Karnataka. In **2018** a committee of the Karnataka government considered **Arag**, near **Thirthahalli** in Shivamogga district, the most probable place. His wife's name is given as **Saraswati Bai**.

## Renunciation and Initiation

It is said that Srinivasa Nayaka was very miserly and that one incident changed his life. In the popular story a poor Brahmin asks him for help, his wife donates her nose-ring, and that very nose-ring comes to the merchant to be pawned. After this he is seized by detachment. This story belongs to the bhakti tradition; it is not confirmed historically.

What is certain is that he gave up his wealth and, at about the age of **30**, took **Haridasa** initiation at **Hampi** (the capital of Vijayanagara) from the Madhva saint **Vyasatirtha** (Vyasaraya). Vyasatirtha (1447–1539) was a great scholar and a music-loving saint of Vijayanagara, who joined the Haridasas into an organised tradition called the 'Dasakuta'. After initiation Srinivasa Nayaka came to be called "Purandaradasa".

## The Haridasa Movement

The **Haridasas** were devotees who travelled from village to village singing songs of devotion to Vishnu (Vitthala) in the **Kannada** language. Singing in the language of the people instead of Sanskrit carried devotion to ordinary folk. Names in this tradition include **Sripadaraya**, **Vyasatirtha**, **Vadiraja** and **Kanakadasa**; Kanakadasa was a contemporary of Purandaradasa and a fellow disciple.

Purandaradasa composed in forms such as the **kirtana**, **suladi** and **ugabhoga**. His songs, along with devotion, also comment on society — they satirise showy ritualism, greed and hypocrisy. In his compositions the name "Purandara Vitthala" appears like a signature.

## The 'Father' of Carnatic Music

Purandaradasa's greatest contribution is the **method of teaching** music. Before him there was no single graded framework for music education. He is believed to have:

- made the **Mayamalavagowla** raga the basis of early practice;
- created exercises moving from simple to difficult, such as the **sarali varisai** (swaravali) and **janti varisai**;
- composed **alankaras** to teach tala;
- given the **pillari geethe** as beginners' songs.

These exercises are still the first lessons of every Carnatic music student today. For this reason he is called the **"Father of Carnatic music"**. It should be noted that some of these contributions have been attached to his name by tradition; historians believe that other saints of the Haridasa tradition must also have played a role in building this framework.

## His Last Days and Memory

Purandaradasa died on **2 January 1564** in Vijayanagara — at about the time when Vijayanagara was in the last phase of its splendour, just before the Battle of Talikota (1565). Only a few of his compositions were preserved in manuscripts; much was carried forward by oral tradition, so scholars differ over the authenticity of some songs.

On **14 January 1964**, nearly four hundred years after his death, the Indian postal department issued a stamp on him. The Purandaradasa Mantapa at Hampi and the music festivals held across Karnataka keep his memory fresh even today.
