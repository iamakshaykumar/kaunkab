## What the Policy Was

Under the East India Company in India there were many **dependent princely states**, which relied on the Company for their security and foreign policy. In Indian tradition a childless king could adopt a boy and make him his successor. The Company imposed a condition on this: in such a state an adopted heir needed the Company's approval for the throne, and if approval was not given the state would "lapse" and pass into the Company's control. This is called the **Doctrine of Lapse** in English and **Hadap Niti** ("policy of seizure") in Hindi.

## Before Dalhousie

The idea was not entirely new. The Company's Court of Directors had put it forward as a principle in the 1830s, and even earlier some small states had been taken on this same argument — such as **Kittur in 1824**, where Rani Chennamma resisted it. But Lord **Dalhousie**, Governor-General from **1848 to 1856**, enforced it in the most systematic and aggressive manner. He believed that direct British rule was better than the rule of Indian princely states and that the Company should expand its territory at every opportunity.

## Which States Were Lost

- **Satara (1848)** — this state of the descendants of Shivaji was the first victim of the Doctrine of Lapse.
- **Jaitpur and Sambalpur (1849)**, **Baghat (1850)** and **Udaipur (1852)** in Chhattisgarh — small states.
- **Jhansi (1853–54)** — after the death of Raja Gangadhar Rao in 1853 the adopted son Damodar Rao was not recognised and the state was annexed in 1854. Rani Lakshmibai's petitions were rejected.
- **Nagpur (1853–54)** — after the death of the Bhonsle ruler Raghuji III this large Maratha state was annexed; later even the palace's belongings were auctioned.

Along with this Dalhousie also abolished many titles and pensions. After the death of the last Peshwa Baji Rao II in 1851, the pension of his adopted son **Nana Sahib** was stopped, and the titles of the Nawab of the Carnatic (Arcot) and the Raja of Thanjavur were ended.

## Awadh: Not the Doctrine of Lapse but a Charge of "Misgovernment"

The annexation of Awadh is often linked to the Doctrine of Lapse, but the policy was not applied there, because the Nawab **Wajid Ali Shah** had heirs. In February **1856** Dalhousie annexed Awadh to the Company's territory on a charge of **misgovernment** and sent the Nawab to Calcutta. Awadh had been an old ally of the Company, so this annexation spread deep resentment among its court, the taluqdars and the many sepoys who came from Awadh.

## Consequences and 1857

These annexations increased the Company's territory and revenue, but a fear spread among Indian rulers that no state was safe. Many of the houses whose states or pensions had been taken away were seen in the front ranks in the **Uprising of 1857** — Rani Lakshmibai of Jhansi, Nana Sahib of Kanpur and Begum Hazrat Mahal of Awadh are examples. Historians do not regard the Doctrine of Lapse as the only cause of 1857, but do regard it as a major source of political discontent.

## The End of the Policy

After 1857 the rule of India came directly into the hands of the British Crown. The Queen's Proclamation of **1858** promised respect for the rights and dignity of native princes. After this the British government gave up the Doctrine of Lapse and gave sanads recognising the right of princely states to adopt. Now, instead of wiping out the princely states, the British adopted a policy of keeping them as loyal allies.
