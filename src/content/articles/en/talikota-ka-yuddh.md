## Background: Rama Raya and Deccan Politics

After Krishnadevaraya (1509–1529), struggles over succession followed in Vijayanagara. From the 1540s, power was actually in the hands of Krishnadevaraya's son-in-law **Aliya Rama Raya**, while **Sadasiva Raya** was king in name.

To the north, the Bahmani kingdom had broken up into five sultanates — **Bijapur, Ahmednagar, Golconda, Bidar and Berar**. They also fought one another. Rama Raya skilfully intervened in these quarrels, siding with one sultanate at one time and another at another. For some years this benefited Vijayanagara, but gradually the sultans came to feel that Rama Raya had become a threat to all of them.

## The Alliance of the Sultanates

Around 1564, helped by marriage ties among them, four sultanates united:
- Ahmednagar — **Hussain Nizam Shah I**
- Bijapur — **Ali Adil Shah I**
- Golconda — **Ibrahim Qutb Shah**
- Bidar — **Ali Barid Shah**

Scholars disagree about whether Berar joined; many believe it did not take part in the battle.

## The Day of the Battle

The battle took place on **23 January 1565** — this is the most widely accepted date, though sources differ. The site was the area of the villages of **Rakkasagi and Tangadagi** near the Krishna River, which is why it is also called the **'Battle of Rakkasa-Tangadi'**. Accounts also differ on whether the battle lasted a few hours or several days.

The aged Rama Raya led the army himself. After early successes, the battle turned. Rama Raya was captured and beheaded — according to accounts, Hussain Nizam Shah did this himself or it was done on his orders. With the death of their leader, the Vijayanagara army scattered.

## Reasons for the Defeat: Old and New Views

- One old account says that two Muslim commanders of the Vijayanagara army (the **Gilani brothers**) changed sides in the middle of the battle. Many modern scholars consider this a story invented later, beginning with the account of the Venetian merchant **Cesare Federici**.
- Many historians regard the sultanates' superior **artillery and gunpowder weapons**, along with their mounted archers, as decisive.
- Colonial and nationalist writers described it as a clash of 'Hindu versus Muslim' civilisations. Historians such as **Richard Eaton** reject this — in their view it was a war of Deccan power politics, in which Rama Raya himself had earlier been an ally of several sultanates.

## The Plunder of Hampi

Rama Raya's brother **Tirumala** retreated with the surviving army and the treasury, leaving the capital almost undefended. The victorious armies entered **Vijayanagara (Hampi)** and plundered it. Older accounts speak of plunder lasting several months (about six months) and almost total destruction, while newer archaeological studies show that the damage was concentrated mainly in the **royal centre**. Whatever the case, this great city never became a capital again and gradually fell into ruin.

## Consequences and Significance

The Vijayanagara Empire did not end at once. Tirumala laid the foundation of the **Aravidu dynasty**, and the capital shifted first to **Penukonda**, then to Chandragiri and Vellore. The kingdom lasted until the 1640s, but its old power never returned. The Nayakas of Madurai, Thanjavur and Mysore gradually became independent.

On the other side, the unity of the victorious sultanates also broke down soon, and they began fighting one another again. Talikota is regarded as a decisive turning point in South Indian history — it ended the capital of a great empire and opened the way for the rise of regional powers.
