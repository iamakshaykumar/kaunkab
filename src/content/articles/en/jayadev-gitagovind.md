## The Poet Jayadeva

**Jayadeva** was a Sanskrit poet of twelfth-century eastern India. His period is roughly taken as **c. 1170 to 1245**, but very little is known for certain about his life. In the *Gita Govinda* he gives the name of his village as "**Kindubilva**", and its identification is debated even today — some scholars link it to **Kenduli Sasan** in Odisha, some to **Joydev Kenduli** in Birbhum district of West Bengal, and some to Kenduli near Jhanjharpur in Bihar. Several sixteenth-century works describe him as belonging to **Utkala** (Odisha).

According to tradition he was born in a Brahmin family and his parents were **Ramadevi** and **Bhojadeva**. Some temple inscriptions of Odisha suggest that he studied and taught poetry, music and dance at **Kurmapataka** near Konark. Both Bengal's tradition and history count him among the court poets of the Sena king **Lakshmana Sena**. His wife **Padmavati** is described as a dancer; this rests mainly on tradition, though the name Padmavati does occur in the poem.

The court of Lakshmana Sena was then a major centre of Sanskrit poetry, where along with Jayadeva there were poets such as **Dhoyi**, **Govardhana**, **Sharana** and **Umapatidhara**. In this atmosphere, when Vaishnava devotion was spreading in Bengal and Odisha, the *Gita Govinda* was composed. It should be remembered that many things about Jayadeva's life come from the later devotional tradition, so they should be read separately from firm history.

## The Structure of the Gita Govinda

The *Gita Govinda* is a unique **lyric poem** in Sanskrit. It has **12 cantos**, divided into **24 prabandhas**. Each prabandha has songs of eight couplets each, called **ashtapadis**. Between the songs are verses that carry the story forward.

Its greatest distinction is that it is among the early works in which **the raga and tala of every song** are also written down. It is therefore a work not only for reading but for singing. The poem begins with praise of the **ten avatars** of Lord Vishnu.

## Story and Mood

The story is very simple: in the spring season at Vrindavan, **Krishna** dances the rasa with other gopis, at which **Radha** becomes upset. Then come separation, a message through a friend, Krishna's remorse and finally the **union** of the two. In this journey there is a beautiful depiction of the eight states of the heroine — the **ashta-nayika** — which later deeply influenced Indian dance and music.

Many readers regard it not just as a love poem but as a spiritual allegory — in which the soul (Radha), after wandering, finally meets the Supreme Being (Krishna). In the Bhakti movement, especially in the Vaishnava tradition of Bengal and Odisha, this work became central.

## Temples, Dance and Painting

At the **Jagannath Temple** in **Puri** the *Gita Govinda* is sung every night, and this tradition is said to have continued since Jayadeva's time. The largest number of its manuscripts is found in Odisha, and **Odissi** dance and music are closely connected with it. In **Kerala** its ashtapadis became part of the **Sopana Sangeetam** sung in temples.

Many commentaries were written on it. The oldest known commentary was written in Odisha around **1190**, and **Rana Kumbha** of Mewar composed the famous commentary called **Rasikapriya**. Painters too were greatly attracted to it — such as the famous *Gita Govinda* series of the eighteenth-century Guler painter **Manaku**, one painting from which is in this article.

## Beyond India

**Two hymns** of Jayadeva are included in the Sikh scripture, the **Guru Granth Sahib**. The *Gita Govinda* has been translated into nearly all modern Indian languages and many European languages. Its first English translation was made by **William Jones** in **1792**, and in **1977** Barbara Stoler Miller's translation *Love Song of the Dark Lord* became very famous.

More than eight hundred years later, the *Gita Govinda* lives on as a work joining poetry, music, dance and devotion.
