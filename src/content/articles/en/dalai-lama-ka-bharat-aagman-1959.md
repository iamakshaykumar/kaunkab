## Uprising in Lhasa

In **1950** the Chinese army entered Tibet, and in **1951**, under an agreement, Tibet came under Chinese rule. Tibet's religious and temporal leader, the **14th Dalai Lama** — born **Lhamo Thondup** on **6 July 1935** in the village of Taktser in the Amdo region — was then still a teenager. Over the next few years, discontent and armed resistance against Chinese policies grew in eastern Tibet.

In **March 1959** a rumour spread in Lhasa that Chinese officials wanted to seize the Dalai Lama. On **10 March 1959** thousands of Tibetans surrounded his summer palace, **Norbulingka**, to keep the Dalai Lama from leaving. This is called the **1959 Tibetan uprising**. China described it as a "reactionary rebellion", while the exiled Tibetan community observes 10 March as "Tibetan National Uprising Day".

## The Journey Across the Himalaya

On the night of **17 March 1959**, disguised as an ordinary soldier, the Dalai Lama left Norbulingka with his family and some ministers. A few days later heavy fighting broke out in Lhasa, and the Chinese army crushed the uprising.

The Dalai Lama's party moved south for about two weeks along difficult mountain paths and over high passes. Tibetan guerrilla fighters protected them on the way. On **31 March 1959** they crossed the Indian border near **Khenzimane**, in the **Tawang** area of today's **Arunachal Pradesh** (some sources give the date as 30 March). There soldiers of the **Assam Rifles** and local Indian officials welcomed him. After staying a few days at Tawang monastery, he travelled via **Bomdila** to **Tezpur** in Assam.

## Asylum in India and Nehru's Policy

Prime Minister **Jawaharlal Nehru's** government decided to give **asylum** to the Dalai Lama and his companions. On **18 April 1959** the Dalai Lama issued his first public statement at Tezpur. He then went to **Mussoorie** in Uttar Pradesh (today Uttarakhand), where Nehru met him.

Nehru's policy was balanced — India gave the Dalai Lama asylum on humanitarian grounds and full respect as a religious leader, but did not recognise his government as the government of an independent country. **China** regarded India's step as interference in its internal affairs. After this, relations between the two countries deteriorated rapidly, and it became part of the background to the 1962 India-China war.

## From Mussoorie to Dharamshala

On **29 April 1959** the Dalai Lama established the **Tibetan government in exile** in Mussoorie. Following the Dalai Lama, thousands of Tibetan refugees crossed the Himalaya into India, Nepal and Bhutan. Their number is estimated at about **80,000**. At first many refugees were put to work on tasks such as road building, and they suffered great hardship from the hot climate and disease.

In **May 1960** the government in exile moved to **Dharamshala** in Himachal Pradesh. Its centre became **McLeod Ganj** in upper Dharamshala, an old hill town from the British era. The Dalai Lama's residence, the main temple **Tsuglagkhang** and the offices of the exile administration were built here. The Dalai Lama's elder sister **Tsering Dolma** started a nursery for orphaned and refugee children, which later grew into the **Tibetan Children's Village**. With the help of the Government of India, Tibetan settlements were also established at places such as **Bylakuppe** in Karnataka.

## Democracy in Exile and the Situation Today

The exile administration, today called the **Central Tibetan Administration**, gradually adopted a democratic structure — an elected parliament and an elected political head. In **2011** the Dalai Lama handed over his political powers to the elected leadership and kept only the role of spiritual leader.

Since the 1970s the Dalai Lama has spoken of the **"Middle Way"** policy, under which he seeks genuine autonomy for Tibet within China rather than full independence. In **1989** he received the **Nobel Peace Prize**. The Chinese government calls him a "separatist" and considers Tibet an inseparable part of China.

## Legacy

This arrival in 1959 was a major humanitarian and diplomatic event in the history of modern India. It deeply affected India-China relations and gave rise to a large, organised refugee community in India. Today Dharamshala is a leading centre of Tibetan culture, Buddhist learning and exiled Tibetan politics, and pilgrims and tourists come here from all over the world.
