## Background: The Tangle over Customs Duty

From the seventeenth century the English East India Company had been buying cotton cloth, silk and saltpetre from Bengal and sending them to Europe. According to an order (nishan) of Prince Shah Shuja in 1651, the Company could avoid customs duty by paying a fixed sum of **3,000 rupees** every year. But local officials often did not obey these orders and collected tolls at the checkposts on the roads.

The Company wanted a **farman** from the emperor himself, which no governor could set aside.

## The Surman Delegation

For this purpose the Company sent a delegation from Calcutta to Delhi, which reached the court of the emperor **Farrukhsiyar** (reigned 1713–1719) in **1715**. Its members were:
- **John Surman**, the leader,
- **Khwaja Sarhad**, an Armenian merchant and interpreter,
- **Edward Stephenson**, secretary,
- **Dr William Hamilton**, physician.

At first the negotiations at court made no progress. At that time real power was in the hands of the **Sayyid brothers**, and many nobles were not in favour of granting concessions to the Company. According to the traditional account, the situation changed after Dr Hamilton cured the emperor's illness. In 1717 the emperor issued separate farmans for Bengal, Bombay and Madras.

## What Was in the Farman?

The main points of the Bengal farman and the accompanying orders (hasb-ul-hukm) of the wazir Sayyid Abdullah Khan were:
- In return for a **peshkash of 3,000 rupees a year**, the Company's goods would be **exempt from customs duty** in Bengal, Bihar and Orissa.
- The heads of the Company's factories could issue a **dastak** (pass), on showing which goods would pass without tax.
- The Company could take the zamindari of the villages around Calcutta and rent more villages.
- With permission, the Company could have its gold and silver minted into coins at the **Murshidabad mint**.
- Officials would help in recovering stolen goods and in arresting absconding debtors.

## The Governor's Opposition

The powerful governor of Bengal, **Murshid Quli Khan**, was not pleased with these concessions. He opposed the zamindari of 38 villages and the use of the mint, and provincial officials also enforced the farman only half-heartedly. That is, the order from Delhi and the ground reality in Bengal were different.

## Why Did It Become Important Later?

British writers called this farman the **'Magna Carta'** of the Company's trade, because it gave the English a big advantage over Indian and other European merchants. Trade grew many times over, but the peshkash remained the same 3,000 rupees, which caused heavy loss to the imperial treasury.

The problem grew when Company employees began to use the dastak for their **private trade** as well, though the exemption was only for the Company's goods. Alivardi Khan and then **Siraj-ud-Daulah** objected to this misuse. This tension led to the **Battle of Plassey** in 1757. Later Mir Qasim also took on a conflict with the Company over the same issue, which turned into the **Battle of Buxar (1764)**.

## Conclusion

Farrukhsiyar's farman shows how a weakening Mughal centre gave a foreign trading company privileges whose far-reaching consequences it had not even imagined. In 1719 Farrukhsiyar himself was deposed and killed by the Sayyid brothers, but the farman he had given remained the Company's greatest legal weapon for decades.
