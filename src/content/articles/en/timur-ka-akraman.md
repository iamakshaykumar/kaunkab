## Background: The Crumbling Tughlaq Sultanate

After the death of **Firuz Shah Tughlaq** in 1388, the Delhi Sultanate weakened rapidly. In succession quarrels, several sultans changed within a few years. By the 1390s there were two rival sultans — **Nasir-ud-din Mahmud Shah** in Delhi and Nusrat Shah at nearby Firozabad — each claiming to be the true ruler. Real power was in the hands of nobles such as the amir **Mallu Iqbal**, and many provinces (such as Gujarat, Malwa and Jaunpur) had become practically independent.

Meanwhile in Central Asia, **Timur** (also called 'Timur-i-Lang', that is, Timur the Lame, in Persian sources) had made Samarkand his capital and built a vast empire stretching from Iran to the borders of Russia. India's wealth and Delhi's weakness drew him this way.

## From the Indus to Delhi

Timur's grandson **Pir Muhammad** had already besieged and taken Multan. Timur himself crossed the Indus on **30 September 1398** with about 90,000 soldiers. On the way, **Tulamba** was plundered and its people slaughtered. The fort of **Bhatner** (today's Hanumangarh, Rajasthan) was also besieged, captured and razed. In many villages along the way, local people, especially Jat peasants, resisted, and this was ruthlessly crushed.

## The Battle of Delhi: 17 December 1398

Before reaching Delhi, Timur had the prisoners captured during the campaign massacred, fearing they would revolt during the battle. His historians gave the number as about one hundred thousand; modern historians consider this probably an exaggerated figure, but there is no doubt about the fact of the massacre.

On **17 December 1398** the army of Sultan Mahmud and Mallu Iqbal took the field with war elephants. To deal with the elephants, Timur had trenches and obstacles prepared; according to one famous account, camels loaded with burning grass were driven towards the elephants, so that the panicked elephants began trampling their own army. The Delhi army was defeated and Sultan Mahmud fled.

## The Sack of Delhi

After the victory Timur's army entered the city. The citizens took up arms in protest against the soldiers' plunder and cruelty, and in return a terrible massacre and plunder went on for several days. Thousands of people were taken away as captives and slaves, including craftsmen. Timur stayed in Delhi for about fifteen days and set out back in early January 1399. On the return march too there were raids at Meerut and in the upper Ganges region, and by April 1399 he reached Samarkand.

## Consequences

Timur did not try to establish a kingdom in India; his aim was plunder and prestige. But the consequences were far-reaching:
- Delhi remained desolate for years; famine and epidemic added to the suffering.
- The Tughlaq Sultanate was reduced to a name. Sultan Mahmud returned, but with his death in about 1412–13 the Tughlaq dynasty ended.
- Timur had made **Khizr Khan** governor of the Multan-Lahore region; in 1414 he took over Delhi and laid the foundation of the **Sayyid dynasty**.
- The independence of the provinces became firm and an age of regional kingdoms began in northern India.

About a hundred and twenty-five years later, Timur's descendant **Babur** won Panipat (1526) and established the Mughal Empire in India.
