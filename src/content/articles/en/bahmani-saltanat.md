## Background: The Revolt Against Tughluq

By the middle of the 14th century the distant provinces of the Delhi Sultanate were discontented with the harsh policies of Muhammad bin Tughluq. The "amiran-i-sada" (officers over a hundred villages each) posted in the Deccan rebelled and seized Daulatabad.

Out of this revolt emerged **Zafar Khan**, also called **Hasan Gangu**. On **3 August 1347** he became sultan under the name **Alauddin Hasan Bahman Shah**. There is a famous story behind the name "Gangu" — that Hasan was earlier a servant of a Brahmin named Gangu — but this tale comes from the later historian Firishta, and historians regard it as a legend. The sultans themselves linked the dynasty's name "Bahmani" to the legendary Persian king Bahman.

## From Gulbarga to Bidar

The first capital was **Gulbarga** (today's Kalaburagi, Karnataka), also called Hasanabad. Its fort and the huge **Jami Masjid** are hallmarks of this period; most of this mosque is covered with domes, which is unusual in India.

- **Firuz Shah Bahmani** (1397–1422) was a scholarly ruler. He also gave places in his court to many Hindu officials.
- **Ahmad Shah Wali** (1422–1436) moved the capital to **Bidar** and built a new fort there. Because of his devotion to Sufi saints he was called "Wali".

## The Long Struggle with Vijayanagara

The southern neighbour of the Bahmani Sultanate was the Vijayanagara empire. Wars between the two continued through almost the whole period over the fertile **Raichur Doab** between the Tungabhadra and Krishna rivers. Victory and defeat went to both sides — sometimes the Bahmani army advanced, sometimes Vijayanagara pushed it back. These battles constantly drained the resources of both states.

## Mahmud Gawan: The Peak of the Sultanate

The merchant-scholar **Mahmud Gawan**, who came from the Gilan region of Iran, reached the Bahmani court around 1453 and later became wazir. From about 1466 to 1481 he was the real architect of the government.

- His campaigns extended the sultanate to Goa on the western coast.
- He increased the number of provinces (tarafs) from four to eight, so that no governor could become too powerful.
- In 1472 he built a magnificent **madrasa** at Bidar, with a library and rooms for teachers and students. A part of its minaret with coloured tiles still stands today.

In the court there was deep factionalism between the "Deccanis" (locals) and the "Afaqis" (those who had come from outside). Using a forged letter, his opponents accused Mahmud Gawan of treason, and in **1481** the sultan Muhammad Shah III had him executed. Most historians believe that this event was the beginning of the sultanate's decline.

## Disintegration and the Five Deccan Sultanates

After Mahmud Gawan the sultans grew weaker and real power passed into the hands of the governors. Around 1490 Bijapur (Adil Shahis), Ahmednagar (Nizam Shahis) and Berar (Imad Shahis) became independent; the Barid Shahis rose in Bidar and the Qutb Shahis in Golconda around 1518. In the time of the last Bahmani sultan, **Kalimullah**, by about **1527**, the sultanate had become a name only.

## Significance

The Bahmani Sultanate began a new political and cultural tradition in the Deccan, independent of Delhi. From a blend of Persian, Turkic and local Marathi-Kannada-Telugu cultures, the "Deccani" language, architecture and art developed. Its successor sultanates shaped the politics of South India for the next one hundred and fifty years.
