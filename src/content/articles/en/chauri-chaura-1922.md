## Background: The Surge of Non-Cooperation

In **1920**, under the leadership of **Mahatma Gandhi**, the Congress launched the **Non-Cooperation Movement**. People were leaving government schools, courts, titles and foreign cloth, and picketing liquor shops. Gandhi had promised that if the movement remained fully **non-violent**, swaraj could be won within a year. Volunteer groups were formed village by village, and people called them 'volunteers'.

**Gorakhpur** district in the United Provinces (today's Uttar Pradesh) was a major centre of the movement. In February 1921 Gandhi himself had visited Gorakhpur, and the peasants held him in deep reverence. In the villages, pickets were being held in markets against rising prices and the sale of liquor. **Chauri Chaura** was a small town about 25 kilometres from Gorakhpur, with a police station (chowki).

## 2 to 4 February 1922: What Happened

On **2 February 1922**, in nearby **Gauri Bazaar**, volunteers were protesting against high food prices and the sale of liquor. Police led by the station's **Daroga Gupteshwar Singh** beat them and drove them away. Several leaders, including **Bhagwan Ahir**, were arrested. Bhagwan Ahir had earlier served in the army, and news of his beating spread great anger among the people.

In protest, on **4 February 1922**, about **2,000 to 2,500** people marched in procession towards the liquor shop in the market. The police first fired warning shots into the air. The crowd took this as a sign of fear and began throwing stones. The police then fired directly into the crowd, killing **three people** and wounding many.

The firing made the crowd uncontrollable. The policemen fell back and took shelter inside the police station. The enraged crowd surrounded the station and **set it on fire**. Those constables who ran out were also killed. In this incident **22 policemen and chowkidars**, including the station officer, were killed. (Some accounts give the number as 23, because one constable who was believed dead was later found alive.)

## Gandhi's Decision: Withdrawing the Movement

The news was a deep shock for Gandhi. He believed the country was not yet fully ready for non-violence, and that if the movement continued, violence might spread. After a meeting of the Congress Working Committee at **Bardoli**, the Non-Cooperation Movement was **suspended** across the country on **12 February 1922**. This is known as the **'Bardoli Resolution'**. It asked workers to give up civil disobedience and focus on the **constructive programme** — khadi, prohibition, Hindu-Muslim unity and the removal of untouchability. As penance, Gandhi also undertook a **five-day fast**.

The decision was highly controversial. Leaders such as **Motilal Nehru, Chittaranjan Das, Jawaharlal Nehru** and **Subhas Chandra Bose**, who were then in jail or active in the movement, were disappointed. They argued that it was wrong to halt a nationwide movement at its peak because of an incident in one village. Gandhi's supporters argued that without discipline a non-violent movement would turn into a violent revolt and give the government an excuse for harsh repression.

As soon as the movement stopped, the government arrested Gandhi in **March 1922**, and in a sedition trial he was sentenced to **six years**. However, he was released in **February 1924** on grounds of poor health.

## The Trial and the Hangings

After the incident, the police made large-scale arrests in the surrounding villages. In the **Gorakhpur Sessions Court**, **225** people were tried on charges of rioting, arson and murder. In the verdict of **9 January 1923**, **170** people were sentenced to death, which was seen across the country as extremely harsh.

An appeal was filed in the **Allahabad High Court**. The role of the noted leader and lawyer **Pandit Madan Mohan Malaviya** in defending the accused is remembered; the local saint **Baba Raghav Das** also made efforts to help them. In its verdict of **30 April 1923**, the High Court upheld **19** death sentences and gave the rest life imprisonment or shorter terms, while many were acquitted. These 19 people were hanged in different jails in **July 1923**.

## Memorials and Legacy

In 1923 the British government built a memorial to the policemen who had been killed. After independence, people began to remember the hanged villagers as **freedom fighters**. In **1973** a local committee built a memorial column in memory of the martyrs, and later the government built a larger **Martyrs' Memorial** inscribed with the names of those who were hanged. A train running from Gorakhpur is also named the **'Chauri Chaura Express'**.

On **4 February 2021**, on the centenary of the incident, the Prime Minister inaugurated the **Chauri Chaura Centenary Celebrations**, and a postage stamp was released.

The significance of Chauri Chaura in history is twofold. On one hand it shows how deeply the Non-Cooperation Movement had reached ordinary peasants in the villages; on the other, it became a test of Gandhi's principle that **the means must be as pure as the ends**. After the movement was withdrawn, many young people turned to the revolutionary path, while new experiments such as the **Swaraj Party** emerged within the Congress. About eight years later, in 1930, Gandhi launched another great mass movement with the Salt Satyagraha.
