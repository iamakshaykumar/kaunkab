## The Earliest Mentions

The **Cheras**, along with the **Cholas** and the **Pandyas**, are counted among the three great dynasties of ancient Tamilakam. Their region was roughly present-day central and northern Kerala and the Kongu region of western Tamil Nadu. In the third century BCE, **Ashoka**'s edicts mention 'Keralaputra' among the neighbouring kingdoms of the south, which is linked to the Cheras. The Cheras' royal emblem was the **bow and arrow**, which also appears on many of their coins.

Where the Cheras' capital **Vanji** lay has been debated for a long time. Most scholars now link it with **Karur** in Tamil Nadu, while **Muziris** (Muchiri in Tamil) and **Tondi** on the coast were their main ports.

## The Chera Kings of the Sangam Age

Most of our information about the Cheras comes from Sangam literature. The collection **Patirruppattu** ("Ten Tens") has ten poems each in praise of different Chera kings; its first and last decades are lost. On this basis the names of some major kings are known:

- **Udiyan Cheralatan** — one of the early kings;
- **Nedum Cheralatan** — of whom it was said that he seized the Yavanas (Greek-Roman traders) on the coast and came to be called 'Imayavaramban' (whose boundary reached the Himalaya) — this is the poet's exaggeration;
- **Senguttuvan** — described in the epic *Silappadikaram* as the builder of the temple of Kannagi.

The dates of these kings are approximate and are generally placed in the first-second century CE. Tamil-Brahmi inscriptions of about the second century CE found at **Pugalur** near Karur give the names of three generations of Chera rulers and mention the making of a cave dwelling for a Jain monk — this is important inscriptional evidence for the Cheras.

## Muziris and the Roman Trade

The biggest source of the Cheras' prosperity was the **pepper** trade. In the first century CE the Roman writer **Pliny** and the Greek sailors' handbook **'Periplus of the Erythraean Sea'** describe Muziris — according to the Periplus, it was crowded with Greek, Roman and Arab ships. Sangam poems also paint a picture of Yavana ships bringing gold and carrying away pepper.

A large number of **Roman coins** have been found in Kerala and the Karur-Coimbatore region. The Cheras also issued their own silver coins, bearing the king's face and names such as 'Makkotai' or 'Kuttuvan Kotai' in Brahmi; on some Roman coins the Cheras also put their own stamp. As Roman trade declined in the third-fourth century, this prosperity too began to fade.

## Decline and a Long Darkness

Between the third and fifth centuries the influence of the Cheras declined. In the Tamil country came the age of the **Kalabhras**, about which very little is known. The evidence for Chera history over the next few hundred years is very scattered, and in the meantime the Pandyas and Pallavas became the major powers of the south.

## The Chera Perumals of Kodungallur

In the ninth century a new dynasty under the Chera name rose in central Kerala, called the **Chera Perumals**. Its capital was **Mahodayapuram** (Makotai), which is identified with present-day **Kodungallur**. Its earliest known king was **Sthanu Ravi** (from about 844 CE). In this period the port of **Kollam** became a centre of Arab and Chinese trade, and from 825 CE the 'Kollam Era' of Kerala began. After pressure from the Cholas in the eleventh century, the Perumals' power broke up by about **1124 CE** and Kerala was divided into many small kingdoms. Stories such as that of the last Perumal going to Mecca are folk tradition, and have no historical confirmation.

## Legacy

The Cheras connected Kerala to the trading world of the Indian Ocean. The sea route that developed in their time for pepper went on bringing Arab, Chinese and European traders to the Malabar coast. The Sangam poems, the Pugalur inscriptions and the Pattanam excavations together are gradually clarifying the story of this ancient dynasty.
