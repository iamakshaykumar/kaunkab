## Persian Roots

The story of Mughal painting begins with Humayun's exile. Around 1544, at the court of Shah Tahmasp of Iran, Humayun saw the tradition of Persian miniature painting. Two masters there — **Mir Sayyid Ali** and **Khwaja Abd al-Samad** — joined Humayun in Kabul around 1549. Abd al-Samad taught the young Akbar painting. After Humayun's return to Delhi in 1555, these two went on to become the foundation of the royal workshop.

## Akbar's Workshop and the Hamzanama

Akbar turned painting into an organised industry under royal patronage. His workshop had more than a hundred painters, among them a large number of Indian — especially Hindu — artists. Many hands often worked on one painting: one did the drawing, another the colouring, another the faces.

Its biggest project was the **Hamzanama** — an illustrated collection of folk tales about Amir Hamza, uncle of the Prophet. This work, which ran from about **1562 to 1577**, was directed first by Mir Sayyid Ali and later by Abd al-Samad. Its large folios are today scattered across many museums of the world. Such projects moulded artists of different backgrounds into a common style — Persian ornamentation, Indian colour and movement, and gradually growing realism.

## Masters of Akbar's Time

- **Daswanth:** Said to be the son of a palanquin-bearer. Abu'l Fazl counted him among the finest painters of the age. He composed many paintings of the **Razmnama** (1582-86), the Persian translation of the Mahabharata. He died prematurely in 1584.
- **Basawan:** (active about 1580-1600) Famous for crowded scenes, the illusion of depth and the expressions of faces. His son Manohar also became a leading painter in Jahangir's time.
- Artists such as **Kesu Das** adopted new techniques by copying European engravings that reached the court.

The Persian translations of the Akbarnama, Baburnama, Ramayana and Mahabharata were also illustrated in this period.

## Jahangir — The Peak Period

Jahangir stressed the fine detail of individual paintings rather than big collective projects. In his time portraits, court scenes and nature studies became prominent, and these were arranged in muraqqas (albums) with beautiful borders.

- **Ustad Mansur** (active about 1590-1624) was a master of accurate paintings of animals, birds and flowers.
- **Abu'l Hasan** (1589-about 1630), son of the Persian painter Aqa Riza, made allegorical paintings such as 'Jahangir shooting an arrow at the head of Malik Ambar' — which was not a real event but a depiction of the emperor's wish.
- **Bichitr** created royal images with European-influenced halos and light and shade.

## Shah Jahan and After

In Shah Jahan's time paintings became more formal and grand; the finest example is the illustrated **Padshahnama**. Aurangzeb was not an enthusiastic patron of painting, and after about 1668 royal patronage declined. Many painters moved to provincial and Rajput courts, where Mughal technique gave new form to the Rajasthani and Pahari styles. In this way the influence of Mughal painting remained in Indian art even after the decline of the empire.
