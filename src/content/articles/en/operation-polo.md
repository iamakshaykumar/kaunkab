## The Largest Princely State

**Hyderabad** was the largest princely state of India in area and population, surrounded on all sides by Indian territory. Its ruler, **Nizam Mir Osman Ali Khan** (Asaf Jah VII), was a Muslim, while a large part of the population was Hindu, and the high posts in the administration and army were mostly held by Muslims. In 1947 the Nizam chose to remain independent rather than join India or Pakistan.

## The Standstill Agreement and the Razakars

In November 1947 a one-year **'Standstill Agreement'** was made between India and Hyderabad, so that negotiations could continue. But conditions inside the state kept worsening. The paramilitary organisation **'Razakars'** of **Qasim Razvi**, leader of the **Majlis-e-Ittehadul Muslimeen**, committed violence against those demanding merger with India and against villagers; many incidents of the Razakars' rampage are recorded. **Mir Laiq Ali**, under Razvi's influence, became prime minister. On the other side, in **Telangana**, an armed peasant revolt led by Communists had been going on since 1945–46 against landlords and the Nizam's administration. The State Congress satyagraha and clashes on the border also kept increasing.

On behalf of the Government of India, **Sardar Patel** and **V. P. Menon**, secretary of the States Ministry, were negotiating, and India's agent in Hyderabad was **K. M. Munshi**. Before Mountbatten left, a settlement proposal was also discussed in June 1948, but it did not come to anything. In August 1948 Hyderabad announced that it would take its case to the **United Nations Security Council**.

## A Five-Day Operation

On the morning of **13 September 1948** the Indian Army entered Hyderabad. Under the overall command of Lieutenant General **Rajendrasinhji**, the main column under Major General **Jayanto Nath Chaudhuri** advanced from the west from the direction of **Solapur** and another column from the east from the direction of **Vijayawada**. The Hyderabad state army and the Razakars could not hold out for long. On **17 September** the Nizam declared a ceasefire and the Laiq Ali ministry resigned. On **18 September** the chief of the Hyderabad army, Major General **Syed Ahmed El Edroos**, surrendered before Chaudhuri. The Government of India officially called this operation a **'police action'**.

## Loss of Life and Later Violence

According to the available figures on military losses, the Indian Army's losses were very limited, while about 800 of the Hyderabad state army and about 1,300–1,400 Razakars were killed. The saddest aspect was the violence against ordinary people during and after the operation. In many areas — especially Marathwada and its surroundings — revenge for the Razakars' earlier atrocities was taken on the Muslim population. The committee of **Pandit Sundarlal**, sent by Nehru, in its 'very cautious estimate' put the number of dead **between 27,000 and 40,000**; some other observers have estimated a far larger number. This report was not published for decades and became public in 2013. The exact number of dead is still a matter of dispute today.

## After the Merger

After the action, military administration was imposed in Hyderabad, with J. N. Chaudhuri as its head. In September 1948 itself the Nizam withdrew his complaint from the United Nations. In a radio speech on 23 September he said that the Laiq Ali government had been imposed on him under pressure from the Razakars. Qasim Razvi was tried and imprisoned; later he went to Pakistan. When the Constitution came into force on 26 January 1950, Hyderabad became a state of India and the Nizam was made its **Rajpramukh**. In the States Reorganisation of 1956, the territory of the state was divided on the basis of language into Andhra Pradesh, Bombay (later Maharashtra) and Mysore (later Karnataka).

## Significance

Operation Polo ended the possibility of an independent state in the very middle of India and nearly completed the process of integration of the princely states. In Telangana and Marathwada, 17 September is still observed today in memory of this merger. At the same time, historians also stress that the communal violence that accompanied the merger is a painful side of this chapter, which must be remembered honestly.
