## An Island in the Middle of the Rann

In Kutch district of Gujarat spreads the **Great Rann of Kutch**, a salty, flat expanse that fills with water in the rains. In the middle of this Rann lies an island called **Khadir Bet**, where the village of Dholavira in Bhachau taluka is situated. About a kilometre from the village there was a large mound that the local people called "Kotada Timba". Beneath this mound lay buried a huge city of the Harappan civilisation.

It is said that in the early 1960s a villager, **Shambhudan Gadhvi**, drew the government's attention to this site. In 1967–68 **Jagat Pati Joshi** of the Archaeological Survey of India formally discovered it. Large-scale excavation began in 1989–90 under the direction of **Ravindra Singh Bisht** and continued for thirteen seasons until 2005.

## The Layout of the City

Spread over about **47 hectares**, Dholavira is counted among the largest Harappan cities found in India. Most cities such as Mohenjo-daro had two parts, but Dholavira was divided into **three parts**:
- **The Citadel** — the highest part, enclosed by double walls, with a separate enclosure (the "bailey") believed to be for officials.
- **The Middle Town** — the middle section with its own rampart.
- **The Lower Town** — the settlement of ordinary people and craftsmen.

Between the citadel and the middle town is a large open ground, which many archaeologists consider a place for festivals or assemblies, but its real use is only a guess. The whole city was enclosed by an outer rampart and its buildings were made mainly of **local stone**.

## The Art of Saving Water

Rain in Kutch is scanty and uncertain. The people of Dholavira found a very clever solution. Two seasonal streams flowed to the north and south of the city, the **Mansar** and the **Manhar**. Dams were built at several places on them to divert the rainwater, which was channelled through drains into reservoirs built inside the city.

According to the excavations, **sixteen or more reservoirs** were built here. Some reservoirs were cut into the rock; the larger ones among them are about 79 metres long and 7 metres deep, with steps to go down into them. The slope of the land also helped this system. This water management gives Dholavira an identity distinct from the other Harappan cities.

## The "Signboard" with Ten Signs

One of the most talked-about discoveries at Dholavira is the so-called **"signboard"** found in a room near the northern gate. It has **ten large signs** of the Indus script, each about 37 centimetres high. These signs were made of pieces of white **gypsum** and inlaid on a wooden board; the wood rotted away, but the signs were found lying on the ground in the same order.

It is believed that this board was mounted above the gate so as to be visible from afar. But the Indus script has still not been deciphered, so no one knows what was written on it, whether the name of the city, the name of a ruler or something else.

## The Cemetery and the Decline

A cemetery has been found outside the city walls to the west. It has several kinds of burials, some of which contained no skeletons, so some scholars consider them memorial structures. Mud-brick hemispherical structures have also been found nearby.

In Bisht's original chronology, the life of Dholavira was divided into seven stages from about 2650 BCE to 1450 BCE. Later studies have taken the early settlement to be older, going back to the pre-Harappan period. After about 2100 BCE the city gradually began to shrink; in between it was also empty for some time and then resettled on a small scale. There are differences of opinion about the causes of the decline, such as increasing dryness of the climate or a fall in trade.

## Significance

On 27 July 2021, at the 44th session of UNESCO's World Heritage Committee, **"Dholavira: a Harappan City"** was added to the World Heritage List. It became India's 40th World Heritage Site. Dholavira shows that the Harappan people could build planned cities even in a difficult, dry region, and the understanding they showed of conserving every drop of water offers a lesson even in today's age of water crisis.
