## A Judge's Curiosity

**Sir William Jones** (1746–1794) was a British lawyer and a master of many languages. In 1783 he came to Calcutta as a judge of the Supreme Court. To understand cases concerning Hindu and Muslim law in court, he began learning Sanskrit, and soon a deep interest in India's history, literature and science awoke in him.

On **15 January 1784** a meeting of about thirty European gentlemen was held in the **Grand Jury Room** of the Supreme Court, presided over by Judge **Robert Chambers**. Here the **'Asiatic Society'** was founded, and Jones became its first president. According to him, the limits of the Society's study were "the geographical limits of Asia" — that is, every subject concerning man and nature.

## The Kinship of Languages

In an annual discourse in 1786, Jones said that Sanskrit, Greek and Latin bear such deep resemblances that they seem to have sprung from some common source. This idea later became an important basis for **comparative linguistics** and the concept of the 'Indo-European language family'. Members of the Society made English translations of the *Abhijnanashakuntalam*, the *Manusmriti* and other works, which spread discussion of Indian literature in Europe. The Society's journal ***Asiatic Researches*** carried articles on history, geography, astronomy, botany and languages.

Jones died in Calcutta on **27 April 1794**, but the institution continued to grow. In 1808 it moved into its own building on **Park Street**, and in 1814 its museum was started.

## Prinsep and Brahmi

In 1832 **James Prinsep** became secretary of the Society and began editing its new journal (*Journal of the Asiatic Society of Bengal*). Copies of inscriptions and coins reached him from all over the country. Taking the final letters recurring in the small donation inscriptions of Sanchi to be "danam" (gift), he identified the letters of the **Brahmi script** in articles published between 1836 and 1838, of which the work of **1837** is considered decisive. In the same way, with the help of the bilingual-script coins of the Indo-Greeks, he also read **Kharosthi**.

Once Brahmi was read, the edicts of **"Devanampiya Piyadasi"** written on pillars and rocks could be read. With the help of Sri Lankan chronicles this king was soon identified with the Mauryan emperor **Ashoka** — which allowed the dates and events of ancient Indian history to be placed on a firm foundation.

## Membership and Name

In the early decades the Society was only for Europeans. In **1829**, on the initiative of H. H. Wilson, Indians began to be admitted as members, and in **1885** **Rajendralal Mitra** became its first Indian president. The name of the institution also changed several times — 'Asiatic Society of Bengal' in 1832, 'Royal Asiatic Society of Bengal' in 1936, and from 1951 again 'The Asiatic Society'.

## Assessment

The Asiatic Society played a major role in discovering India's past, printing texts and preserving manuscripts; even today thousands of manuscripts are safe in its library. On the other hand, modern historians also point out that this knowledge was tied to the needs of colonial rule — the rulers had to understand the language, law and customs of the ruled — and that the contribution of Indian scholars and pandits in it was often undervalued. Only by looking at both sides together can the historical importance of this institution be understood.
