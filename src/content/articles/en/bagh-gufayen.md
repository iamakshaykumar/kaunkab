## A Buddhist Monastery on the Vindhya Slope

In **Dhar district** of Madhya Pradesh, about 97 kilometres from the city of Dhar, on the southern slope of the Vindhya range, lie the **Bagh Caves**. They are cut into a high sandstone cliff on the far bank of a seasonal river, the **Baghini**.

Originally there were **nine** rock-cut caves here, of which **five** survive today. All of them are **viharas** — that is, monasteries where Buddhist monks lived and meditated. Their plan is square: a large hall in the middle, cells for monks all around, and at the back a small shrine room containing a stupa.

## When and By Whom Were They Built?

Scholars generally date these caves to the **fifth-sixth century CE**; some sources extend their date to the seventh century. The name of the person who had them built is not known for certain.

The most important clue to the date is a **copper plate**. This copper plate, connected with Cave 2, mentions a donation by **Maharaja Subandhu** for the repair of the vihara. The plate is undated, but another copper plate of Subandhu, found at Barwani, is dated **Gupta year 167 (about 487 CE)**. This shows that by the end of the fifth century Cave 2 had been built and was already in need of repair.

## Rang Mahal and the Main Caves

- **Cave 2** — the best-preserved cave, which local people call the **'Pandava Cave'**.
- **Cave 4** — the most important, called the **'Rang Mahal'**. The most beautiful paintings of Bagh were made on the walls of its veranda.
- Fragments of paintings have also been found in Caves 3, 5 and 7.

## Paintings Resembling Ajanta

The real identity of Bagh is its **murals**. They were made by the **tempera** method: first a thick plaster of red-brown clay was applied to the wall, a layer of lime was given over it, and then the colours were filled in. The paintings show bodhisattvas, scenes of dance and music, and elephants and horsemen walking in processions.

In style these paintings closely resemble **Ajanta**, so many scholars believe that painters of the same artistic tradition were working at both places. The difference is said to be that Bagh's paintings give more glimpses of the **worldly life** of that time than of religious stories.

## Discovery and Conservation

In the nineteenth century British officers turned their attention to these caves. **Lieutenant F. Dangerfield** of the Bombay Army is generally credited with reaching here in **1818** and publishing a description. Later travellers counted the caves as nine.

Sandstone is soft, so with time the roofs and pillars of many caves collapsed, and the paintings began to flake because of dampness. To save the paintings from further damage, in **1982** most of them were carefully removed from the walls and placed in the **Gujari Mahal Archaeological Museum at Gwalior**. The **Archaeological Survey of India**, in about 17 years of work, repaired the caves and supported the pillars.

## Significance

Along with Ajanta, the Bagh Caves are rare evidence of how developed the art of mural painting was in India in the Gupta period and just after it. Sculptures on stone survive in many places, but coloured paintings this old are found in only a few. Also, Subandhu's copper plate shows that local kings of the Malwa-Narmada region also patronised Buddhist monasteries.
