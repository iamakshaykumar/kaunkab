## Background: Iltutmish's Heir

Razia was born around 1205. She was the eldest daughter of the sultan of Delhi, **Iltutmish** (reign 1211–1236). According to later accounts, during the Gwalior campaign (about 1231) Iltutmish left the affairs of Delhi in Razia's charge and, impressed by her ability, chose her as his successor. Many historians doubt parts of this story, but it is clear that Razia was considered fit to rule.

After Iltutmish died in 1236, the nobles made his son **Rukn-ud-din Firuz** sultan. The real power was in the hands of his mother **Shah Turkan**, whose harshness spread discontent in the court.

## The Road to the Throne

According to the contemporary historian **Minhaj-us-Siraj**, when Rukn-ud-din was away from the capital, Razia appealed directly to the people of Delhi. The people and some soldiers came out in her support, Shah Turkan was imprisoned, and after Rukn-ud-din was removed in November 1236, Razia became sultan of Delhi. In this way she became the first woman ruler of the Sultanate and gained the throne with the support of the people.

## Style of Rule

Razia established herself as a full sultan:
- She issued coins in her own name.
- According to Minhaj, with time she gave up the veil and began to appear at court and in public places wearing a man's coat (qaba) and cap (kulah); she went out among the people seated on an elephant.
- She broke up a faction of rebel nobles by diplomacy and made her authority accepted in many regions.
- She gave high posts to some non-Turkish officers, so as to reduce dependence on the powerful Turkish nobles.

Minhaj praised her, saying she had all the qualities of a ruler; but he also wrote that, because she was a woman, these qualities were not considered sufficient in the eyes of that society.

## The Opposition of the Nobles and the Question of Yaqut

A powerful group of Turkish nobles (which later writers call the 'Chihalgani', or the Forty) wanted to keep the sultan as a puppet in their hands. Razia's independent attitude displeased them. The biggest dispute was over **Jamal-ud-din Yaqut** — this officer of Abyssinian (African) origin had been made master of the stables (Amir-i-Akhur). In later stories and popular tales, speculation was made about the personal relations between the two, but contemporary sources give no clear proof of it; most historians regard it as a pretext for political opposition.

## Revolt, Marriage and End

In 1240 the governor of Tabarhinda (Bathinda), **Ikhtiyar-ud-din Altunia**, revolted. Razia set out to put it down, but on the way the nobles killed Yaqut and took Razia prisoner, handing her over to Altunia. Meanwhile in Delhi, in April 1240, her half-brother **Muiz-ud-din Bahram** was made sultan.

Razia married Altunia, and together they tried to win back Delhi. But they were defeated, and in **October 1240** both were killed near Kaithal.

## Significance

Razia's rule lasted less than four years, but it is a unique chapter in the history of medieval India. Her life shows how delicate the balance of power was in that period between the sultan and the powerful nobles. Later this same struggle turned towards the sultan's absolute power under **Balban**. Razia is still regarded as a symbol of courage and ability.
