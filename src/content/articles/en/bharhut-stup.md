## The Lost Stupa of Satna

In the **Satna district** of Madhya Pradesh there is a small village called **Bharhut**. A large Buddhist **stupa** once stood here, surrounded by a carved red sandstone **railing** (vedika) and four **gateways** (toranas) in the four directions. With time the stupa was almost completely destroyed, and local people began taking its stones for construction.

In 1873 **Alexander Cunningham**, the founder of the Archaeological Survey of India, came here and had it excavated the following year. His assistant **Joseph David Beglar** carried the work forward and took photographs on a large scale. In 1879 Cunningham published a full book on it.

## When Was It Built?

It is believed that the original stupa of Bharhut was perhaps built of bricks in the time of **Ashoka** (3rd century BCE). Its stone railing is generally dated to about **125–100 BCE** and the surviving eastern gateway to about **100–75 BCE**.

On one pillar of the gateway there is an inscription recording a gift of a king named **Dhanabhuti**, which says that the construction was done "**sugana raje**". This is generally taken to mean "in the reign of the Shungas", and on this basis Bharhut is called art of the **Shunga period**. But some scholars draw another meaning from this phrase, and some recent researchers date the entire carving as late as the 1st century CE — so this date is not entirely certain.

## The Buddha in Symbols

The greatest feature of Bharhut's art is that the Buddha is nowhere shown in **human form**. His presence is indicated by symbols — the **Bodhi tree**, the **Dharmachakra**, an **empty seat** or **footprints**. In art history this is called "aniconic" or abstract depiction. Human statues of the Buddha became popular later, in the art of Gandhara and Mathura.

## The Stories and Their 'Captions'

On the round **medallions** and the upright pillars of Bharhut's railing are carved **Jataka tales** (stories of the Buddha's previous births), events from the Buddha's life, and scenes of earlier Buddhas. The inscriptions here include 136 donative records and 82 records that name the scenes.

One famous medallion shows the merchant **Anathapindika** of Shravasti buying the Jetavana garden — according to Buddhist tradition, he paid its price by covering the whole ground with coins, and the medallion shows people unloading coins from a bullock cart. On the railing there are also large figures of **yakshas and yakshis**, indicating the inclusion of folk deities in Buddhist art.

Mason's marks in the **Kharosthi** script have been found on the gateway, suggesting that artisans from the north-west also worked here. In one figure a **Yavana** (Greek) warrior is shown as a gatekeeper.

## Where Bharhut Is Today

A large part of the remains of Bharhut is today in the **Indian Museum in Kolkata**, where its eastern gateway and railing have been reassembled and placed in a separate "Bharhut Gallery". Some pieces are in other Indian museums, including the Prayagraj (Allahabad) Museum, and two panels are in the Freer Gallery in Washington. Along with Sanchi, Bharhut is counted among the earliest and most important links of Indian Buddhist art.
