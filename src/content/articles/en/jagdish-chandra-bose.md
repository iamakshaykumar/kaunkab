## From Bengal to Cambridge

**Jagadish Chandra Bose** was born on **30 November 1858** in **Mymensingh** in Bengal (today in Bangladesh). At **St. Xavier's College**, Calcutta, he was deeply influenced by his physics teacher **Father Eugène Lafont**. He then went to England — first he began studying medicine in London but had to give it up for reasons of health, then studied natural sciences at **Christ's College**, Cambridge, and took a B.Sc. degree from London.

## Against Discrimination in Pay

In **January 1885** Bose became Professor of Physics at **Presidency College**, Calcutta. At that time Indian professors were paid less than European professors. In protest Bose **refused to draw a salary for three years** and went on teaching without pay. In the end his appointment was made permanent. At first the college did not even have a good laboratory for him; he had many instruments made himself with the help of local craftsmen.

## Experiments with Invisible Waves (1894–96)

In those days work was moving fast in Europe on the electromagnetic waves discovered by Heinrich Hertz. From **November 1894** Bose began experiments on very short waves — from about **2.5 centimetres to 5 millimetres** — which are today called **millimetre waves** or microwaves.

In a demonstration in Calcutta in **1895** he sent these waves about **23 metres**, across intervening walls, and on the other side they **rang a bell** and **ignited a little gunpowder**. For this he designed many parts himself, such as the transmitter, the coherer (wave detector), dielectric lens, polariser and **horn antenna**. In **1897** he lectured on his research at the **Royal Institution** in London. He also developed a detector using a **galena crystal**, for which a US patent was filed in **1904** — it is counted among the early examples of semiconductor detectors.

## Marconi and the Question of Credit

It is often asked whether Bose "invented radio". The answer is not simple. Bose's aim was the scientific study of the properties of waves, not building a commercial communication system; he took little interest in patenting most of his work. **Marconi**, on the other hand, gave wireless telegraphy a practical and commercial form. Some historians hold that the design of a coherer used in Marconi's 1901 transatlantic signal was linked to Bose's work, but the matter is disputed. What is certain is that Bose's work on millimetre waves was far ahead of its time — such waves are used today in radar, satellite communication and 5G.

## The Response of Plants

From the beginning of the twentieth century Bose's attention turned to biophysics, especially **plants**. With extremely sensitive instruments he recorded the responses of plants to stimuli such as electricity, heat, chemicals and injury. His **crescograph** could record the extremely slow growth of plants magnified many times. He argued that there are similarities in the responses of living and some non-living matter. Some of his conclusions — such as the idea of "feeling" in plants — were regarded by many scientists, then and later, as exaggerations, while his basic work on electrical signals in plants is appreciated again today.

## The Bose Institute and Legacy

On his **59th birthday**, **30 November 1917**, Bose founded the **Bose Institute** (Basu Vigyan Mandir) in Calcutta and remained its director until the end of his life. In **1917** he was knighted and in **1920** he was elected a Fellow of the **Royal Society**. He died on **23 November 1937** at **Giridih** in Bihar (today Jharkhand).

Bose was among the early pillars of modern Indian science. He showed that even in colonial India, with limited means, world-class original research was possible.
