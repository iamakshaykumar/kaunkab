## From Gymnasiums to a Secret Organisation

At the start of the twentieth century, gymnasiums (akharas) were opening in Bengal's towns for young men, where wrestling, lathi-fighting and swordsmanship were taught. Out of these, the **Anushilan Samiti** emerged in Calcutta in **1902**. The student **Satish Chandra Basu** and the barrister **Pramathanath Mitra** were among its early organisers. The word 'anushilan' means constant practice of body and mind; the name is believed to have been inspired by the ideas of Bankim Chandra Chattopadhyay.

**Aurobindo Ghose**, then working in Baroda, also took an interest at this time in building a secret organisation in Bengal, and sent his younger brother **Barindra Kumar Ghosh** to Calcutta. Outwardly the Samiti was a centre for physical and moral training, but inwardly it was preparing for armed resistance to British rule.

## Expansion After the Partition of Bengal

The **Partition of Bengal** in 1905 gave the movement fresh energy. In East Bengal, **Pulin Behari Das** led the **Dhaka Anushilan Samiti**, whose branches soon grew to more than 500 and which became larger than the original body in Calcutta.

In Calcutta, Barindra Ghosh's group brought out the Bengali weekly **'Jugantar'** in **March 1906**; among its editors was **Bhupendranath Dutta**, the younger brother of Swami Vivekananda. The paper openly spoke of opposing British rule and of revolution; in 1907 Bhupendranath was sentenced to one year in prison on a charge of sedition. In time, the group of Barindra and his associates came to be known as the **'Jugantar'** party, after this paper.

## The Bomb Factory at Maniktala

In a garden house at **Murarapukur** in the Maniktala area of Calcutta, Barindra's associates began making weapons and bombs. **Hemchandra Kanungo** and **Ullaskar Dutta**, who had returned after learning bomb-making abroad, were prominent in this work. In December 1907 an attempt was made to blow up the train of Andrew Fraser, the Lieutenant Governor of Bengal, but it did not succeed.

On **30 April 1908**, **Khudiram Bose** and **Prafulla Chaki** threw a bomb at a carriage in Muzaffarpur, mistaking it for that of the magistrate Kingsford; two English women were killed. During the investigation, on **2 May 1908**, the police raided the Murarapukur garden and arrested more than three dozen people, including Aurobindo Ghose and Barindra Ghosh.

## The Alipore Bomb Case

The case 'Emperor vs. Aurobindo Ghose and Others' was tried at the Alipore Sessions Court before Judge **C. P. Beachcroft**. One of the accused, **Narendranath Goswami**, turned approver, but on **31 August 1908** **Kanailal Dutta** and **Satyendranath Bose** shot him dead inside Alipore Jail. Both were hanged in November 1908.

Aurobindo Ghose was defended by **Chittaranjan Das**. The verdict came on **6 May 1909** — several accused, including Aurobindo, were acquitted, while **Barindra Ghosh** and **Ullaskar Dutta** were sentenced to death, which was later commuted to life imprisonment. They and several other associates were sent to the Andamans; they were released under the general amnesty of 1920. After his acquittal, Aurobindo withdrew from active politics and moved to Pondicherry in 1910.

## Repression and the Road Ahead

Through laws such as the Criminal Law Amendment of December 1908 and Regulation III of 1818, the government tightened its grip on secret organisations and deported many leaders without trial. Even so, the movement did not end. During the First World War, the Jugantar leader **Jatindranath Mukherjee ('Bagha Jatin')** planned to bring in arms from Germany; the plan failed, and in 1915 he died of wounds after an encounter near Balasore. In the 1930s many Anushilan members turned towards Marxism, and later the **Revolutionary Socialist Party (RSP)** was formed.

## Significance

The Anushilan Samiti and Jugantar laid the foundation of an organised revolutionary movement in Bengal. The Alipore Bomb Case drew the attention of the whole country and gave national recognition to names such as Aurobindo Ghose and Chittaranjan Das. Historians acknowledge the courage and sacrifice of this period, but also record that the path of secret violence failed to bring the masses along on a large scale and was crushed by heavy repression. Later, many leaders of movements such as the Chittagong Armoury Raid came from this same tradition.
