## Into the War Without Being Asked

On **3 September 1939** Britain declared war on Germany, and on the same day Viceroy **Lord Linlithgow** declared India too a party to the war. Neither the Central Legislative Assembly nor the elected provincial governments were consulted. The Congress opposed fascism, but it demanded that Britain first make its war aims clear and promise India's freedom.

On **17 October 1939** the Viceroy offered only an assurance of consultation on the constitution after the war. Dissatisfied, the Congress Working Committee on **22 October** asked its ministries to resign, and between **the end of October and November 1939** the Congress governments of eight provinces resigned. The Muslim League celebrated this as a "Day of Deliverance". In the years that followed, the **August Offer (1940)**, the Individual Satyagraha, the **Cripps Mission (1942)** and the **Quit India Movement (1942)** all took place against the background of this war.

## Indian Soldiers on Fronts Across the World

In 1939 the Indian Army had about **205,000** soldiers. At the height of the war this figure rose above **2.5 million**. Recruitment was entirely voluntary, so it is called the largest volunteer army in history. The Royal Indian Navy and the Indian Air Force also expanded greatly during this period.

- **North Africa:** The 4th, 5th and 10th Indian Divisions fought in the desert against the German general Rommel's Afrika Korps.
- **East Africa:** In 1941, in the hard battle of **Keren** in Eritrea, Indian units defeated the Italian army.
- **Italy:** Here India's contingent was the third-largest of the Allies, after those of the United States and Britain.
- **Malaya and Singapore:** At the fall of Singapore in **February 1942**, thousands of Indian soldiers became Japanese prisoners of war. Many of them later joined the **Indian National Army (Azad Hind Fauj)**.
- **Burma:** In the Burma campaign, Indian soldiers formed the largest fighting force on the Allied side.

More than **87,000** Indian soldiers were killed in the war. Soldiers and officers of the Indian Army received **31 Victoria Crosses**.

## War at India's Doorstep

In 1942 Japan occupied Burma and the war reached India's eastern frontier. In March 1942 Japan took the **Andaman and Nicobar Islands**, which on **21 October 1943** it handed over, in name only, to the Azad Hind government of **Subhas Chandra Bose**. Calcutta and Chittagong were bombed by Japanese aircraft. Lakhs of Indian refugees from Burma walked back to India along difficult mountain routes.

## Imphal and Kohima (1944)

In **March 1944** Japan's Fifteenth Army under General **Renya Mutaguchi** launched **Operation U-Go**. The aim was to capture the British-Indian base at **Imphal** in Manipur. Units of the Indian National Army were also with the Japanese army. On the other side was General **William Slim's** Fourteenth Army, most of whose soldiers were Indian.

- **Imphal (8 March – 3 July 1944):** Imphal remained besieged for nearly three months, and the army was supplied by air. On **22 June** two British-Indian corps met near Milestone 109 on the highway and the siege was broken. On **3 July** Mutaguchi ordered the offensive to be called off.
- **Kohima (4 April – 22 June 1944):** General **Kotoku Sato's** 31st Division surrounded the Kohima ridge. A small garrison held out on **Garrison Hill**. On the **tennis court** of the Deputy Commissioner's bungalow, the trenches of the two sides were so close that grenades were thrown between them.

In the Imphal area alone, about **55,000** soldiers on the Japanese side were killed or wounded, while Allied losses were about **12,600**. Hunger and disease further devastated the retreating Japanese army. After this defeat Japan could advance no further into India, and in 1945 the Allied forces reconquered Burma. That is why these battles are sometimes called the "Stalingrad of the East".

## The Burden on the Economy

To pay for the war, money was printed in huge quantities, and inflation rose rapidly. Grain, cloth and essential goods began to slip out of the reach of ordinary people. Amid the stopping of rice imports from Burma, wartime policies and inflation, the **Bengal Famine** struck in **1943**, in which an estimated **0.8 to 3.8 million** people died.

On the other hand, in return for goods and services produced in India, Britain ran up a large debt to India. These sums accumulated in London as **"sterling balances"**. According to different estimates, by the end of the war this amount was about **1.3 to 1.5 billion pounds**. In this way India, which had been Britain's debtor before the war, now became its creditor. Some industries also grew because of wartime demand, but little of the benefit reached ordinary people.

## Legacy

The war left Britain economically and militarily exhausted. In 1945 a Labour Party government came to power there, which favoured handing power to India. Lakhs of Indian soldiers came home having seen the world, and the **INA trials** and the **1946 naval mutiny** showed that the British could no longer trust the loyalty of the Indian armed forces as before. All this shortened the road to independence.

Today, in the Kohima War Cemetery, near that same old tennis court, these words are carved on a memorial — "When you go home, tell them of us and say: for your tomorrow, we gave our today." In **April 2013**, in a contest held by Britain's **National Army Museum**, Imphal-Kohima was voted "Britain's greatest battle" — a battle in which the majority of soldiers were Indian.
