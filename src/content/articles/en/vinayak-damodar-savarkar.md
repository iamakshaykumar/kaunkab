## From Bhagur to Abhinav Bharat

**Vinayak Damodar Savarkar** was born on **28 May 1883** into a Marathi Chitpavan Brahmin family in the village of **Bhagur** near Nashik. He became politically active while still at school. In **1903**, with his elder brother **Ganesh (Babarao) Savarkar**, he set up an organisation in Nashik called the **'Mitra Mela'**, which later turned into a secret revolutionary society named **'Abhinav Bharat'**. (Sources give both 1904 and 1906 as the year of this renaming.) The aim of the society was to overthrow British rule through armed revolution.

While studying at **Fergusson College** in Pune, Savarkar organised a bonfire of foreign cloth in **1905** in protest against the Partition of Bengal, which is counted among the earliest such events in the country.

## London, India House and the Book on 1857

In **1906**, having won the **Shivaji Scholarship** offered by **Shyamji Krishna Varma**, Savarkar went to London to study law. There he lived at **India House**, a centre of expatriate Indian revolutionaries. It was here that he wrote a book on the **revolt of 1857**, published in English in **1909** as **'The Indian War of Independence'**. It described 1857 not merely as a "sepoy mutiny" but as India's first organised **war of independence**. The British government banned the book, yet copies kept secretly reaching revolutionaries.

In the same period, **Madan Lal Dhingra** assassinated Curzon Wyllie in London in 1909, and in **December 1909** the Collector of Nashik, **A. M. T. Jackson**, was killed by **Anant Laxman Kanhere**. The prosecution alleged that the pistol used to kill Jackson was one of the Browning pistols that Savarkar had sent from London to India. Before this, his brother Ganesh Savarkar had also been sentenced to transportation for life in 1909.

## Arrest, Marseille and the Cellular Jail

On **13 March 1910** Savarkar was arrested in London and sent to India for trial aboard the ship **S.S. Morea**. On the way, at the French port of **Marseille**, he squeezed out through the ship's porthole, jumped into the sea and swam ashore, but he was caught again there. The dispute between France and Britain over this arrest on French soil went to the **Permanent Court of Arbitration** at **The Hague**, which ruled in Britain's favour on **24 February 1911**, though it accepted that there had been an irregularity in the arrest.

After trials in India, Savarkar was given **two sentences of transportation for life** — a total of **50 years**. In **July 1911** he was sent to the **Cellular Jail** in the Andamans, where prisoners were made to do hard labour such as turning oil mills and were kept in solitary cells.

From jail Savarkar sent several **mercy petitions** to the government — in **1911, 1913, 1917** and **1920**. Historians differ over what these petitions meant. **Supporters say** they were a strategy to get out of jail and resume work, of a kind other prisoners also adopted at the time. **Critics say** they contained promises of loyalty to the British government, and that after his release he turned away from the path of armed revolution.

## Ratnagiri and 'Hindutva'

In **1921** Savarkar was brought from the Andamans to jails on the Indian mainland. On **6 January 1924** he was released on condition that he would not leave **Ratnagiri** district and would not take part in politics. These restrictions were lifted in **1937** by the new government of Bombay Province.

His tract **'Essentials of Hindutva'**, written during his imprisonment, was published in **1923** (it was later also printed as 'Hindutva: Who is a Hindu?'). In it he defined **Hindutva** as a cultural and political identity rather than a religious faith — according to which a Hindu is one who regards India as both fatherland and holy land. Savarkar called himself an atheist and was a critic of many religious customs. In Ratnagiri he worked against **untouchability** and supported a temple that people of all castes could enter.

## The Hindu Mahasabha, the Gandhi Murder Trial and Death

After the restrictions were lifted, Savarkar became president of the **Hindu Mahasabha** in **1937** and held the post for several years. In his presidential address of 1937 he said that there were mainly "two nations" in India — Hindus and Muslims; scholars count this among the early expressions of the two-nation idea. During the Second World War the Mahasabha urged Hindus to join the army and opposed the **Quit India Movement**; in some provinces it also took part in coalition governments with the Muslim League. The Congress and critics regarded these policies as working against the national movement, while the Mahasabha described them as a strategy for the militarisation of Hindus.

After the assassination of **Mahatma Gandhi** on **30 January 1948**, Savarkar too was arrested on a charge of conspiracy. The special court **acquitted** him for **lack of sufficient evidence**. The **Kapur Commission**, set up later, remarked in its report (1969) that the role of Savarkar and his group could not be ruled out; but in 2017 the amicus curiae appointed by the Supreme Court said that it was not possible to reach any definite conclusion on this.

He held that giving up one's body was justified once one's life's mission was complete. In keeping with this belief he gave up food and water, and he died in **Bombay** on **26 February 1966**.

## A Contested Legacy

Savarkar's legacy remains a subject of sharp debate in Indian politics. **Supporters** call him 'Veer Savarkar' — a brave revolutionary who endured the torment of the Andamans, gave 1857 the status of a war of independence and worked against caste discrimination. The airport at Port Blair is named after him, and his cell in the Cellular Jail is today a place visitors come to see. **Critics** question his mercy petitions, his opposition to the Quit India Movement and the concept of Hindutva, which they regard as exclusionary towards minorities. For a student of history it is important to view every phase of his life — the young revolutionary, the long imprisonment and the later political ideologue — in the context of his times.
