## Background: Soldiers Taken Prisoner After the War

Under the leadership of Subhas Chandra Bose, the **Indian National Army (INA)** had fought the British on the Burma and north-eastern frontier together with Japan. After Japan's defeat in 1945, thousands of INA soldiers became prisoners of the British. Most of them had earlier been soldiers of the British Indian Army, so the British regarded them as 'traitors'.

At first there was talk of trying thousands of people, but gathering evidence was difficult and the political atmosphere was also heated. In the end about ten courts-martial were held. For the first trial the government chose the **Red Fort in Delhi** — the same place where the last Mughal emperor Bahadur Shah Zafar had been tried in 1858.

## The First Trial: Sahgal, Dhillon, Shah Nawaz

On **5 November 1945** a joint trial began of Captain **Shah Nawaz Khan**, Captain **Prem Kumar Sahgal** and Lieutenant **Gurbaksh Singh Dhillon**. All three were charged under Section 121 of the Indian Penal Code with 'waging war against the King'; Dhillon was also charged with murder, and the other two with abetment of murder.

In 1945 the Congress formed an **INA Defence Committee**. It had people such as **Bhulabhai Desai**, **Tej Bahadur Sapru**, **Asaf Ali**, **Kailash Nath Katju** and **Jawaharlal Nehru** — Nehru put on a lawyer's gown again after many years. The main argument for the defence was made by Bhulabhai Desai.

## Bhulabhai Desai's Argument

Desai's main argument was based on international law. According to him the Indian National Army was the regular army of a provisional free Indian government (the Azad Hind government), which had been recognised by several countries including Japan and Germany. Therefore its soldiers should be covered not by the ordinary law of sedition but by the laws of war. He also said that a subject people has the right to fight for its freedom. This argument is counted among the celebrated arguments of Indian legal history.

## The Surge of the People

News of the trial was printed in the newspapers every day and the whole country became involved. A slogan such as "The voice from the Red Fort — Sahgal, Dhillon, Shah Nawaz" rang in every lane. At meetings the Congress tricolour and the green flag of the Muslim League were seen side by side. Funds were collected, shops were closed, and in November 1945 firing on a students' demonstration in Calcutta killed several people.

The most worrying thing for the British was that sympathy for the INA began to show even among Indian soldiers of the British Indian Army.

## The Verdict and Release

At the end of December 1945 the court found all three guilty and sentenced them to transportation for life (Kala Pani). But seeing the growing public anger, the Commander-in-Chief **General Claude Auchinleck** remitted the sentence using his special powers, and around **3 January 1946** all three were released (the sources differ a little on the exact dates of the verdict and the release).

In later trials the charge of sedition was dropped. When Captain **Abdul Rashid** was given a seven-year sentence in February 1946, there were again big demonstrations in Calcutta.

## Significance

The INA trials showed that the British could no longer trust the loyalty of the Indian army. A few weeks later, in February 1946, the **naval mutiny** took place. Many historians believe that these events pushed Britain towards an early transfer of power, and in the same year the **Cabinet Mission** came to India. However, how decisive the INA's role was among the causes of independence is a matter of disagreement among historians.
