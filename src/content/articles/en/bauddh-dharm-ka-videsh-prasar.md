## Ashoka and 'Dhamma-vijaya'

The spread of Buddhism outside India did not come from a single event but from a process lasting centuries. Its first major link is the Mauryan emperor **Ashoka** (c. 268–232 BCE). After the Kalinga war Ashoka called 'dhamma-vijaya' the true conquest. In his **Thirteenth Rock Edict** he claims that his message of dhamma reached the Greek kings of the west — it names Antiyoka (Antiochus) of Syria, Turamaya (Ptolemy) of Egypt and three other kings. There is no solid proof that Buddhism actually spread in these countries at that time; but it shows Ashoka's desire for contact with the outside world.

## The Third Council and the Dhamma Missionaries

According to the Pali chronicles of Sri Lanka, the **Dipavamsa** and **Mahavamsa**, in Ashoka's time the **Third Buddhist Council** was held at Pataliputra under the presidency of the monk **Moggaliputta Tissa**. After this, dhamma missionaries were sent in different directions — for example Majjhantika to Kashmir-Gandhara, Mahadeva to Mahishamandala (the Mysore region), **Sona and Uttara** to Suvarnabhumi, and **Mahinda** to Lanka. These accounts come from later Theravada texts, so historians take some details of the council and these missions with caution.

## Sri Lanka: Mahinda and Sanghamitta

According to tradition, Ashoka's son **Mahinda** (Mahendra) reached Sri Lanka with his companions and met King **Devanampiya Tissa**, who was hunting in the hills of **Mihintale**. The king adopted Buddhism and gave the Mahamegha garden in the capital **Anuradhapura** to the monks, which later became the **Mahavihara**. Mahinda's sister **Sanghamitta** came to establish the order of nuns and brought a branch of the Bodhi tree. In the 1st century BCE the Pali Tipitaka was first committed to writing in Sri Lanka itself. In time Sri Lanka became the centre of Theravada Buddhism.

## To China along the Silk Road

In the north-west the **Kushan empire** (especially the time of Kanishka) linked Gandhara and Central Asia. Merchants and monks reached China through the oasis towns of the **Silk Road**. Chinese tradition has the story of Emperor Ming's dream and the 'White Horse Temple' of Luoyang, but this is considered a legend. The solid evidence is from the 2nd century: the Parthian monk **An Shigao** began translating Buddhist texts into Chinese at Luoyang around **148 CE**. Later **Kumarajiva** of Kucha (at Chang'an from about 401 CE) became a great translator. In the opposite direction, Chinese travellers such as **Faxian** and **Xuanzang** came to India in search of texts. From China, Buddhism went on to Korea and Japan.

## Tibet: Shantarakshita and Padmasambhava

Buddhism began reaching Tibet from the 7th century, but its roots took hold in the 8th century in the time of King **Trisong Detsen**. He invited the Nalanda scholar **Shantarakshita**. According to Tibetan tradition, because of local opposition and to pacify the 'spirits', the tantric master **Padmasambhava** (Guru Rinpoche) was invited, and Tibet's first monastery, **Samye**, was built. Many miraculous stories were later attached to Padmasambhava's life; scholars now accept him as a historical person but keep the stories apart.

## Southeast Asia by Sea

Buddhism reached Southeast Asia mainly with **maritime trade** — through the mutual contact of Indian and local merchants, monks and royal courts. The kingdom of **Srivijaya** in Sumatra became a centre of Buddhist learning, and in Java around the 9th century the huge **Borobudur** stupa-temple was built. Later the Theravada tradition spread from Sri Lanka into Myanmar, Thailand, Laos and Cambodia.

Today crores of people in Asia follow Buddhism, while in India it had become very weak by the 12th–13th century. In this way a tradition of India deeply influenced the language, art and thought of many civilisations of Asia.
