## Who Were the Indo-Parthians?

In the first century BCE and first century CE the north-west of the Indian subcontinent was an arena of several foreign ruling dynasties — the **Indo-Greeks**, the **Shakas** (Indo-Scythians) and then the **Indo-Parthians**. The Indo-Parthians were connected with the Parthian (Arsacid) Empire of Iran and probably came from the local ruling family of **Sistan** (ancient Drangiana). The most famous king among them was **Gondophares I**, whose reign is usually placed **between about 19 and 46 CE**.

## Extent of the Kingdom

Gondophares broke away from Parthian overlordship, declared himself an independent ruler and adopted the Greek title "**Autocrator**". His kingdom extended from Sistan and **Arachosia** (the Kandahar region) to **Gandhara**, and he took the Kabul valley, Punjab and parts of Sindh from the Shaka kings. The capital is believed to be **Taxila**. But this empire was loosely held and soon broke up after his death — his nephew **Abdagases** became ruler in Gandhara and **Orthagnes** in the western parts. At about the same time the **Kushana** power was rising from the north, which later brought this entire region under its control.

## Coins and the Takht-i-Bahi Inscription

The main basis of our knowledge about Gondophares is his **silver and bronze coins**. On these there is a legend in **Greek** on one side and in Prakrit in the **Kharoshthi** script on the other, and the king is given high titles like "King of Kings". The coins also show Greek deities such as the victory goddess **Nike** or Zeus — evidence of that mixed Gandhara culture.

The second important source is the **Takht-i-Bahi inscription**. This is a stone inscription in the Kharoshthi script, now in the **Lahore Museum**; there is some uncertainty about its original place, but it is linked with the famous Buddhist monastery of **Takht-i-Bahi** near Mardan (Pakistan). It is read as giving the **26th year** of the reign of Maharaja "**Gudavhara**" and **year 103** of some era. Most scholars take this era to be the one beginning around 57 BCE (the Azes or Vikrama era), which makes year 103 about **46 CE** — and from this the start of his reign is worked out as about 19–20 CE. The identification of the era is still debated.

## The Tradition of St Thomas

In the text **"Acts of Thomas"**, written in Syriac in the early third century CE, there is a story that a merchant took **St Thomas**, a disciple of Jesus, to India to build a palace for the king **"Gudnaphar"**, and that Thomas preached Christianity there. When in the nineteenth century a real king named Gondophares came to light from coins, many scholars identified the two.

Caution is necessary here:

- "Acts of Thomas" is a **religious narrative** text, not history; it is full of tales of miracles.
- The match of names shows only that the author knew the name of a real Indian king, but it **does not prove Thomas's journey to India**.
- The separate tradition of the Syrian Christian community of Kerala links Thomas to the Malabar coast (52 CE) and to Mylapore in Chennai — this too is only tradition.

## Significance

The era of Gondophares shows that first-century Gandhara was a meeting place of Iranian, Greek and Indian cultures — a Parthian king, coins in Greek and Prakrit, the Kharoshthi script, and flourishing Buddhist monasteries all around. His story also teaches that **hard evidence** like coins and inscriptions and later **religious traditions** should be weighed separately.
