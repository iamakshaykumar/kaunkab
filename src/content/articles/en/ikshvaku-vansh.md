## After the Satavahanas

By the beginning of the third century CE the power of the vast **Satavahana** empire of the Deccan had broken up and many regional dynasties rose in its place. One of them was the **Andhra Ikshvaku** dynasty, which ruled for about a century over parts of today's Andhra Pradesh and Telangana in the lower valley of the Krishna river. They are also called the **"Ikshvakus of Vijayapuri"**, to distinguish them from the legendary Ikshvaku dynasty of Ayodhya in the Ramayana — although these kings themselves had adopted that same prestigious name. The **"Sriparvatiya Andhras"** of the *Matsya Purana* are probably this same dynasty.

Scholars differ about their dates; roughly they are placed between **about 225 and 340 CE**.

## The Kings and Their Sacrifices

The inscriptions of Nagarjunakonda give the names of four main kings:

- **Vasishthiputra Chantamula** — the founder of the dynasty. Later inscriptions describe him as having performed the **Agnishtoma, Vajapeya** and **Ashvamedha** sacrifices. A tortoise-shaped structure like a sacrificial altar and a horse skeleton found in excavation are counted as support for this claim.
- **Virapurushadatta** — ruled for at least 24 years. Buddhist construction was greatest in his time.
- **Ehuvala Chantamula** — ruled for at least 24 years. In his time Sanskrit inscriptions and temples of Brahmanical deities in Vijayapuri increased.
- **Rudrapurushadatta** — the last known king, with inscriptions found up to his 11th year.

The kings themselves were followers of **Shaiva** and Vedic tradition, but they let Buddhism flourish.

## The Queens and Buddhist Donations

The most unusual feature of the Ikshvaku period is the **Buddhist patronage by the women of the royal family**. According to the inscriptions, Chantamula's sister **Chantisiri** (Chantashri) made a large donation for the **Mahachaitya** (main stupa) of Vijayapuri and construction around it; this stupa is believed to have held relics of the Buddha. Other queens and princesses also had viharas, chaityas and pillars built.

At its peak Vijayapuri had **more than thirty** Buddhist viharas, which made it the largest Buddhist centre of South India. The limestone carving here is in the tradition of the **Amaravati style**. One vihara is believed to be linked to monks coming from Sri Lanka, which shows Vijayapuri's wide Buddhist contacts.

## Marriages and Relations

The Ikshvaku kings made marriage alliances with neighbouring dynasties. Virapurushadatta married several of his maternal and paternal cousins, and one of his queens, **Rudradhara-Bhattarika**, is described as the daughter of the ruler of **Ujjain** — probably of the **Western Kshatrapa** dynasty. His daughter was married to the ruler of **Vanavasa** (Banavasi). Carvings found in the royal palace area of Vijayapuri also show soldiers in Shaka (Scythian) dress.

## Decline

By the middle of the fourth century Ikshvaku power had ended. An inscription of the **Abhira** dynasty has been found at Nagarjunakonda, from which it was surmised that the Abhiras held the place for some time, but this is uncertain. By about this time the region had come under the **Pallavas** of Kanchi.

## Discovery and a Drowning Heritage

The remains of Nagarjunakonda came to light after a local schoolteacher's report in 1926. **A. H. Longhurst** carried out systematic excavation here in 1927–31. When it was decided to build the **Nagarjuna Sagar dam** on the Krishna river, a large rescue excavation was done in **1954–60** under R. Subrahmanyam. The original site was submerged in the lake, but many monuments were lifted and rebuilt on the hilltop — now an **island** in the middle of the lake — and on the mainland. A museum opened on the island in 1966.

The modern name of the site is after the Buddhist philosopher **Nagarjuna**, but this name arose in the medieval period; ancient inscriptions call it **"Vijayapuri on Sriparvata"**, and there is no archaeological proof that Nagarjuna lived here.
