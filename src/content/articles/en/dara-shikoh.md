## Prince and Heir

Dara Shikoh was born on **20 March 1615** at Ajmer. He was the eldest son of Prince Khurram (later Emperor **Shah Jahan**) and **Mumtaz Mahal**. In 1633 he married **Nadira Banu** at Agra, and around the same time he came to be regarded as the heir to the throne (wali-ahd).

Shah Jahan was very fond of this son and gave him high mansabs at court and titles such as 'Shah-i-Buland Iqbal'. But because he stayed close to the court, Dara did not gain the same experience of war and administration as his brothers, especially **Aurangzeb**, who spent a long time in the Deccan. He also led the siege of Kandahar in 1653, which failed.

## Sufi Seeker and Scholar

Dara was drawn to spirituality from a young age. He was associated with the Qadiri Sufi saint of Lahore, **Mian Mir**, and his disciple **Mulla Shah Badakhshi**. He wrote books on the lives of Sufi saints, such as 'Safinat-ul-Auliya' and 'Sakinat-ul-Auliya'.

His most famous work is the Persian **'Majma-ul-Bahrain'** ('The Confluence of Two Oceans'), written in 1654–55. In it he compared the terminology of Sufi thought and Vedanta and argued that both traditions are different paths to the same truth. His dialogues with the Hindu saint Baba Lal Das were also recorded in Persian.

## 'Sirr-i-Akbar': The Translation of the Upanishads

Under Dara's supervision, with the help of Sanskrit pandits, about fifty Upanishads were translated into Persian, completed in **1657**. It was named **'Sirr-i-Akbar'** ('The Great Secret'). Different manuscripts give the number of Upanishads as anywhere from 50 to 52. The Persian translations of the Yoga Vasistha and the Bhagavad Gita are also believed to have been made under Dara's patronage.

The influence of this work reached beyond India. The French scholar **Anquetil-Duperron** made a Latin translation of it, printed in 1801–02, and through it the Upanishads reached European philosophers on a large scale for the first time.

## The War of Succession

In 1657, as soon as Shah Jahan fell seriously ill, a struggle for the throne broke out among his four sons: Dara, Shuja, Aurangzeb and Murad.

- In 1658, at Dharmat and then at **Samugarh** near Agra, the combined army of Aurangzeb and Murad defeated the imperial army.
- Dara fled towards Punjab and Sindh, then returned after raising an army from Gujarat, but was defeated again in **March 1659** at the Battle of **Deorai** near Ajmer.
- Seeking refuge, he reached the Afghan chieftain **Malik Jiwan**, whom he had once helped. Malik Jiwan seized him and handed him over to Aurangzeb (June 1659).

## The End and an Assessment

Dara was brought to Delhi and paraded through the streets seated on an elephant. An assembly of nobles and ulama declared him an apostate, and on the night of **30 August 1659** (by the Hijri/Julian reckoning; about 9 September in the Gregorian) he was put to death. He was buried in **Humayun's Tomb**.

Historians differ in their assessment of Dara. Many see him as a symbol of religious tolerance and dialogue and imagine that India's history would have been different had he become emperor. Many others point out that he was relatively weak militarily and politically, and that his execution was the result of power politics more than of religious reasons. Whatever the case, his writings are an important part of India's multi-religious intellectual tradition.
