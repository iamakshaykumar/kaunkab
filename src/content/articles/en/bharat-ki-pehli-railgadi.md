## Background: Why Railways Were Needed

By the middle of the nineteenth century, the railway had become the backbone of the Industrial Revolution in England. In India, British traders wanted a cheap means of quickly carrying raw materials such as cotton from the interior to the ports, and of taking goods made in England to every corner of the country. For the government, moving the army quickly from one place to another was also a major need.

In 1849 the **Great Indian Peninsula (GIP) Railway** company was formed, and it was given the task of building a railway line inland from Bombay. To attract companies, the government gave a **guarantee** of a fixed interest on their capital — meaning investors were assured of a return even if there was a loss.

## The Day of 16 April 1853

On the afternoon of **16 April 1853**, at about half past three, the first passenger train left **Bori Bunder** station in Bombay for **Thane**. (Sources give the departure time variously as 3:30 or 3:35.) On this journey of about 34 kilometres (21 miles), the 14 coaches carried around 400 distinguished guests.

The train was pulled by three steam locomotives, named **Sahib**, **Sindh** and **Sultan**. A public holiday was declared in Bombay that day, and all along the route people thronged to see this 'train that runs on fire'. A trial run had already been held on the same line a few months earlier, in November 1852.

## Dalhousie and the Railway Plan

The Governor-General of India at that time was **Lord Dalhousie** (1848–1856). In 1853 he wrote a famous **'Railway Minute'** (policy paper) in which he outlined the main lines to connect the country's major cities and ports. For this reason Dalhousie is often regarded as the chief planner of the Indian railways. During his tenure, the telegraph and the modern postal system also expanded along with the railways.

## Tracks Spreading Across the Country

After the first train, the railway network spread rapidly:

- **15 August 1854** — The first passenger train ran in eastern India, from Howrah to Hooghly.
- **1 July 1856** — In the south, a line opened from Royapuram in Madras towards Arcot.
- In the 1860s, tracks were also laid over the difficult ascents of the Western Ghats (the Bhor Ghat and the Thal Ghat).

## The Dual Impact of the Railways

The railways changed India in many ways. Long journeys began to take hours instead of days, people and ideas travelled from one province to another, and it became easier to send grain in times of famine. Later, the railways also played a major role in the countrywide tours and movements of national leaders.

But nationalist thinkers also criticised them. The railway lines were built mainly to suit the needs of British trade and administration, the engines and iron came from England, and because of the guarantee system foreign investors kept making profits from the money of Indian taxpayers. Cheap foreign goods reaching the villages also harmed many native crafts.

## Legacy

That short journey from Bori Bunder to Thane is today regarded as the beginning of one of the world's largest railway networks. Every year on 16 April, the Indian Railways remembers this historic journey.
